# Niwaki pruning reference

Reference distilled from "Niwaki: Pruning, Training and Shaping Trees the Japanese Way" by Jake Hobson (Timber Press, 2007). Distilled 2026-11-03 from Tim's copy; page refs are to the book's own numbering.

Use: read a photo of a tree, advise a niwaki-style prune, and plan crew work by season. The book is written for Japan and southern England; nothing in it is specific to coastal BC. Anything not in the book is marked [not in book].

## 1. How niwaki thinking differs

- Aim is "the character of maturity": branches trained and pruned to look larger and older than they are (p45). Niwaki = garden trees grown in the ground (not pots), shaped into a "proper tree form" (p26, p43).
- Training gives outstretched, horizontal (even below-horizontal) branches like old trees; pruning raises bud count and condenses the tree while keeping proportions (p45, p52).
- Structure first: thin to reveal trunk and framework, then build foliage "pads/clouds" (branch ends clipped into rounded layers) on the framework (p50-55, p103).
- Space (the gaps between pads) matters as much as foliage: shadows and outlines give depth; thinning is as important as clipping (p83-84, p103).
- Asymmetry and odd numbers: irregular balance over symmetry; opposite-branch trees are thinned to alternate even if not natural; trees planted singly or in threes (p46-47). Karikomi never makes perfect spheres (p47, p83).
- Trunk line is a feature: bends, kinks, one-sided branches, raised trunks that show bark. Start young while flexible (p58-59).
- Versus Western shearing: Western topiary is geometric or loosely "cloud" hedge; niwaki clips to low, wide, organic, tapered shapes, and also thins by hand/scissors to keep a natural look (tea-garden style) (p27, p82-83, p34).
- Maintenance is the art: little and often, twice a year minimum once shaped; the tree is "a work in progress all its life" (p54-55).

## 2. Techniques glossary

Only terms in the book.

| Term | Meaning | When used |
|---|---|---|
| Fukinaoshi ("to re-do") | Cut back to trunk plus main-branch framework, then rebuild shape from adventitious (old-wood) buds | Resprouting species only; nursery practice, not garden; start in spring after worst frosts (p49-51) |
| Midoritsumi ("green picking") | Pinch new pine candles at base (or up to 2.5 cm for P. densiflora) before needles open, to force a small second flush | Early summer (p70-72) |
| Momiage | Autumn pine thinning: reduce second flush to 1-2 shoots, pluck old needles | Autumn (Oct-Nov in Japan); may wait to spring for bushier look (p70-72) |
| Karikomi (o-/ko-/nami-) | Clipping shrubs (mainly evergreen azalea) into low organic mounds; ko = single, o = group, nami = wave | Little and often through growing season (p77, p82) |
| Tamazukuri | "Ball" style: each branch clipped to a rounded end; dense or hollow versions | Junipers, Cedrus, Ilex integra, yew etc. (p27, p95-97) |
| Edasukashishitate | Thinning to keep natural habit, with chirashi and pollard-like branch ends | Cedrus deodara, weeping Chamaecyparis; mid-summer and autumn/early winter (p97-99) |
| Chirashi | Thinning branches/foliage rather than shaping | Tea gardens, evergreens (p30, p97) |
| Daisugi / Kitayamasugi | Cryptomeria with permanent base and repeatedly renewed upright leaders | Cryptomeria japonica var. radicans (p86-89) |
| Daimochi | Ilex integra on a fat stump with grafted new growth | Nursery-made (p106) |
| Kobushishitate ("fist pruning") | Annual cut of all current-season growth to build bolls | Lagerstroemia, autumn (p116) |
| Monkaburi | Branch trained over a gateway | Pines, Podocarpus (p29, p92-93) |
| Nemawashi / nemaki | Root-balling / rootball wrapping | Autumn to spring for first move (p126-131) |

## 5. Multi-year plan

- Fukinaoshi on an established, resprouting tree: transform over four to five years (p51). Done at the nursery, not in the garden (p49-50).
- Year 0 (spring after frosts): remove dead, broken, suckers, epicormic growth; cut trunk above a side-branch set at chosen height; thin to a branch about every 15 cm around trunk (more to the sides than front/back); cut remaining branches back to about 30 cm (p50). Check tree is healthy and resprouts (p50).
- Within 1 year: tie side branches down about 10 degrees below horizontal with natural-fibre string; thick branches splinted to bamboo; very thick ones cut back and new shoot trained (p52-53). Check string yearly so it does not cut bark (p53).
- Years 2-3: regular trims build branch pads, wide, gently sloping, low and flat; clip twice a year minimum (early summer after first flush, late summer) (p54-55).
- Head: choose neck height; four shoots tied down; cut to about 15 cm; next year cut new growth to two buds; thicker twigs cut about 2.5 cm below foliage level after years (p55-56).
- Order of removal: structure first (dead/epicormic, then excess branches), then pad building. Do not remove all new growth at once; work up the tree choosing each branch (p101).
- Conifers that will not resprout (pines, 'Kaizuka', Cupressus): no cutback; prune new growth only and start young (p69-70, p94).
- Neglected or overgrown: for resprouting species, fukinaoshi as above; "Wading in" quick fix: thin to reveal branch structure, shape outline with shears, accentuate branches; if it goes wrong it grows out (p60-61). [Staging a cut-back over several seasons for a garden tree: not in book]

## 6. Reading a tree before cutting

From photos identify:
- Species: does it resprout from old wood? (pine, juniper, cypress do not) (p69-70, p94).
- Trunk line: single, multi, bend; any leader or competing leaders; epicormic growth and suckers (p50).
- Main branches: spacing (about every 15 cm on small trees), alternate vs opposite, branches too big or small for balance, crossing, twisted, damaged (p103).
- Branch direction: up/down growth, inward-facing shoots, branches too upright or wide (p110-111).
- Where foliage ends on a conifer: green extent vs bare old wood (cut limit) (p94).
- What to keep: the best-placed branches that show trunk line and horizontal layers (p103).

Cannot judge from photo, check on site:
- Tree health and whether old wood will resprout (p50).
- Branch flexibility for tying, which depends on season and species (p52).
- Depth of green growth vs dead interior on conifers (p94).

## 7. Mistakes the book warns against

- Cutting into leafless old wood on pines, 'Kaizuka' junipers, Cupressus (p69-70, p94-95).
- Cutting too hard even within green growth on 'Kaizuka' (grey prickly juvenile foliage) (p94).
- Leyland cypress as niwaki material (p49, p96).
- Clipping box in bright sun; cutting hard late in the year (p83).
- Leaving clippings and old leaves around box (blight) (p84).
- Delaying post-flower clip on azalea: woody shoots, bare patches (p82).
- Autumn pruning of Camellia and Osmanthus that flower on summer growth (p106-107).
- Regular pruning of flowering cherries; only deadwood (p112-113).
- Cutting too close to the bud, too far above it, or leaving a snag (p48).

## 8. Tools and cuts

Tools (p134-135):
- Uekibasami (round-handled garden scissors) for thinning; mekiribasami (spring-action) for pine buds; hakaribasami one-handed shears or long hedge shears (karikomibasami) for clipping.
- Japanese pull-stroke saws; secateurs for small work; kyatatsu tripod ladder.

Cut rules (p48-49):
- Cut just above a bud. Alternate buds: slope the cut down away from the bud. Opposite buds: straight cut.
- The last bud left is dominant: choose an outward bud (or one that fills a space).
- Branch to trunk: cut just outside the bark collar, small circumference, no snag.
- Thick branch: cut further out first, undercut, cut from above, then remove stub.
- Thinning vs heading: cutting the leader releases lower side branches (auxin) and old-wood buds (p45); cutting leaders to one or two buds builds density near the trunk (p54).
- Cutting through leaves with shears is fine (p54, p93).
- Clip from the top down, undersides flat and sharp, shake clippings out (p54, p93).
