# Pruning and shaping principles from bonsai (for garden-scale trees)

Source: *The Bonsai Bible*, Peter Chan (Mitchell Beazley, 2014). Distilled 2026-11-03 from Tim's copy; only the parts that transfer to garden-scale trees. Page refs are the book's own. Tim's copy is a text export with no page numbers, so refs below are the book's section or species-entry names.

**Coverage warning:** mostly species care sheets; almost no design theory, style descriptions or training timeline. Gaps are marked [not in book].

## 2. Design principles that transfer

- **Trunk line and taper:** a good trunk tapers gradually from a broad base to a narrow apex, with surface roots radiating evenly from the base (Choosing and buying: Selecting a plant).
- **Branch quality:** look for "elegant placement and arrangement" of branches and a fine branch structure; two similar trees differ hugely in value on this alone (same section).
- **Apex and taper by cutting:** repeated cutting of the apex develops a tapering trunk, and frequent trimming of secondary shoots builds the branch framework (Ash entry).
- **Thickness first:** a thick trunk comes from growing in open ground for a couple of years (Japanese hornbeam, Field maple, Ash entries). A large oak looks best with a "reasonably sized trunk" that takes time (Common oak).
- **New branch:** let one bud run unchecked until thick enough, then shape it; about one season (Hedging honeysuckle).
- **Light and air:** light pruning lets light reach inner branches, improves air circulation and reduces pests and disease (Structural shaping, pruning and pinching).
- **Direction of shoots:** cut to an outward-pointing bud; remove inward-pointing shoots (Brazilian rain tree, Blackthorn, Sageretia). Remove crossing branches (Japanese grey-bark elm).
- First/second/back branch rule, bar branches, branches on the inside of a curve, negative space, viewing side, asymmetry: [not in book].

## 3. Styles (book's own wording; suits)

- **Formal upright:** "absolutely straight" trunk. Japanese cedar (naturally straight), Hinoki cypress, horse chestnut, Chinese quince.
- **Informal upright:** S-shaped, "the most common" style. Japanese white pine, privet, camellia, oak, elm, mulberry, elaeagnus, satsuki azalea, stewartia, most maples.
- **Slanting, windswept:** named only. Dwarf Scots pine, Mugo pine, Prunus. Maples and lime are not suited to windswept.
- **Cascade:** procumbens juniper (creeping habit), cotoneaster, forsythia, climbing hydrangea, black pine.
- **Literati:** bare slender trunk. Leggy Scots pine, ash, potentilla, Prunus. Not lime.
- **Broom:** domed "mop head", "most natural looking". Zelkova (its natural habit), Chinese elm, oak, dwarf maples.
- **Multi-trunk, twin, clump:** Japanese hornbeam, flowering quince (clump), witch hazel.
- **Group/forest:** larch, Japanese hornbeam, ash, birch, yezo spruce.
- **Driftwood (jin/shari):** junipers, yew, Mugo pine, box, hornbeam. Needs thick wood. Maples are not suited.
- **Natural forms:** ginkgo flame shape, willow weeping.

## 4. Branch and shoot work

- **Structural (hard) pruning:** builds the framework or reshapes a tree that lost form; spring or midsummer, when it heals readily.
- **Pinching:** remove the tip of a soft new shoot. Deciduous: when two or three leaves appear. Shoots grow in spurts, so the job is not constant. Pines make candles once a year; other evergreens and deciduous trees flush two or three times.
- **Thinning:** remove inner and crossing twigs for light and air; cut to dormant buds to stimulate shoots.
- **Leaf pruning:** defoliating a healthy deciduous tree in early summer; new smaller leaves in four or five weeks. Healthy trees only; risky on buttonwood. Maple, oak, elm, hornbeam types, horse chestnut, lime.
- **Never shear across needles or scale foliage:** brown tips on juniper, cypress, cedar (Juniper entries).
- **Rest:** let flowering and fruiting trees grow unchecked once every 3-4 years.
- **Flowering rule:** buds sit on the current season's growth; do not prune or pinch after midsummer. Plump axil buds are flower buds.
- Back-budding and ramification: only the pine candle and cut-back advice below. General method [not in book].
- Cut hardness: only the entries below. Large-wound sealing: Japanese cut paste on non-resinous trees; pines need none.

| Species | Season | Method | Caution |

## 5. Timing by group

- **Deciduous (maple, elm, hornbeam):** major cuts in summer while vigorous. Heavy branch removal mid-summer. Light prune early spring or before leaf-out to see the framework.
- **Conifers:** pines: candles late spring, branches summer to late autumn. Other conifers: prune spring or summer, trim lightly in autumn. Evergreens need trimming less often than deciduous.
- **Broadleaf evergreen (box, camellia, olive, myrtle):** box takes regular scissor pruning; olive in late summer after flowering and fruit set; camellia after bloom.
- Deciduous trees are usually pruned twice a year (Structural shaping).

- Build the framework by structural cuts, then wiring if needed.
- Refine with pinching through the season; wire left 6 months to 2+ years by thickness.

## 7. Faults the book names

- **Long shoots left to extend:** spoil shape and block laterals (Sweet gum; Structural shaping). Pinch earlier.
- **Overfeeding or over-watering:** long internodes and coarse leaves (hornbeam, pines, fig). Reduce.
- **Overgrown leggy branch ends:** hard to make dense again (Eastern white cedar). Pinch regularly.
- **Branch die-back without light:** cut back to healthy tissue (beech, birch, elm).
- **Inward growth:** prune to an outward bud.
- **Wire scars:** maple, alder, lime scar fast; remove early.
- **Late pruning of flowering trees:** loses next year's flowers.
- **Shearing across foliage** on conifers.
