# The Sugarman Method — Adweek Copywriting Handbook, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Joseph Sugarman's *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (Wiley, 2007), built from the owner's copy of the book. Sugarman ran JS&A (mail-order electronics) and BluBlocker, wrote every ad himself, and paid for every flop, so this is a direct-response method, not brand theory. Nothing here is verbatim; the ad case studies describe technique, not the ads.

Use this reference for **long-form persuasive copy** (landing pages, sales pages, service pages, direct mail, long emails) and for **diagnosing why copy isn't being read**. For headline formulas see `copy-frameworks.md`; for the other legends see `legends-library.md` (Volume 7 there is the short version of this).

**Section 11** is built from Sugarman's earlier book, *Advertising Secrets of the Written Word* (DelStar, 1998) — the direct predecessor of the 2007 Adweek Handbook, same seminar (1977–1988), same 13 ad case studies, same appendix structure. The two books are roughly 90% identical; Section 11 covers only what's genuinely new or different in the earlier edition, so it doesn't repeat what Sections 1–10 already say.

## 1. The 15 Axioms

These are the book's spine. Numbering is Sugarman's; wording is ours.

1. **Copywriting is a mental process.** Output = general life knowledge × specific product knowledge × the ability to get it on paper to sell. Feed the first two before blaming the third.
2. **Every element of an ad has one real job: get the first sentence read.** Headline, subhead, photo, caption, logo, price, layout: their conventional purposes are secondary.
3. **The first sentence's only job is to get the second sentence read.** And the second's is to get the third read. No benefits or features in the opening lines.
4. **Layout and the opening paragraphs must create the buying environment** that suits this product: discount-store energy for bargains, gallery calm for premium.
5. **Get the reader nodding yes.** Open with short, true statements the target reader can't disagree with. One "no," "I don't believe that," or "that's not me" and they're gone.
6. **Copy must be a slippery slide.** So compelling the reader can't stop until the end. Past roughly a quarter of the way, most readers finish.
7. **Don't assume constraints that aren't there.** "You can't sell X by mail / to this audience / at this price" is usually a learned belief, not a fact. Test it small.
8. **Keep the reader reading through curiosity.** Plant seeds where copy slows.
9. **Never sell the product; sell a concept.** Positioning, big idea, angle. The only exception is a product so new it *is* the concept.
10. **Incubation is real.** Load up on knowledge, then walk away. The idea arrives in the shower. Time pressure, ego, creative orientation, and environment set the speed.
11. **Copy should be exactly as long as it takes to make the reader act.** Not pro-long, not pro-short. Pro-sale.
12. **Every communication should be personal**, one writer to one reader, whatever the medium. "I" and "you," never "we at Company invite all of you."
13. **Ideas must flow logically**, anticipating and answering the prospect's questions in the order they'd ask them face to face.
14. **Editing means saying exactly what you mean in the fewest words.** Same feeling, fewer words. Expect to cut half.
15. **A cure sells far more easily than a preventive**, unless the preventive is positioned as a cure or its curative side is emphasized.

**Three emotion principles** (sit alongside the axioms):
- Every word carries an emotion and tells a small story ("cottage" vs. "motel"; "fix" vs. "repair").
- A good ad is an emotional outpouring of words, feelings, and impressions, tuned later by editing.
- People buy on emotion, then justify with logic. Supply both, in that order.

## 2. The Writing Process

### The seven steps (Chapter 22)

1. **Become an expert on the product.** Use it, read everything, interrogate the maker. Most big ideas hide in a technical detail nobody thought to mention (a watch display sealed by laser; a walkie-talkie that happened to sit on a CB channel). *Apply:* list every fact, quirk, and origin detail before drafting; ask the maker "why does this exist now?"
2. **Know the prospect.** Product expertise without customer knowledge is a handicap. *Apply:* one-paragraph buyer profile plus the single motive that tips them.
3. **Write headline and subheadline.** Their only job is enough curiosity to reach sentence one. Sugarman's own pattern: two-to-four-word headline, roughly sixteen-word subhead, first sentence under ten words. *Apply:* draft several short headline candidates; don't describe the product in them.
4. **Write the copy without stopping.** No grammar, no structure, no rereading. Dump everything. *Apply:* timebox a no-edit brain-dump.
5. **Edit.** Fix mechanics, cut every word that doesn't carry the thought. *Apply:* set a word-count reduction target.
6. **Incubate.** Put it away overnight or longer. *Apply:* never ship copy in the sitting it was edited.
7. **Take a final look**, then loop steps 5–6 until nothing changes.

### Incubation (Chapter 13)
Prepare (reread all notes, jot headlines and appeals), then stop thinking about it. The subconscious runs the problem against everything you know. Returning early interrupts it. Write the idea down the moment it lands.

### Restating the problem (Chapter 16)
Before choosing an angle, write five to ten restatements of the brief: "sell this pinball machine" → "introduce what's unique about it" → "make it easy to buy and feel like fun." The later restatements unlock the concept.

### Patterning
Borrow *structure* from an admired ad for a similar product (long headline → long headline; many captions → many captions). Never borrow surface.

### The editing pass (Chapter 17)
Five moves, run as a checklist:
1. **Hunt "that."** Everything up to and including it can often go.
2. **Edit for rhythm.** Vary sentence length: short, long, medium, short, short, very long.
3. **Combine sentences** where a dash or comma saves a word.
4. **Cut needless words** ("seeing the positive results" → "seeing positive results").
5. **Rearrange for flow and emotion.** Lead with the pleasure, then the explanation.

Delete throat-clearing openers ("Finally, it is important to note that…"). Kill stacked intensifiers and redundant pairs ("guidance and advice," "very critical"). Ten drafts is normal. A shorter slide is slipperier, and a shorter-looking ad gets more people to start.

Sugarman's worked example: a bathroom-scale paragraph cut roughly forty percent with the feeling intact, by leading with the one pleasure of dieting (stepping on the scale and seeing progress) and then explaining the scale as a report card.

## 3. The Opening

### The slippery slide (Chapter 8)
Picture a playground slide greased end to end. Headline compels the subhead, subhead compels the caption, caption compels sentence one, sentence one is so easy it compels sentence two. Traffic is not circulation; traffic is readers who get *into* the copy. A reader with no interest in thermostats once wrote to complain she'd been pulled through an entire thermostat ad and lost five minutes. That is the goal.

Three ways to start the slide:
- **A story or odd news item**, which need not relate to the product at all as long as you bridge honestly later (a prison anecdote opened a rowing-machine ad; the ad confessed the link was invented and readers loved it).
- **A candid flaw.** Mock the product's ugly case and silly name, promise a teardown, then discover its good points one by one. Candid negatives start the slide *and* build trust.
- **An outrageous claim that resolves honestly** ("stolen merchandise" that turns out to be refurbished returns sold at a discount).

*Apply:* keep a clipping file of odd facts and stories. Write headline, subhead, caption, and first paragraph as one chain and test each link: would a reader with zero need keep going?

### The first sentence (Chapter 5)
Make it short. So short it's barely a sentence, with no multisyllable words, almost incomplete so the reader must continue. The locomotive analogy: enormous effort to start, easy once rolling. Sugarman tried opening with the sales message repeatedly to disprove himself; it always lost.

*Apply:* cut the opener to five or six plain words that create a small itch. Move every benefit and feature out of paragraph one.

### The buying environment (Chapter 6)
A gallery saleswoman once led Sugarman from the showroom into a carpeted back room, dimmed the lights, raised the music, spotlit the painting, and nearly closed a sale on a piece he hadn't meant to buy. In print you control the room completely, and you build it mostly with words: phrasing, vocabulary, the integrity you convey. Match look and tone to the offer. No fine art at the army surplus store, no bargain bins at Tiffany's.

*Apply:* decide what "room" the reader should feel in, then choose type density, tone, and vocabulary to match. Check that the opening paragraphs set it, not just the design.

### Harmony (Chapter 7)
The Buick salesman's chain: "Nice day?" yes. "You keep your car clean." yes. "You drive a Buick, we sell Buicks." yes. "May I show you the new model?" yes. Obvious true statements keep the head nodding. Speak in the prospect's own language and format (Sugarman bought ad space from a magazine that pitched him using a knock-off of his own ad format). Link the product to a feeling the reader already holds (a survival-food ad opened with the fuel shortage everyone had just lived through).

*Apply:* open with two or three short statements the target reader cannot disagree with. Strike anything a skeptic could answer "no" or "not me" to.

### Seeds of curiosity (Chapter 10)
Short bridge sentences at the end of a paragraph that give a reason to read on, the same trick a TV host uses before a break. Stock patterns: "But there's more." "So read on." "But I didn't stop there." "Let me explain." "Now here comes the good part." "Here's what happened next." "And that's not all." Also the front-loaded seed: promise a payoff early and deliver it only at the end.

*Apply:* plant one seed wherever copy slows (specs, price justification). One per slow spot; more feels mechanical. Audit every paragraph break for forward pull.

### Assumed constraints (Chapter 9)
A grown elephant stays tied to a small stake because as a calf the chain hurt when it pulled. The nine-dot puzzle is solved only by drawing outside the square the dots imply. Sugarman's own list of "can't be done" that he did: a used aircraft sold by ad in ten days above the expected price, a $600 pinball machine by mail, ten million pairs of one sunglass style by mail, toll-free credit-card ordering with no signature. His broker's "wrong" mailing list (company presidents) beat all eight of his sensible ones.

*Apply:* when you hear "that won't work for this audience / medium / price," write the belief down, ask where it came from, and test it with a small run.

## 4. Concept, Emotion, Length, Voice, Sequence

### Sell the concept, not the product (Chapter 12)
- A walkie-talkie became "Pocket CB" (pocket was the idea; CBs existed). A calculator with a text display became "Pocket Yellow Pages," sold through the scene of pulling it out at a phone booth. A smoke detector became "The Nose," a thing on the ceiling that sniffs the air.
- A chess computer sold through three successive story-concepts: a challenge against Soviet technology, a threatened lawsuit, and finally the champion's acceptance. Competitors selling "chess computers" failed.
- **Price changes the concept.** Identical copy read as serious radio at $39.95, walkie-talkie at $29.95, toy at $19.95.
- When a category matures, stop describing and start positioning: each digital-watch ad needed its own concept (thinnest, built-in alarm, laser-sealed).

*Apply:* name the concept in a phrase before writing. If you can only name the product, you're not ready. Turn endorsements, news, and setbacks into narrative, not static claims. Set price to match the concept.

### Copy as emotion (Chapter 11)
Find the unspoken emotional driver (prestige, membership, showing off, relief), then supply the logical reasons the buyer will repeat to a spouse or boss. A line can be illogical and still work if it conveys the right feeling ("prompt and courteous refund"). Choose the *true* word with the better charge; never bend facts for an emotional word.

*Apply:* open with the buyer living the moment; put specs in the justification block. On each edit pass, swap flat nouns and verbs for true synonyms with the right connotation.

### How long? (Chapter 14)
Length is irrelevant when involvement is intense (you'd read three thousand words if the headline named your family as heirs). Two things demand more copy:
1. **Price.** Higher price needs more justification. A price so low it seems unbelievable also needs *more* copy, for credibility. Education raises perceived value.
2. **Unusual products.** The stranger it is, the more you must relate it to the reader and explain it.

Short copy works when the environment is built elsewhere: a beautifully photographed catalog with very low prices needs little text.

*Apply:* set length from price, novelty, reader intensity, and how much environment the visuals and format already supply. Never cut copy that a genuinely interested prospect would want.

### Personal communication (Chapter 15)
Rewrite "We at ABC invite all of you" as one person remembering you from last time and offering to demo the machine personally. Include one concrete personal detail a form letter couldn't contain. Use a byline; it licenses "I" and "you." Pick a persona and keep every touchpoint consistent with it (a discount club that mailed in leftover envelopes and used novelty membership cards built real affection).

### The copy sequence (Chapter 16)
Build order: headline → subhead → caption for the imagined image → first sentence → down the slide → block-diagram the finished piece as a one-way flowchart where each block answers the next question the reader would ask.

Sugarman's content order for a $650 pinball machine, reusable for any considered purchase:
1. Hook and excitement.
2. Drama and difference (what's genuinely new).
3. How it works, how it differs, unique features.
4. Construction, quality, more features.
5. **Justification for the emotional purchase** (cost vs. alternatives; a business use; who else will enjoy it).
6. **Lasting value** (answers "will it gather dust?").
7. **Service** (answers "what if it breaks?").
8. Ask for the order.

*Apply:* list the objections a face-to-face buyer would raise, in the order they'd raise them; make each a copy block in that order.

## 5. The 64-Point Checklist

Sugarman's seminar promised sixty-four things every ad should cover: 10 graphic elements, 23 copy elements, 31 psychological triggers. They vary wildly in weight. Paragraph headings are near-cosmetic; honesty, raise-and-resolve, and satisfaction conviction can double response.

### 5a. The 10 graphic elements (Chapter 4)

| # | Element | Conventional job | Real job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headline | Grab attention | Get the subhead read |
| 2 | Subheadline | Add information | Get the caption / first sentence read |
| 3 | Photo or drawing | Show the product | Pull the eye toward the copy |
| 4 | Caption | Describe the image (widely read) | Get the first sentence read |
| 5 | Copy | Carry the sell | Keep the reader sliding |
| 6 | Paragraph headings | Chunk the text | Make the copy look less daunting |
| 7 | Logo | Identify the seller | Lend familiarity, then step aside |
| 8 | Price | State the cost | Be findable in one scan |
| 9 | Response device | Take the order | Look unmistakably orderable |
| 10 | Overall layout | Arrange the rest | Set the buying environment |

*Apply:* audit each element with one question: does it push the eye into sentence one? If it only informs or brands, rework it.

### 5b. The 23 copy elements (Chapter 18)

1. **Typeface.** Legibility beats style. Serif body copy tested far better for comprehension. Never pick a font for brand at the cost of reading.
2. **First sentence.** Short, easy, no facts yet.
3. **Second sentence.** Nearly as important. First two paragraphs exist only to keep the reader moving and set the mood.
4. **Paragraph headings.** Break up the wall; they barely sell (nonsense headings in a test drew no comments). Place mid-column, never side by side.
5. **Product explanation.** Explain a complicated product simply and a simple product in depth (a premium smoke detector outsold cheaper ones by narrating its internals). Have others read the draft and list the questions they still have.
6. **New features.** Give the differentiators their own emphasis, separate from the general explanation.
7. **Technical explanation.** One dense, accurate expert paragraph signals mastery even if the reader can't follow it. Must be real; readers see through fakes.
8. **Anticipate objections.** You aren't in the room. Raise the obvious objection yourself, early. Ignoring it reads as evasion.
9. **Resolve objections.** Every raised objection gets a concrete, truthful answer (snap-out circuit boards; "replace the battery every six months").
10. **Gender / audience fit.** Know who buys and speak in their terms; screen for anything that alienates part of the audience.
11. **Clarity.** Short, simple, direct. Big words mark you as pompous. The technical paragraph is the one exception.
12. **Clichés.** If it could appear in a generic ad for any product, cut it.
13. **Rhythm.** Deliberately irregular sentence lengths. Group examples in threes.
14. **Service.** For anything expensive or awkward to return, a dedicated paragraph on what happens if it breaks. Make it generous (a five-year warranty with door pickup and a loaner built a superb customer list).
15. **Physical facts.** Weight, dimensions, speed, limits: all of them. Any missing spec becomes the excuse not to order.
16. **Trial period.** Mandatory for anything the buyer can't touch first. Longer trials produce *fewer* returns.
17. **Price comparison.** Anchor against a named alternative. Comparisons must be accurate and fair.
18. **Testimonials.** Endorsers must fit (an astronaut for a space-age alarm). The "reverse testimonial" turns a rival's celebrity fee into your price advantage. Never fabricate.
19. **Price.** Big if price is the argument; findable but underplayed if it isn't. Readers look for it at unpredictable moments, so it must be scannable.
20. **Offer summary.** Near the end, restate exactly what they get and for how much. Widely omitted.
21. **Avoid saying too much.** Draft everything, then cut half to four-fifths. A succinct salesperson outsells a talkative one.
22. **Ease of ordering.** The response mechanism must be visually unmistakable (dotted coupon borders tested better than plain).
23. **Ask for the order.** After the summary, explicitly. Inexperienced salespeople finish selling and never ask.

### 5c. The 31 psychological triggers (Chapter 19)

Each: what it is → how to apply it.

1. **Involvement / ownership.** Get the reader mentally handling the product; describe what they'll do *when it arrives*. Involvement devices (find the misspellings, earn a discount per error) look juvenile and can double or triple response. → Second person, present tense, sensory verbs; add a task tied to the product where possible.
2. **Honesty.** If you keep one trigger, keep this. Readers collectively smell dishonesty even while skimming. → State the negatives yourself, early, then contextualize them.
3. **Integrity.** Layout and type reflect character. Screaming bars, underlines, and hyped photos read as low integrity. → Let restraint signal trust.
4. **Credibility.** A too-good price needs a believable reason (closeout, bulk buy). Unresolved objections, hidden faults, and clichés destroy it. Credibility can be borrowed from venue, brand, endorser, even company name. → Justify every remarkable claim; resolve all objections; choose names carefully.
5. **Value and proof of value.** Even the rich want assurance they aren't being taken. Teaching value equals lowering price. → Compare, or educate on intrinsic worth.
6. **Justify the purchase.** "Can I justify this?" unanswered becomes "I'll think about it." Scale justification to price. → Write the rationalization the buyer will need to tell their spouse.
7. **Greed.** Bargains sell unneeded things; too low a price kills credibility unless explained. → Use bargain pricing deliberately, always with a reason it's possible.
8. **Establish authority.** Say something in every piece that positions you as the specialist. Keep the line running even when *you're* tired of it. → Craft a true authority tagline and keep it.
9. **Satisfaction conviction.** More than a guarantee: a gesture so confident the reader thinks "they must really believe in this." Replacing a plain refund with a refund *plus interest* doubled response in one test. → At the close, over-answer the final hesitation.
10. **Nature of the product.** Every product has a personality: a toy is fun, a blood-pressure monitor is serious, an alarm must be reliable and easy to install. → One sentence stating the product's essential nature; let it govern tone.
11. **Nature of the prospect.** Sell to the emotional need, not the feature. → Profile the driver before writing.
12. **Current fads.** Ride them briefly and consciously; don't let a fad define a lasting product. → Use current vocabulary; plan the exit.
13. **Timing.** Nobody can predict readiness; test small, watch external events (a presidential plea against credit cards crashed response overnight). → Test before committing.
14. **Linking.** Bridge the new to something already understood ("vitamins for your car"; a nose on the ceiling). Avoid links with negative associations. → Name the familiar thing your product resembles.
15. **Consistency.** Make the first commitment tiny; once a customer, they act consistently and accept add-ons. → Simple initial offer; plan the upsell for the moment after "yes."
16. **Harmonize with the market.** Sell what the market already buys before what you'd rather sell; the fix for an out-of-harmony product is usually removing a feature, cutting price, or simplifying. → Reshape the offer to fit demand; earn the right to be different later.
17. **Desire to belong.** People buy to identify with the group that owns it. → Show who already owns it and make that group attractive.
18. **Desire to collect.** An existing buyer is the best prospect for another unit. → Design offers as series; market to past buyers of the same category.
19. **Curiosity.** The strongest mail-order motivator. Withhold what can only be experienced by buying (never showing the view through the sunglasses, only people's reactions). → Identify what can't be experienced remotely and tease it.
20. **Sense of urgency.** "Let me think about it" means no. Devices: real price rises, last ad, closeouts, limited-time bonus, ship-by dates. Invented scarcity torches integrity at the close. Urgency is wasted if any fact is missing. → End with a true, specific reason to act today.
21. **Fear.** Sells protection during genuine risk; use carefully. → Name a real risk plainly; position the product as protection.
22. **Instant gratification.** Retail's edge; compensate with explicit fast shipping and service. → State delivery speed as a benefit.
23. **Exclusivity / rarity.** "Only six in the state, one already gone" closed Sugarman on a snowmobile he'd been mocking. → Where true, say how few exist.
24. **Simplicity.** One product, one offer, one price. One black watch outpulled nine styles three to one. → Remove every element that complicates the decision.
25. **Human relationships.** Byline, first person, a hand holding the product, light humor, a story. → Write as one person to another; add faces, hands, voice.
26. **Storytelling.** We're primed for stories from childhood; the best salespeople always have one. → Open with a relevant discovery story.
27. **Mental engagement.** The mind enjoys reaching a conclusion it reaches itself. State that the rival watch retails at $300 and costs jewelers $150, and let the reader conclude who's profiting. → Lay out the facts and stop one step short.
28. **Guilt.** Over-deliver useful information so not responding feels ungrateful; repeated contact with small gifts compounds it. → Give more than you ask for.
29. **Specificity.** "Ninety-two percent of new dentists" beats "dentists everywhere." Exact figures read as research. → Replace every vague adjective with a number, mechanism, or named fact.
30. **Familiarity.** A known logo and format is a friend among strangers. Never retire a campaign because *you're* tired of it; stop only when sales fall. → Consistent look and voice; run winners until the numbers say stop.
31. **Hope.** The implied possibility of a benefit keeps people buying; a specific dated promise, once missed, ends it. → Imply results through testimonials and experience; never attach a quantified deadline.

**Cross-cutting patterns**
- **Two critical positions: the very beginning and the very end.** The opening only starts the slide. The close must, in little space, summarize the offer, justify, add satisfaction conviction, create urgency, ease ordering, and ask.
- **Raise-and-resolve is the credibility engine.** Surface every objection yourself and over-answer it.
- **The trust stack:** honesty → integrity → credibility → authority → specificity → technical explanation → familiarity. Different levers on one question: can I believe you?
- **Emotion buys, logic justifies.** Sell on involvement, belonging, exclusivity, hope, curiosity; then supply value proof, comparisons, physical facts, and justification so the buyer can defend the purchase.

## 6. Cure vs. Prevention, and Readability

### Sell the cure (Chapter 20, Axiom 15)
Nobody thinks the bad thing will happen to them, so preventives are a hard sell; once it has happened, the same person pays many times more for the fix. Cures command a premium; preventives are price-sensitive.

Four questions for any product: Is it a preventive or a cure? Can it be *positioned* as a cure? Is a trend shifting perception (rising theft turned a steering-wheel lock into a cure)? Or is it a pure preventive with too small a market?

Three repositioning moves:
1. **Find the sub-audience for whom it's already a cure** (a burglar alarm is prevention for most, a cure for the just-robbed). Advertise calmly so the merely concerned *file the ad* and call months later; write copy that survives being saved.
2. **Lead with curative claims, underplay preventive ones** ("stops knock, eliminates ping, passes the emissions test you just failed" rather than "prevents injector fouling").
3. **Ride the trend** when perception is already moving.

Breakthrough products are the hardest sell because nobody believes the claim; budget extra credibility work.

### Readability (Chapter 21)
Score the opening hundred words with a fog-index method: average sentence length plus percentage of three-syllable words, times 0.4, gives a grade level. Mass-market openings should land around grade five to eight. Sugarman's most successful ad opened at 4.9 and rose to 7.2 only in the third block, once the reader was committed. Don't chase a low score at the cost of sense; vary sentence length, use a long word when it's the right one.

## 7. Lessons from the 13 Annotated Ads

Technique only; the ads themselves are not reproduced.

| Ad | What it demonstrates | Reusable lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Lazy Man's Way to Riches** ($10 book, ~3M sold) | Product withheld for most of the ad; margin disclosed openly; cheque not cashed for 31 days; success kept relatable (a Cadillac, not a Rolls); a "doesn't require" list broadens the market; sworn accountant's statement above the coupon. | Sell the response before revealing the product; keep proof of success within the reader's reach. |
| **Fluke of Nature** (grapefruit club, written by the farmer) | Opens "I'm a farmer"; origin story; rarity ladder (not in stores → only 4–5% of the crop qualifies); obsessive grading detail as expertise; free trial box carries the risk. | Give a commodity an origin story, a rarity ladder, and a selection process narrated in the seller's own voice. |
| **Lingerie for Men** (catalog request) | Objection answered at the instant it forms; department-store humiliation contrasted; reader complimented as a connoisseur. **Failed close:** no guarantee, no credit of the catalog fee against a first order, no byline. | Never let a good story fizzle at the close; the guarantee and incentive belong at the decision point. |
| **The More You Learn** (aircraft maker after a crash) | Persuasive engineering copy undermined by a missing front end: it resolved the objection without raising it, so silence read as evasion. Rewrite names the crash first. | Raise the embarrassing objection first, then resolve it. |
| **A More Stimulating Way** (corporate video, agency ad) | Unreadable all-caps headline, no subhead, a first sentence that lists every business medium. Consistency with a "boring" theme is no virtue. | Every ad needs a readable headline, a short first sentence, and a story. Tell one customer's switch and result. |
| **Magic Baloney** (thermostat, ran 3 years) | Reverse pitch: promises to tear the product apart and does. Leads with the objection that actually blocks the sale (installation) and defuses it. Company-trust objection raised late and answered with due diligence. | Voice the worst things a prospect will think, then convert them one by one, starting with the real blocker. |
| **Pet Plane** (used aircraft, one buyer needed) | Every spec delivered inside a story about the loyal mechanic; care detail proves condition; price anchored to a newer equivalent; self-mockery placed at the very end. | One high-ticket item sells on a warm personal story that carries the specs; humor must never displace the offer. |
| **Mail Order Mansion** ($6M house; failed) | All narrative, a "why am I here?" thread, guarantee as both joke and close. Heavy publicity, no sale. | Attention isn't response; check the offer fits the medium and audience before buying space. |
| **Hungarian Conspiracy** (puzzle game) | Parody news report urging you *not* to buy; offer withheld to the last paragraph; real product facts complete inside the joke. | A consistent comic premise can carry a full feature story if the facts underneath are real. |
| **Vision Breakthrough** (sunglasses launch) | Two-word headline that refuses to say "sunglasses"; first paragraph promises a true story; discovery via a trusted friend in dialogue; educational payload reframes the category; curiosity only a purchase can satisfy; specs only after belief. | Reposition a familiar product as a discovery, teach something that reframes the category, and make buying the only way to resolve the curiosity. |
| **Gold Space Chains** (off-brand item in a gadget catalog) | A story of a persistent salesman and principled refusals lets the seller flatter customers while appearing not to sell. Sold out, but Sugarman credits a gold-price spike and owns lines that alienated women readers. | When a product doesn't belong, invent a story-based reason for its presence, and reread for anything that alienates part of the audience. |
| **Consumers Hero** ($5 club membership) | One-word headline; "stealing" reframed as Robin Hood; open economics of a refurbished clock radio justify both margin and bargain; membership fee refunded with interest if you never buy. | Match the customer's register completely, show the economics openly, and guarantee even the entry fee. |
| **Nautilus Spelling Sale** (closeout, price floor imposed) | Listed above retail with 25 planted misspellings worth $10 each; readers earn their own discount; rules double as humor; non-buyers can enter for a catalog credit. | When price is constrained, build a mechanism that lets the reader earn the discount; involvement can beat a plain price cut. |

## 8. Writing for Different Media (Chapter 36)

Everything above transfers; only the weighting changes. Draft the sell as a print ad first, whatever the final medium, because words alone force you to find the real selling essence.

- **Catalogs / product pages.** Cover (or hero) is the headline. A proprietor's letter with photo up front; a store without a visible owner feels cold. Per item: short headline → subhead → exhaustive first-person copy. Omitting one small fact (even weight) gives the buyer an excuse to defer. Contact info on every page; script the upsell for whoever answers.
- **After-the-sale inserts.** Every delivery, invoice, or completed job is a free ad slot to an already-trusting reader.
- **Direct mail.** The most personal copy you write. The letter is the engine; brochures illustrate but never close. Envelope is the headline. The PS is often the most-read line: restate the offer. Own recent buyers are the hottest list. Always split-test.
- **Newspapers.** Mail-order ads must pay out on one insertion; retail and service ads need frequency, and response builds over repeats. A local service business belongs in the community weekly with the same format, a regular slot, and rotating copy.
- **Billboards, truck and yard signs.** One idea, one line, one image, graspable at speed.
- **News releases.** Write it as the editor would print it; find the trend hook; supply a quotable line. Head it "News Release."
- **Radio.** One core message per spot, news tone, curiosity open, a spoken response mechanism that survives misspelling. Buy repetition over reach.
- **Short-form TV.** Impulse price, value that looks almost too good, one offer with no choices, urgency, web plus phone, mass appeal. Bonus gifts must appeal to the whole audience or they kill the main sale.
- **Infomercials.** Mimic whatever show format is currently popular; needs margin or a back-end.
- **Back-end.** The moment after "yes" is a major selling moment: related items, deluxe versions, second-unit discounts, continuity. Match follow-up channel to acquisition channel.

### The internet: what still holds and what's dated
Sugarman wrote this section in 2006. **Still holds:** subject line as headline (short, trusted sender or a topic the reader cares about); short emails linking to long copy; opt-in lists and value-first relationship building (educate, give, then ask); long web copy that justifies price with a CTA visible on every screen (today: sticky buttons), because web readers won't scroll to the bottom for the offer the way print readers skip to the coupon; headings that link to checkout; real-name and video testimonials; the author's photo; exhaustive product descriptions (most stores fail on thin copy; people happily read about what they're about to buy, and it lowers returns); the fewest clicks to purchase; same-day shipping; cheap constant testing down to single words; partner-list launches. **Dated:** keyword-based spam-filter tricks, catalogs and TV as the main feeder of web traffic, thirty-page HTML sales letters as the default, celebrity-name headlines for search, seeded viral games, and the absence of mobile, social, paid search, and analytics.

## 9. Pre-Ship Checklist (Sugarman pass)

Run this on any long-form draft before handing it over.

**Opening**
- [ ] First sentence under ten words, no benefit, no feature, creates an itch.
- [ ] First three sentences read aloud without a stumble; each only earns the next.
- [ ] Opening two or three statements are ones the target reader cannot say "no" or "not me" to.
- [ ] Buying environment set by the tone and vocabulary of the first paragraphs, not just the design.
- [ ] Opening hundred words score around grade five to eight.

**Middle**
- [ ] A concept is named, not just a product.
- [ ] Every paragraph break has a forward pull; one curiosity seed at each slow point.
- [ ] Objections listed in the order a live buyer would raise them; each raised *and* resolved in the copy.
- [ ] One dense, true technical paragraph proving expertise.
- [ ] Every physical fact present (size, weight, time, limits, what's included).
- [ ] Every vague adjective replaced with a number, mechanism, or named fact.
- [ ] Emotional driver named; logical justification supplied after it.
- [ ] Copy written as one person to one person, signed.

**Close**
- [ ] Offer summary: exactly what they get and for how much.
- [ ] Justification the buyer can repeat to someone else.
- [ ] Satisfaction conviction beyond the expected guarantee, answering the last hesitation.
- [ ] A true, specific reason to act now.
- [ ] Ordering is unmistakable and one step.
- [ ] The copy explicitly asks.

**Edit**
- [ ] Draft cut by roughly half; every "that," intensifier, and throat-clearing opener hunted.
- [ ] Sentence lengths irregular; examples grouped in threes.
- [ ] Nothing a skeptic could catch as untrue; negatives disclosed by us, not discovered by them.
- [ ] Incubated at least overnight before the final read.

## 11. What's New in the 1998 Edition

Sugarman's earlier book states the same seminar material, mostly identically, but with one real historical difference and two chapters the 2007 edition compressed into single triggers rather than keeping as full chapters.

### The trigger list grew from 24 to 31

The 1998 book explicitly calls its psychological-triggers chapter a list of **24** triggers ("Some of the 24 psychological triggers to buying may not have been obvious to you"). By 2007, the same chapter had grown to 31. Sugarman kept refining and adding triggers over the intervening decade — the 31-trigger list in Section 5c above is the mature version and the one to use; this note is here only so the discrepancy doesn't look like an error if you ever see both books cited together. The 1998 book's own axiom numbering also differs from the 2007 book's (cure-vs-prevention is Axiom 15 in the 2007 book; the 1998 printing's OCR is too degraded to state its number with confidence, but it is clearly a lower number, confirming the axioms were renumbered, not just added to, as the list grew).

### Getting the Mind to Work (a full chapter in 1998; folded into Trigger 27 by 2007)

**The claim:** the more effort a reader's mind has to spend to reach a conclusion on its own, the more enjoyable and persuasive the copy feels. Sugarman ties this to a whole-brain theory of engagement — thinking, intuition, sensation, and emotion all firing together — and warns that copy which spells out its own conclusion patronizes the reader and reads as flat.

**The Seiko example** (the same one behind Trigger 27's one-line summary in Section 5c, given in full here): the ad states the rival Seiko sells for $300 and costs jewelers $150, then stops — never adding the obvious next line, that jewelers are making a fortune on every sale. The reader reaches that conclusion unprompted, and Sugarman argues the unstated conclusion lands harder than a stated one would have.

**The jogging-machine counter-example (new, not in the 2007 book):** Sugarman ran an ad for an indoor jogging platform showing only the unit and its digital distance readout — deliberately withholding a photo of someone actually jogging on it, on the theory that showing too much would rob the reader of the small mental work of picturing themselves using it. It sold well. His Japanese supplier pushed back, insisted a photo of a person jogging on the unit would obviously sell better, and got their way — full-color, with a model shown using it. The ad bombed. Sugarman offers this as a direct, costed lesson: showing the reader the conclusion instead of letting them reach it can kill an otherwise-working ad.

**Apply:** for any claim with an obvious, slightly uncomfortable implication (a markup, a competitor's weakness, a social contrast), state the facts and stop one line short of the conclusion — and before adding an image or line that "helpfully" completes the picture for the reader, ask whether it's removing the small mental work that was making the ad land.

### Telling a Story (a full chapter in 1998; folded into Trigger 26 by 2007)

Sugarman states plainly that his most successful print campaigns — BluBlocker, the Bone Fone, the Magic Stat thermostat ("Magic Baloney") — all used a story as the ad's structural spine, not just an opening hook. Three examples given in full here, in more narrative depth than the compressed ad summaries in Section 7:

- **BluBlocker ("Vision Breakthrough"):** opens as a first-person true story about a friend named Len raving about a pair of sunglasses before any product claim is made — the story itself is the vehicle that gets the reader through the ad's UV/blue-light education and into the pitch. Reported result: 100,000 pairs at $59.95 from this ad alone, then roughly 8 million pairs over six years once it moved to infomercial.
- **"Mail Order Mansion":** the story (an invitation to a real-estate developer's Malibu party, a private jet, the reveal of a $6 million house for sale) runs nearly the *entire length* of the ad, with the actual offer only summarized at the very end — the story is not decoration on top of the pitch, it structurally *is* the ad.
- **"A Fluke of Nature"** (Frank Schultz's grapefruit ad, written right after attending Sugarman's seminar): opens "I'm a farmer," then spends five paragraphs on the discovery story before the product claims arrive — Sugarman notes the ad ran in some form for 18 years.

**Apply:** when a product is plain or the category is dull, don't reach for a stronger claim — reach for a story that can carry the reader through the education and the pitch on its own, the way BluBlocker's origin story did more work than any UV-protection statistic could have alone.
