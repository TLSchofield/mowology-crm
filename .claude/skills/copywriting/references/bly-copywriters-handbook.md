# Bly's Copywriter's Handbook — Frameworks, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Robert W. Bly's *The Copywriter's Handbook* (4th edition, 2020). **Source caveat:** the owner's file is a third-party chapter-summary edition of the book, not the full text, and each chapter is truncated partway through. What survives intact is Bly's framework lists (headline types, the 4 U's, the motivating sequence, the clear-copy tips, the media-specific checklists) plus the summary's Q&A. Treat this file as a reliable index of *what Bly's frameworks are*, and as a thinner source than `sugarman-handbook.md` for *how to execute them*. Where the two authors overlap, the Sugarman file carries the execution detail; where Bly adds a framework Sugarman lacks (headline taxonomy, BDF, brochure and landing-page checklists, content marketing), it lives here.

Use this reference when you need a **taxonomy or checklist to reason from** (which kind of headline, which motivator, what a service brochure must contain) rather than a full method for long copy.

## 1. Bly's Stance

- A copywriter is a salesperson working in print. The job is to sell, not to entertain or win awards. Simplicity usually outsells cleverness.
- Copy cannot create desire; it channels desire that already exists toward a specific product (the Schwartz idea, restated).
- Three forces now shape effective copy: human emotion (unchanged), data analytics (what actually converts), and platform compliance (an ad that violates a platform's rules never runs at all).
- Old-school principles survive the internet because information overload and skepticism make relevance and clarity *more* valuable, not less.
- Humor is a double-edged sword: unless the joke is welded to the product, viewers remember the joke and forget the brand.
- People still read. They read *relevant, concise* copy. "Nobody reads anymore" is a myth serious buyers disprove daily.

*Apply:* judge every draft by whether it moves a reader toward a purchase, not by whether it is admired.

## 2. Headlines

### The four functions of a headline
1. **Get attention** by appealing to self-interest or promising news.
2. **Select the audience**: name who the ad is for so the right reader stops and the wrong one moves on ("To homeowners over 65 who…").
3. **Deliver a complete message**: many readers see nothing else, so the headline alone should carry the core promise.
4. **Draw the reader into the body copy** with curiosity or an unfinished promise.

### The eight headline types
1. **Direct**: states the selling proposition outright.
2. **Indirect**: raises curiosity that only the body resolves.
3. **News**: announces something new or improved ("Introducing," "Now," "At last").
4. **How-to**: promises useful information or a solution.
5. **Question**: asks something the reader would answer "yes, that's me" to.
6. **Command**: tells the reader what to do.
7. **Reason-why**: numbers the reasons or steps ("7 ways…").
8. **Testimonial**: lets a customer's words be the headline.

### The 4 U's
A headline should be **Urgent** (a reason to act now), **Unique** (something new, or something familiar presented freshly), **Ultra-specific** (precise details, not generalities), and **Useful** (a benefit the reader wants). Score a draft headline on each; strengthen the weakest two.

### Other headline rules
- Promise a benefit, be clear, be specific, relate logically to the product.
- Words that pull: free, how to, new, easy, guarantee, save, results.
- Avoid puns and wordplay; they distract from the selling message.
- Keep a swipe file of proven headlines by type and adapt the structure, not the words.
- To generate headlines: write down the customer, the product's features, the competitors, and the key buyer benefit, then draft one headline per type.

*Apply:* draft eight headlines, one per type, then score each on the 4 U's and pick the most specific believable one.

## 3. Writing to Communicate

Research cited by Bly: ads that are easy to understand sell more. Most ads fail on clarity, not cleverness.

### Eleven rules for clear copy
1. **Put the reader first.** Write in "you."
2. **Organize the selling points** so the main message and the secondary ones are in a logical order.
3. **Break copy into short sections** with headings.
4. **Short sentences**, averaging around fourteen to sixteen words.
5. **Small, simple words.**
6. **No technical jargon** unless the reader uses it.
7. **Be concise**; cut every word that doesn't earn its place.
8. **Be specific**; concrete facts build credibility.
9. **Go straight to the point.** Start with the selling point; don't warm up to it.
10. **Friendly, conversational style**, like a letter to one person.
11. **Avoid sexist language**; it narrows the audience for no gain.

### Tricks of the trade
End sentences with prepositions when it sounds natural. Use fragments for rhythm. Start sentences with "And" or "But." Use one-sentence paragraphs. Use bold, italics, and bullets to guide the eye.

### Bullets that pull
Be specific about the *problem* and vague about the *solution*, so the reader must keep going (or buy) to learn the answer.

### The communicate checklist
Does the copy fulfil the headline's promise? Is it interesting and easy to read? Does it establish believability? Does it offer specifics? Does it flow smoothly and end by asking for action?

### Print vs. screen
Serif type for print, sans-serif for screens. Shorter paragraphs online. Digital copy can be corrected and tested cheaply; use that.

*Apply:* after drafting, run the eleven rules as an edit pass, then the five-question checklist.

## 4. Writing to Sell

### Features vs. benefits
A feature is a fact about the product; a benefit is what that fact does for the buyer. Build a two-column list before writing and sell from the right-hand column. Features still belong in the copy for technical or specialized buyers, placed after the benefit that makes them matter.

### The motivating sequence (Bly's expansion of AIDA)
1. **Get attention.**
2. **Show a need**: state the problem the reader has, even one they haven't named.
3. **Satisfy the need**: position the product as the solution.
4. **Prove it**: testimonials, demonstrations, comparisons, data.
5. **Ask for action**: a clear, specific next step.

### "False logic"
Facts framed so the reader reaches the conclusion you want, while every statement stays true. It is emotion-first persuasion with a logical trail the buyer can use to justify the purchase. Never cross into misstatement.

### Unique selling proposition
One clear advantage competitors don't offer, stated so the reader has a reason to choose you.

### The secondary promise
When the main promise sounds too good, add a smaller, entirely believable promise. The skeptic buys on the small promise and hopes for the big one.

### Know the customer: the BDF formula
Write down the target's **Beliefs** (what they hold true about the problem and the category), **Desires** (what they want), and **Feelings** (what they fear, resent, or hope for). Copy that addresses all three reaches the reader on intellectual, emotional, and personal levels.

### Copy motivators (a starter list)
To be liked, to be appreciated, to feel important, to make money, to save money, to save time, to feel secure, to be comfortable, to be convenient, to avoid effort, to be healthy, to gain knowledge. Pick the one or two that drive *this* purchase.

### Long vs. short copy
Length follows the product, the audience, and the purpose. High-involvement or emotional purchases need long copy to answer every question; low-involvement, familiar, or impulse products can be sold short.

### Generational marketing and positioning
Each cohort has distinct values and vocabulary; write to the cohort you're targeting. Positioning is the place the product occupies in the reader's mind relative to alternatives; the copy must establish it, not just list benefits.

*Apply:* before drafting, fill in the BDF sheet, the feature/benefit table, the USP, and the secondary promise. Then order the body by the five-step motivating sequence.

## 5. Getting Ready to Write

### Research, three ways
1. Start with the client: existing ads, brochures, market research, customer letters, competitor literature.
2. Hire or use a researcher for data and statistics.
3. Do your own digging, judging sources by expertise and trustworthiness (peer-reviewed, official, financial).

### Four-step preparation
1. Gather everything previously published about the product.
2. Ask about features, benefits, and what's unique.
3. Ask about the audience: who buys, why, and who influences the decision.
4. Fix the objective: sales, leads, awareness, or traffic.

### Interviews
Product experts know facts the documents omit. Prepare specific questions, be respectful of time, and record answers.

### Organize, outline, document
Type up and filter your notes so the facts are familiar before drafting. Outline longer pieces and get the outline approved to avoid a full rewrite. Footnote sources so claims can be verified.

### The writing process
Get the ideas down, edit, proofread. Expect several drafts.

### Nine-step method for producing selling ideas
1. Identify the problem.
2. Assemble the pertinent facts.
3. Gather general knowledge.
4. Look for combinations of existing ideas.
5. Sleep on it.
6. Use a checklist to prompt more angles.
7. Get feedback.
8. Team up with someone who thinks differently.
9. Give new ideas a chance before criticizing them.

*Apply:* no draft before the four-step prep is on paper.

## 6. Format Checklists (Print and Traditional)

### Print ads: four missions
Direct sale (mail order; long, complete, answers every objection and explains ordering), lead generation (invite an inquiry, don't tell everything), awareness (build familiarity over time), and traffic (send people to a store or site). Know which one the ad is for before writing. Newspapers suit broad, immediate, price-led messages; magazines suit targeted audiences and better reproduction.

### Direct mail
- The most personal medium: first person, conversational, as if to a friend.
- **The letter sells; the brochure tells.** Most of the selling happens in the letter.
- Personalization lifts response, especially with existing customers and senior buyers.
- **Fifteen ways to open a sales letter:** state the offer; highlight free literature; make an announcement; tell a story; flatter the reader; speak peer to peer; a personal message from the top; a provocative quote; a question; make it personal; name the reader's problem; stress a benefit; human interest; share inside information; a sweepstakes.
- Envelope teasers must be compelling or they backfire; a plain envelope that looks personal also works.
- Lift response with a reply mechanism, a premium, and a deadline. Newer formats (magalogs, self-mailers, video brochures) can outperform the classic package.

### Brochures: eleven tips
1. Know where in the buying process it will be used.
2. Know whether it stands alone or supports a sales call.
3. Know the audience.
4. Strong cover headline.
5. Complete information.
6. Organize selling points as a story.
7. Short sections.
8. Visuals that show the product in use.
9. Tell the reader what to do next.
10. Never omit basics (contact details, how to order).
11. Make it worth keeping (tips, reference material).

**Product brochure** contents: intro, benefits, features, how it works, applications, pricing, specs, FAQ, company, support, call to action. **Service brochure**: services offered, benefits, methodology, client list, testimonials, fees and terms, bios of key people. **Corporate brochure**: activities, philosophy, history, structure, markets, achievements, goals.

**Catalogs** differ: brief copy per item, hard sell, snappy headlines, a personal letter from the owner, key facts, best-sellers flagged, an easy order form.

### Public relations
A release informs; it doesn't sell. Editors publish news relevant to their readers and bin puff. Clear, accurate, one page ideally, contact details, a real headline, quotes from credible people. Releases now also reach consumers directly via the company site, so write them for search as well as editors. Topics: new products, promotions, partnerships, staff news, achievements, community involvement, expert opinion. The "Pizza Meter" (linking delivery spikes to national events) shows how a mundane fact becomes news.

### TV and radio
Two camps: creative spectacle vs. straightforward demonstration. Bly sides with demonstration and reason-why (the OxiClean model). Twelve formats: demonstration, testimonial, stand-up presenter, slice of life, lifestyle, animation, jingle, visual-as-hero, humor, continuing character, reason-why, emotion. Rules: visuals lead but must match the audio; win the first seconds; one selling point; show the package and repeat the name. Radio has only sound, so write vivid word-pictures and repeat the response mechanism.

## 7. Digital Checklists

### Websites
- The "organic model": make the site an expert resource, capture email with a lead magnet, then market to the house list. Most online revenue comes from that list, not from cold visitors.
- Assignments: microsites (long-copy sales pages), squeeze pages, transaction pages, emails, online ads.
- Avoid rotating hero banners (ignored by most visitors, slow, bad on mobile). Avoid single-page sites for anything needing search visibility or navigation.
- Set the site's mission and metrics before changing anything based on opinion.
- SEO copy: one keyword theme per page, keywords in titles and meta tags, no stuffing.

### Landing pages: ten conversion tips
1. Build credibility early (logos, testimonials, credentials).
2. Capture non-buyers' emails with a free report or course.
3. Lots of testimonials.
4. Lots of bullets.
5. A headline that arouses curiosity or makes a promise.
6. Conversational style.
7. An emotional hook in the headline and lead.
8. Solve the reader's problem.
9. A clear money-back guarantee.
10. Keep content accurate and current; tie to current events where relevant.
Also: no navigation menu, an uncluttered layout with one focal point, big bold CTA buttons. Companies with many landing pages generate far more leads than those with a handful.

### Email: fifteen tips
1. FROM and SUBJECT lines do the attention work.
2. Make the FROM line the person or brand the reader trusts.
3. "Team [Brand]" can read as collective support.
4. "Free" lifts response even though it risks filters; test it.
5. Strong opening line or headline.
6. A mini-message up front: the offer and a link in the first paragraph.
7. Expanded copy below for readers who want detail.
8. Repeat the offer and link in the body and at the close.
9. Few links, not many.
10. Readable formatting: wide margins, short lines.
11. Sparse capitals.
12. Shorter is usually better, linking to a long page.
13. Key information early, on the first screen.
14. Helpful, friendly, educational tone, not hard sell.
15. Clear opt-out.
Deliverability: ask readers to whitelist you at the top of the email, validate HTML, watch spam complaints. Build the list with a free newsletter of practical how-to content, then run conversion series that open with goodwill before asking.

### Online ads
Banners build awareness more than clicks; medium rectangles and wide skyscrapers perform best; keep files light; placement near where the user is reading beats the page top. PPC: set the maximum cost per click from margin, choose exact keywords, match the ad to the landing page. Newsletter ads: solo, sponsorship, or text. Facebook: bright image, concise copy, clear offer and CTA; video holds attention far longer than static. LinkedIn: professional tone, tight character limits.

### Social media
Be concise, be interesting, invite comments. Use business pages. Tailor the title and tone per platform but funnel everything to the blog or landing page. Post on a schedule with themes. Avoid politics, religion, grievances, and arguing with the uninformed; separate opinion from fact.

### Video
Script types: 15–60 second spot, explainer (1–2 min), interview, corporate, training, online (2–7 min, one topic). Start with a creative brief. Write: know the audience, concise intro, simple direct conversational language, problem then solution, show the outcome, clear CTA. Read it aloud; narration runs about 120 words a minute. Video sales letters (15–45 min): grab attention at once, tell a story, keep it simple, use visual aids, state problem and solution early, stay positive and authoritative.

### Content marketing
Free useful information that builds awareness and leads (the 1980 engineering "tray manual" that sold equipment for decades). **Seven mistakes:** mediocre writing, mediocre research, surface-level content, no expert input, insufficient subject knowledge, uncredited sources, opinion without evidence. **Four levels of content:** why (importance), what (strategy), how (instructions), done-for-you (tools and templates); higher levels build more trust. **Four formats:** read, listen, watch, do. White papers work better renamed as guides or reports; plan them in nine steps (target, problem, solution, content, media, tactics, schedule, budget, metrics). Blogs lift traffic and rankings when updated regularly. Case studies follow customer → problem → solutions considered → result → recommendation, with approved quotes.

## 8. Design and Production

- Copy is king. Serious buyers read; visuals support, never replace, the words.
- The copywriter supplies a "rough": a sketch showing where headline, subheads, body, and CTA go. Most projects don't need one; reuse past roughs.
- Layout: one focal point, a logical reading path, readability above all. Simple layouts are cheaper and usually outperform complex ones. Black type on white. Photos beat drawings. Reader-friendly fonts, especially for older audiences. Simplify order forms.
- Read the finished piece as a customer, not as an editor or advertiser.

## 9. Bly vs. Sugarman

**They agree on:** clarity over cleverness; specifics over generalities; benefits before features; long copy for considered purchases; one person writing to one person; proof before the ask; an explicit call to action; testing everything; humor only when welded to the product.

**They differ on the opening.** Bly says go straight to the selling point in line one. Sugarman says the first sentence's only job is to get the second read, and benefits come later. Reconcile by medium and awareness: for a most-aware reader on a short piece (email, ad, product page), lead with the point (Bly). For a long piece to a problem-aware reader who must be pulled in (sales page, letter), open for momentum and harmony (Sugarman).

**What Bly adds that Sugarman lacks:** a headline taxonomy and scoring formula; the BDF customer sheet; the five-step motivating sequence as a body outline; the secondary promise; format-by-format checklists for brochures, landing pages, email, PR, and content marketing.

**What Sugarman adds that Bly lacks:** the slippery slide and buying environment; the 31 psychological triggers; concept-over-product; cure-over-prevention; the ad case studies; a pre-ship checklist.
