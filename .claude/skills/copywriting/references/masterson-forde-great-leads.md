# Masterson & Forde's Great Leads — The Six Ways to Open, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Michael Masterson and John Forde's *Great Leads: The Six Easiest Ways to Start Any Sales Message*. **Source caveat:** the owner's file is a 15-page third-party reader's summary in bullet form, not the full book. What survives is the complete framework (the Rule of One, the awareness question, the direct-vs-indirect decision, the six lead types with their rules and tests) plus the authors' quoted rules of thumb; what's missing is the book's worked examples and the annotated classic letters. Treat this as a reliable map of *which lead to use and why*, and pair it with `schwartz-breakthrough-advertising.md` (awareness theory) and `sugarman-handbook.md` (how to write the copy that follows the lead).

Use this reference when you have to decide **how a page, letter or email opens**: which of six openings fits the reader's awareness, how direct to be, and what each opening must contain to work.

## 1. Why the Lead Decides the Sale

- Direct response exists to produce immediate action, and action needs both an emotional move and an intellectual justification. The lead is where the emotional move happens; the headline earns attention, the lead creates the connection.
- Copywriting advice built on static rules fails because response is contextual. The lead is the part that has to be matched to the reader, which is why the authors organise everything around who the reader is rather than around formulas.
- Big Ideas drive breakthrough copy, and Big Ideas are usually simple. The lead is where the Big Idea is first delivered.

*Apply:* spend disproportionate time on the first 300 to 600 words. If the lead is wrong for the reader, nothing below it is read.

---

## 2. The Rule of One

Not just one central idea; a fully engaging piece of copy has five "ones":

1. **One good idea.** Lead with one powerful idea, and only one.
2. **One core emotion.** That idea must produce a single emotion that compels a response.
3. **One captivating story** (or one compelling fact) that supports the idea.
4. **One single, desirable benefit.**
5. **One inevitable response.** Point the reader to one action, and only one.

The opposite is the "tossed salad": a pile of features and benefits that overwhelms and confuses. A great advertising idea is big, easy to understand, immediately convincing and clearly useful to the reader.

*Apply:* before drafting, write the five ones on one line each. If any line has an "and" in it, the lead isn't ready.

## 3. The Question That Picks the Lead

"What does your customer already know?" About who you are, about the product and the research behind it, and about himself, his problems and the other solutions on offer. Everything the reader knows before the pitch starts determines what you say and how you say it. This is customer awareness, in Schwartz's five levels:

| Level | What the reader knows | What the copy must do |
|---|---|---|
| **Most aware** | Knows the product; only needs the deal | Give the deal. Beware the "too aware" reader who has already decided against you; you'll struggle to move them. |
| **Product aware** | Knows what you sell, unsure it's right for him; reads and compares reviews for reassurance | Little explaining; mostly proving you can deliver. |
| **Solution aware** | Knows the result he wants, not that you provide it; has hope | Show you understand the outcome he wants, then that you can get him there. |
| **Problem aware** | Senses a problem, doesn't know a solution exists; has only worry | Say "I sympathise" loudly; prove you feel the pain before offering anything. |
| **Unaware** | Knows nothing beyond his own identity and opinions | The hardest reader and the source of new markets. No reason yet to trust or listen. |

Which of the six leads you use follows from where the reader sits on this line.

*Apply:* decide the awareness level of the actual traffic (ad, search query, referral, existing client) before choosing a lead. Search traffic for "strata landscaping cost" is solution-aware; a cold letter to a council is problem-aware at best.

## 4. Direct or Indirect

The cocktail-party rule: with a friend you pick up where you left off; with a stranger you break the ice first. The more aware the reader, the more direct the lead; the less aware, the more indirect. You are waging war on skepticism either way.

**Direct leads work when:**
- the product is easy to understand;
- you can make a very large promise that is easily accepted;
- you have an exceptionally good deal or guarantee;
- the reader knows and trusts you and deals with you often;
- you've made an improvement the market was already waiting for.

**Direct leads come on too strong when:**
- the reader trusts you less than you imagine;
- he doesn't believe the scope of the claim;
- he doesn't believe a solution to his problem is possible;
- he doesn't yet know there's a problem worth solving;
- your claims sound like everyone else's.

**Indirect leads work when:**
- the reader knows little or nothing about you;
- the product needs explanation;
- the reader is jaded and skeptical;
- there's a timely news connection too big to ignore;
- you're reinventing or elevating the product or its idea.

Direct leads deliver an idea the reader is ready to accept. Indirect leads supply emotional momentum and proof so the reader can finish forming conclusions he has only started to make. Indirect leads demand far more work getting inside the reader's head, and they can go too far: if he can't see the connection or what's being sold, you've lost him.

*Apply:* the rule of thumb is one line. Aware and engaged readers: direct. Less aware or skeptical readers: indirect.

## 5. The Six Leads at a Glance

From most direct to most indirect:

| # | Lead | Directness | Best for | Opens with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Offer | Most direct | Most aware | The deal: price, discount, premium, guarantee |
| 2 | Promise | Direct | Product aware, on the fence | The biggest claim |
| 3 | Problem-Solution | Middle | Problem aware | The reader's most emotional, relevant worry |
| 4 | Big Secret | Indirect | Solution aware, curious | A tease of hidden knowledge, revealed late |
| 5 | Proclamation | Indirect | Unaware | A bold, emotionally charged statement or forecast |
| 6 | Story | Most indirect | Unaware or skeptical | A story that carries the promise |

All six work best when focused on one unifying big idea. The two most universally useful are the Story and the Secret.

## 6. The Offer Lead

The most direct opening; it goes straight to the deal. Offer leads mention product, price, discounts, premiums and guarantees very early, often in the headline. The "invitation" (become a member, try the product) is the subtler variant and works when the thing is anticipated or exclusive, because it uses the desire to be included.

**The formula most offer leads share:**
1. Focus immediately on the most emotionally compelling detail of the offer.
2. Underscore the most valuable benefit of that deal.
3. Elaborate that same deal-benefit in the lead that follows.
4. Include a compelling reason why you're making the offer.

Attach a "catch" so the reader feels she's getting both a valuable offer and a steal.

**When to use it:** easy-to-explain products, readers who already know you, the product and its market value. It works best with the most aware; with the "too aware" and skeptical it can be suicide, and you have to go indirect to rebuild trust.

**What to test:** most-aware readers respond to price itself (a discount, a last chance before a rise, a countdown). Slightly less aware readers need a push: free trial, extra premium, iron-clad guarantee. Anything testable in a closing offer is testable in an offer lead. Offer and Promise leads are the two that may be blended with other lead types without breaking the Rule of One.

**Checklist for offer leads:** endorsements help; know the goal; test offer-up-front against CTA-at-the-end; stay short if you can; when in doubt, do the math; find clever ways to price; test time limits; make it easy; provide a parachute (a way out); test highlighting the guarantee.

**A warning about "free":** free works when the gift is nearly as valuable as the product. Piling freebies onto a high-value product can cheapen it.

*Apply:* the offer lead is the right opening for an existing client renewal or a seasonal add-on (a fall cleanup for a current maintenance client). It is the wrong opening for a cold council.

## 7. The Promise Lead

The most common lead. Slightly less direct than the offer because the product is mentioned a little later, but it still opens with the product's biggest claim; in the classic form the promise is the headline, the first line and often the last line too.

- Every lead contains a promise, so often you only need to strip the copy down to the pure promise underneath.
- Best for "mostly aware" readers who are almost ready to buy and need a nudge.
- Advertising works when it promises something people want, not when it is clever, original or shocking. Deliver the emotional promise "barely behind the words": friendship, status, confidence, freedom from worry, inclusion, safety. What the product does matters as much as, or less than, how the reader feels using it and how he is seen using it.
- To find the promise, find the USP: start with the product; what it does and claims must be original; every promise must target a desire the reader already has. You cannot create desire, only awaken it.
- A good promise is big, but a promise too big to believe backfires. Disarm skeptics by acknowledging the reason for their skepticism before they raise it. To size a promise you must know what has been promised before and what the reader expects, so you neither over- nor under-promise.

**Promise leads should:** start with the biggest benefit; hit the targeted promise at once; connect the core benefit to the core desire; sound as new and original as possible; be bold but believable; be followed by even bigger proof; often focus on speed, size or quality of results. They usually fail with skeptics and the unaware, and work very well with fence-sitters.

*Apply:* far more important than the promise's size is how original and relevant it is to this reader. "Photo-verified visits" is a small promise that is original in landscaping; that beats a big generic one.

## 8. The Problem-Solution Lead

"When you advertise fire extinguishers, open with the fire" (Ogilvy, quoted). Once you know what keeps the reader up at night, you have the makings of this lead. It is the classic hot-button approach: delay the product, open by naming the reader's biggest, most emotionally charged, most relevant issue, then follow with the promise.

**The formula:**
1. Target the worries that keep the reader up at night.
2. Make sure they carry deep emotional weight.
3. Stir those emotions first, to prove you feel the pain.
4. Don't linger on the problem too long before offering hope.
5. Offer hope of a relevant solution at some point in the pitch.

**Going negative is right when** the reader is already feeling down: "up" messages aimed at a worried reader can make him feel worse, while acknowledging the problem gives it legitimacy and makes you a credible source. Behind the obvious problem sit deep core emotions: fear, frustration, guilt, shame, self-doubt, inadequacy, embarrassment, regret. The promise that erases those is the real promise.

**Five forms of the problem-solution lead:**
- **"If this, then that."** The commonest form.
- **Inversion.** The negative promise Schwartz loved ("how to do wonders with a little land").
- **Identification.** Written to the person who has the problem and feels it, sometimes as directly responsible for it ("to people who want to write but can't get started").
- **The question.** "Do you make these mistakes in English?" The word "these" works wonders.
- **Instruction.** Invent a name that characterises the problem quickly ("is your home picture-poor?").

All five assume the reader already knows what he'd like to change.

*Apply:* this is the natural lead for problem-aware service buyers: a council whose property looks tired, a manager fielding resident complaints. Name the worry in their words, prove you've seen it, then move to hope within a paragraph.

## 9. The Big Secret Lead

Next to the story lead, the most universal and useful. The lead teases hard-to-come-by knowledge, a formula or a system; the secret can be a solution or a hidden problem; you get the best mileage when buying the product is what reveals it. A good secret lead keeps a reader going for pages before the reveal.

Two techniques: **neologising**, giving a key concept a new name to spark interest; and **transubstantiation**, turning something ordinary into something special. The lead connects to a deep instinct that there are secrets to things.

**Patterns in successful secret leads:**
1. The secret is intriguing and beneficial.
2. It is introduced in the headline.
3. It is not disclosed during the lead.
4. As the letter progresses, more clues are given.

**How to create one:** find a secret already in the product, or take a benefit and rename or reposition it so it seems new. List every quality, characteristic and component of the product and ask which is not well known and whether its benefit is enough to drive the lead. When everything is well known, reinvent by renaming. Tease from the headline, keep dropping clues, and don't disclose until the reader is emotionally ready to buy.

*Apply:* for a service business the "secret" is usually a method the buyer has never seen named: a winter priority map, a month-by-month grounds calendar. Name it, tease what it prevents, reveal the method as the proposal.

## 10. The Proclamation Lead

An emotionally charged statement about what might be true now or in the future: an incredible factoid, a shocking forecast, a bold claim. The goal is to jar the unaware reader out of his seat and disarm him long enough to work back to the product. Well-made proclamation leads read like newspaper stories; the reader briefly forgets he's reading advertising. Indirect leads "borrow" the emotional framework of other forms (news, story, secret) to sell emotionally before the pitch turns direct.

They share traits with story leads: they surprise; the USP is withheld until the proclamation has been proved (the reader wants proof that justifies his curiosity before he'll look for the link to the promise); they must be concise and engaging, usually proved with anecdotes and detailed stories; and specificity is everything, because there is no better shortcut to credibility than specific detail. Most often used to sell consumer products to readers unaware of the product.

**Four rules:**
1. Make the proclamation bold, not reasonable. Being sensible is not the lead's job.
2. Make a promise, explicit or implied; if not in the headline, in the sub-head.
3. The subject must be relevant: unexpected, but about something the reader truly cares about and desires.
4. Return to the proclamation at the close; connect it to the guarantee.

Proclamation leads depend on efficiency more than any other lead, and they come from research: stay alert for remarkable facts as you read, write them down, return to them later.

*Apply:* a true, specific, surprising fact about the reader's world ("Vancouver averages under 40 centimetres of snow a year, and most winter slip claims come from refrozen thaw, not snowfall") is a proclamation lead for an unaware council. It has to be true and it has to connect to the offer at the close.

## 11. The Story Lead

The most indirect lead and, the authors say, the most powerful of the six. It always contains a promise, but far less directly than a promise or offer lead. A well-told story makes the reader forget he is reading an ad; his emotions are invested in the story and his advertising defences are down. Stories work because they convey information in a way that is easy to remember and let the reader experience an event viscerally. Every master copywriter understands the reader's ambivalence: he wants to buy but doesn't want to be sold.

**The Golden Thread:** the product is at one end of the thread, the reader's heart at the other. Every element of the copy must connect to both. If the thread goes slack for a second, the sale is lost.

**The rules of good storytelling produce the greatest effect:** begin in the middle, with a conflict (stated or implicit) affecting a protagonist the reader can identify with, and offer an emotionally satisfying solution. Tell it quickly and keep it in the context of the bigger promise. Testimonials, founder bios, historical proof and track record all yield story leads.

*Apply:* the case study is the service business's story lead. One property, one problem the council recognises, what was done, what changed, with dates. Start in the middle (the November drain that flooded the entrance), not with "we were founded in".

## 12. Making the System Work

- When in doubt, use one of the six. Hybrids come later, once each type is mastered and a single sales message has been isolated.
- Offer and Promise leads tolerate blending with other types; the rest do not.
- Test lead against lead, not just headline against headline. The lead type is the biggest variable in the piece.
- Research habit: keep a file of remarkable facts, worries in the customer's own words, and secrets in the product. The three indirect leads are built from that file.

## 13. Masterson & Forde vs. Schwartz, Sugarman and Bly

- **Schwartz** supplies the awareness ladder this book runs on; Great Leads turns it into a decision procedure (awareness level → directness → lead type). Use Schwartz for *why* and this file for *which opening*.
- **Sugarman** says open to be read, not to sell, with a first sentence under ten words, and sequence the body by objections. Great Leads agrees for indirect leads and disagrees for the two direct leads, where the promise or the deal belongs in the first line. Reconcile them by awareness: Sugarman's opening is an indirect lead for a skeptical reader; for a most-aware reader, lead with the deal.
- **Bly** gives eight headline types and the 4 U's; Great Leads is about the 300 to 600 words after the headline. Bly's "select the audience" function is the Identification form of the problem-solution lead.
- **Kennedy** (`kennedy-ultimate-sales-letter.md`) covers the close and the production process; this book covers the open. Together with Sugarman's middle they make one method.

## 14. Problem → Great Leads Tool

| Problem | Tool |
|---|---|
| Copy tries to say five things in the first paragraph | Rule of One, five elements (§2) |
| Don't know how to open | Ask what the reader already knows (§3), then the table in §5 |
| Page reads as pushy or "salesy" | Reader is less aware than the lead assumes; move down the directness scale (§4) |
| Page reads as vague, no one knows what's for sale | Reader is more aware than the lead assumes, or the indirect lead never connects; move up (§4) |
| Promise feels generic | Find the emotional promise behind the words; check originality and relevance before size (§7) |
| Promise too big to believe | Acknowledge the skepticism first; follow with bigger proof (§7) |
| Reader is worried, not hopeful | Problem-solution lead; go negative briefly, then hope (§8) |
| Product is familiar and commoditised | Secret lead: rename or reposition a benefit (§9) |
| Reader doesn't know a problem exists | Proclamation lead built from a true remarkable fact (§10) |
| Skeptical reader, unbelievable claim | Story lead; begin in the middle; keep the Golden Thread taut (§11) |
| Offer lead underperforming with a warm list | Test price-led vs. risk-led (trial, premium, guarantee) (§6) |

## 15. Applied to Service Copy

For a local service business the six leads map onto real pages and moments:

| Situation | Reader's awareness | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| Renewal or seasonal add-on to a current client | Most aware | Offer |
| Service landing page reached from a search like "strata landscaping Vancouver" | Product/solution aware | Promise, with proof immediately after |
| Blog guide for a council with a tired property | Problem aware | Problem-solution (Identification or Question form) |
| Proposal for a council that has "a contractor already" | Solution aware, skeptical | Story (a case study) or Secret (a named method) |
| Cold letter or article for councils that think grounds are fine | Unaware | Proclamation from a true, specific fact |
| Quote follow-up after a site walk | Product aware | Promise restated in the reader's words from the walk, then the offer |

One lead per piece. If a page needs two, it is two pages.
