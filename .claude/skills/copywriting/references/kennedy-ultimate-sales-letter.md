# Dan Kennedy — The Ultimate Sales Letter, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Dan Kennedy's *The Ultimate Sales Letter*, built from the user's own copy. This is a currently-copyrighted book — paraphrase is mandatory throughout: no verbatim reproduction of his checklists or examples, direct quotes under 15 words and rare. Unlike Sugarman's psychological-trigger approach or Whitman's enumerated-checklist approach already in this skill, Kennedy's contribution is a linear, 28-step PROCESS for actually producing a sales letter from a blank page to the mailbox — use this when the task is "how do I actually get this written and out the door," not just "what makes copy persuasive."

Use this alongside `sugarman-handbook.md` (his 7-step process is the compressed version of the same idea) and `legends-library.md` Volume 8 (the short summary of Kennedy's overall philosophy — this file is the full treatment of one specific book).

## 1. Before You Write a Word

### Anyone can write the letter — you have an unfair advantage the pro doesn't

Kennedy opens by knocking down the idea that sales-letter writing requires a special gift. His argument rests on a simple asymmetry: you already know your business, your product, and your customer better than any hired copywriter ever will. A freelancer brought in to write your letter has to spend real time and effort just catching up to the understanding you start with — getting that understanding is the hard part; putting it into sentences is comparatively easy once you have it.

He backs this with his own story: a high-school graduate with no agency background, no mailroom apprenticeship, who took his first paid copywriting job as a teenager and was running his own agency two years later. He describes later beating a highly credentialed, highly paid "name" copywriter in a head-to-head control test for a client — offered not to boast, he says, but as proof that self-taught, non-credentialed writers can win. He borrows a line from Erasmus, via another copywriter, to make the point that in a field without much sophistication, modest skill goes a long way — most of the letters an ordinary business person writes will compete against amateur mail, not against Madison Avenue's best.

**Apply:** Before worrying about "writing ability," inventory what you know that a generic copywriter wouldn't — the objections you've heard a hundred times, the exact words prospects use, the real reasons people buy from you versus a competitor. That knowledge is the raw material; sentence-craft is secondary.

### The swipe file — build one, and mine it deliberately

The single most concrete habit Kennedy hands you here is the swipe file: a standing collection of other people's ads, mailings, and letters, organized and kept on hand specifically so you can borrow structures, headline patterns, phrases, and offer mechanics from proven work rather than inventing from a blank page every time. He's explicit that this isn't shameful shortcutting — in his framing, real skill in this field is recombining existing ideas well, not manufacturing novelty from nothing. He built his own file this way from the start of his career and still leans on it.

Practically: whenever a piece of mail, an ad, or an email lands with an effective headline, a clever offer structure, or a strong opening, it goes in the file — organized so you can find "headline patterns" or "guarantee language" or "urgency devices" later when you're stuck. When it's time to write, you start by flipping through the file for a structure close to your situation, not by staring at a blank page hoping for originality.

**Apply:** Start (or audit) a swipe file organized by function — headlines, openers, offers, guarantees, closes, P.S. lines — not by industry. When drafting, pull a few analogous examples before writing your first sentence. (See also `modern-swipe-file-2026.md` in this skill for a contemporary version of the same practice.)

### Practical starting advice: think "selling," write in blocks, kill perfectionism

Kennedy's pre-writing advice is unglamorous but load-bearing:

- **Adopt a selling mentality first.** He describes a sales letter as a sales presentation on paper — closer to demonstrating a vacuum cleaner in someone's living room than to "writing" in any literary sense. If you don't already think like a salesperson, get that grounding before worrying about prose style.
- **Externalize what you know.** He suggests keeping running lists or index cards of what you know about your customers and your product, so that knowledge doesn't have to be trusted to memory mid-draft.
- **Write in blocks, not start-to-finish.** Draft chunks — an opening, a benefit section, a guarantee, a close — independently, then assemble them (he mentions letters historically built with literal scissors and tape, or its word-processor equivalent). This removes the pressure of needing the whole thing to flow before any of it exists.
- **Avoid perfectionism entirely.** Most businesses, for most purposes, don't need a perfect letter — they need a working one. A letter built on solid guidelines, a genuinely worthwhile offer, and real customer understanding doesn't have to be flawless to produce results.

**Apply:** If a draft stalls, stop trying to write it in order. Write the guarantee paragraph, the offer paragraph, and the close as separate stand-alone blocks, then stitch them together — momentum on any one block beats a blank page waiting for the whole letter to arrive at once.

## 2. Steps 1-6: Understanding the Customer, the Offer, and Getting the Letter Opened

Kennedy frames the first third of his 28-step system as diagnostic work that happens before a single sentence of the letter gets written. Steps 1-3 are about knowing your reader and your offer well enough to speak to them honestly; Steps 4-6 are about the physical and psychological gauntlet a letter has to survive — the mailstream, the wastebasket, and the reader's attention span — before your carefully diagnosed message ever gets absorbed.

### Step 1: Get "Into" the Customer

Kennedy's starting premise is that persuasion is impossible without genuine understanding of the person you're persuading — you have to inhabit their situation, not guess at it from a distance. He illustrates the failure mode with a story from his own copywriting work: he wrote a home-mortgage infomercial script describing "a typical American home," and the production crew shot it in a white-carpeted living room built around a grand piano — a set that had nothing to do with the actual target buyer. The lesson he draws is that even professional marketers routinely lose touch with the people they're supposedly writing to, and that the closer you stay to your audience's real circumstances, the better your results.

To close that gap deliberately, Kennedy offers what he calls his **"10 Smart Market Diagnosis and Profiling Questions,"** originally taught in his paid copywriting seminars. Paraphrased, they ask you to work out: (1) what worries keep your prospect up at night; (2) what they're afraid of; (3) what makes them angry, and at whom; (4) their three biggest recurring day-to-day frustrations; (5) what trends are already reshaping their business or life; (6) what they secretly want most but rarely say out loud; (7) whether their decision-making has a built-in bias (he gives engineers, who default to hyper-analytical thinking, as an example); (8) whether they use their own specialized vocabulary; (9) who else is already marketing something similar to them, and how; and (10) who has already tried to sell them something similar and failed, and why that attempt fell flat.

Kennedy pushes past demographic data (age, income, hobbies, even what magazines someone reads) toward something closer to felt experience. When a marketer has nothing but a zip code to go on, his advice is almost literal: drive through that neighborhood repeatedly, at different times, until you develop a feel for the people living there — or, for a business audience, sit in on their meetings and read their trade publications. He describes applying this to a real assignment writing to realtors, a field he knew nothing about: he read the trade magazines and books realtors read, and when a major real-estate franchise held its convention in his city that week, he spent time in the hotel lobby and bar eavesdropping on their conversations until he could picture himself as one of them.

He also borrows a visualization discipline from Dr. Maxwell Maltz's *Psycho-Cybernetics* (cited via optometrist and performance researcher Dr. Bill Harrison, who used similar techniques to help elite athletes). Kennedy applies it to copywriting by mentally rehearsing his reader's entire day — how their mail arrives, when and where they open it, what's competing for their attention at that moment — so that when he writes, he's addressing an imagined but specific human being rather than an abstraction.

The payoff of this work is prioritizing the reader's actual concerns over your own. Kennedy tells the story of a heating-system salesman who exhaustively covers BTUs and warranties for an elderly customer, only to have her ask the one question he never addressed: will it actually keep her warm. He saw the same pattern in computer retail, where salespeople talk about what matters to them rather than pausing to find out what matters to the buyer. Writing this into practice, he was hired to write a corporate fundraising letter for a local Arthritis Foundation telethon. Other nonprofits' letters to the same donor pool all focused on the charity's own needs; Kennedy instead put himself in the donor's chair and worked out that a business owner asked to give would really want to know what benefit justified the cost, who else had already committed, and where the money would come from in their budget. The letter built around those donor priorities pulled a response rate of only half a percent, but among the very few who responded was a $13,000 donor whose gift alone covered roughly half that year's local telethon costs — a result the charity's own veteran fundraisers hadn't expected, because the letter addressed the reader's interests instead of the sender's.

**Apply:** Before writing anything, answer Kennedy's 10 questions in the customer's own voice, not yours — and if you don't know the answers, go find out (read what they read, sit where they sit) rather than guessing from a demographic sheet.

### Step 2: Get "Into" the Offer

Kennedy applies the same immersion instinct to the product, service, or offer itself. If it's a physical product, use it the way a customer would, take it apart, demonstrate it to someone else. If it's a service, use it yourself or talk at length to people who do (and to people using a competitor's version). If it's a special offer or promotion, test it on real people to see whether they understand it and find it appealing before you build a letter around it.

From that immersion, he has you build an exhaustive list of every feature and benefit you can identify — he personally does this on index cards so he can physically shuffle and reorder them by importance rather than being locked into a single running list. The recurring trap he warns against is describing features (what the thing is) instead of benefits (what it does for the reader) — he distills this to the idea that people buy outcomes, not specifications.

Beyond the obvious benefit, Kennedy credits fellow copywriter Ted Nicholas with the concept of the **"hidden benefit"** — a real but non-obvious benefit that matters enormously to the customer even though it's not the first thing either the marketer or the customer would name. He illustrates this with an insurance-industry seminar: he and a colleague noticed that the agents attending, despite having paid heavily to be there for career-critical training, spent every break talking about their golf plans. That observation became the seed of one of that colleague's most successful trade-journal ads, whose headline promised that a new recruiting system would let agents automate the tedious parts of hiring so they could get back to the golf course — selling the recruiting system by leading with the hidden benefit (reclaimed personal time) rather than the obvious one (better recruiting results).

**Apply:** List every feature and benefit of your offer on separate cards or lines, sort by importance, and then ask specifically what non-obvious benefit your reader would privately value even more than the one you'd naturally lead with.

### Step 3: Create a Damaging Admission and Address Flaws Openly

Kennedy treats naming your own product's or offer's shortcomings as a credibility-building move rather than a liability. By raising the objections a skeptical reader would otherwise silently hold against you, you get to answer them on your own terms — and readers register the willingness to admit a flaw as evidence that everything else you're saying is trustworthy too. He calls this general technique "damaging admission copy."

His method for finding what to admit is the same index-card brainstorming he used for Step 1's objections: list every reason, excuse, doubt, and fear that could stop someone from responding. He gives a concrete diagnostic example — a doctor who had received a dozen letters for a competing offer, had the money and apparent interest to buy, but never responded, because the offer struck him as too good to be true and that suspicion colored everything else in the letter. Had the marketer anticipated and addressed that specific doubt, Kennedy argues, the response rate would likely have been higher.

He also describes (with some editorializing about the business itself) a client in the adult telephone-service industry who tripled response simply by adding one line addressing an unstated but obvious objection: the buyer's fear that the charge would show up identifiably on a credit card statement seen by a spouse or employer. Naming and resolving that hidden worry directly, rather than hoping it wouldn't occur to anyone, is what moved the number.

For a gentler example, Kennedy cites a small Italian restaurant's promotional letter to its neighborhood, which openly told readers not to expect white tablecloths, an intimidating wine list, or upscale trappings — and then reframed that same absence as the charm of an authentic, unpretentious, home-style alternative. The restaurant converted what could have been read as a weakness into a distinctive, likable personality.

**Apply:** Brainstorm every objection a skeptical reader would hold in reserve, pick the one or two most damaging, and address them openly in the letter rather than hoping they go unnoticed — silence on an obvious flaw reads as evasion, not confidence.

### Step 4: Get Your Sales Letter Delivered

Before worrying about the letter's persuasiveness, Kennedy insists you have to solve a much cruder problem: making sure it physically arrives. He cites postal-industry audits and reporting from the era indicating that a meaningful share of properly addressed bulk mail — his sourced estimates ranged roughly from 10 to 30 percent — never reached its recipient at all, and argues the true number was likely worse, driven not just by processing damage but by postal employees discarding what they judged to be "junk mail." He backs this with several documented incidents: a Minneapolis postal carrier convicted for dumping bundles of undelivered mail because he was tired of carrying it; a Florida postal employee who dumped roughly half a million pieces on his own property; and industry reporting describing widespread bulk-mail dumping found at post offices and bulk-mail centers nationally, with the share of audited sites showing large-scale improper disposal rising year over year.

His practical countermeasure is to make a piece of mail look like something a postal worker would believe is expected correspondence rather than disposable advertising, since (in his account) that belief is what determines whether it gets delivered at all. Concretely, he recommends: preferring first-class postage over bulk when the economics allow it; using genuine affixed stamps rather than metered postage, since a live stamp implies a small, deliberate mailing rather than a mass blast; hand-addressing or ink-jet-addressing envelopes instead of using an obvious adhesive label; and choosing between two opposite envelope strategies depending on the audience — either a plain, unbranded "sneak-up" envelope with no return address or teaser copy that reads as ordinary personal correspondence, or the opposite extreme of an elaborate, official-looking, almost intimidating design that projects institutional importance. He illustrates the aggressive end of that spectrum with his own early experiment mailing a homemade look-alike overnight-courier package via cheap bulk mail; postal staff initially refused to process it, but once forced to, some pieces were mistakenly handled and delivered as genuine express mail in the middle of the night, and the mailing achieved an unusually high response rate because it reliably got delivered and opened.

**Apply:** Choose the envelope strategy and postage class before you finalize the letter's tone, since the two have to match — a plain personal-looking envelope demands a personal-sounding letter inside it, and an official-looking envelope demands the letter live up to that implied importance.

### Step 5: Get Your Sales Letter Looked At

Once delivered, a letter still has to survive being opened. Kennedy's touchstone here is an image he credits to direct-marketing figure Gary Halbert: picture your recipient sorting a stack of mail while standing directly over a wastebasket, deciding envelope by envelope what lives and what dies before anything inside is ever read. He treats that mental picture as one of the most valuable single ideas in the book, because it forces you to design the envelope itself as a survival mechanism, not an afterthought.

He notes that business mail is often filtered first by an assistant, and that consumer mail is typically sorted by whoever in the household handles it, usually under real time pressure — meaning a letter has to clear at least one, sometimes two, snap judgments before a human being with buying authority ever unfolds it. He offers a striking data point from his own experience mailing to doctors: switching from a company-branded envelope to a plain one using an individual doctor's own name and return address (bypassing office staff, since it reads as personal correspondence to the recipient) lifted response by as much as 300 percent in some tests.

Whatever promise or device gets an envelope opened has to be honestly fulfilled by what's inside, or the reader's trust collapses immediately. He gives the example of a "lift letter" — a small separate note, made to look handwritten or personal, that explains why the enclosed material is worth reading — used specifically to deliver on the personal-envelope premise once it's opened.

**Apply:** Design the envelope test first — assume it's being judged over a wastebasket in under two seconds — and make sure whatever claim or personal touch gets it opened is immediately and honestly confirmed by the first thing the reader sees inside.

### Step 6: Get Your Sales Letter Read

Kennedy invokes the classic person-to-person sales sequence — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action (AIDA) — as the order of operations once a letter is actually open in front of someone: you still have to earn interest before you can build desire. He frames the reader's default posture toward an unsolicited letter as hostility, comparing it to an uninvited visitor pounding on your door — annoying by default, and only re-classified as welcome once they say something the recipient immediately recognizes as urgent and valuable to them personally (his own illustration is a stranger who turned out to be warning him his yard was on fire). A sales letter earns the same reclassification only if its opening line clears that same bar of obvious, immediate relevance.

His primary tool for doing that is the headline — a device he insists belongs in a well-built sales letter even though ordinary correspondence doesn't use one. It can sit above the salutation, between the salutation and body copy, or inside a boxed banner (a format he calls a "Johnson box"), typically set apart visually from the rest of the letter's more personal, typewritten look.

Rather than trying to teach headline-writing from first principles, Kennedy offers a set of reusable fill-in-the-blank headline structures that have proven durable across many campaigns. Paraphrased, they include: a "they doubted me but I succeeded anyway" structure that taps our instinct to root for an underdog; a "who else wants ___" structure that implies an in-group already possesses the benefit; a "how ___ made me ___" first-person transformation story; a challenging or curiosity-provoking direct question ("are you ___?"); a first-person "how I ___" achievement story; a straightforward "how to ___" benefit promise, optionally preceded by a flag naming the specific audience it's for ("for busy doctors," etc.); a "if you are ___, you can ___" structure that personalizes the flag technique further; a "secrets of ___" structure that trades on curiosity about privileged information; a "thousands now ___, even though they ___" structure that pluralizes the underdog appeal across many people at once; a "warning: ___" structure that borrows the innate urgency of a caution label; a direct "give me ___ and I'll ___" structure that states the whole offer as a simple trade; and a "___ ways to ___" structure that adds a specific, credibility-boosting number to a how-to promise.

He closes the section with situational advice: mailings to executives and business owners benefit from higher-quality paper, restrained enclosures (bundling secondary material into its own sealed insert rather than stuffing the main envelope), and vocabulary that signals exclusivity and prestige; mass-market consumer mail needs to work harder and faster given short attention spans, benefiting from color, photography, and small interactive elements (stamps to peel, tokens, stickers) that physically involve the reader; direct product-selling letters lean hardest on testimonials, real photographs over illustrations, and visible proof the product is easy to use; and professional-services letters lean on credibility markers (years in business, client counts) and ways of making an intangible benefit feel concrete, such as before/after evidence or case histories.

**Apply:** Write the headline as if it's the one sentence you get to say before the reader decides whether you're worth their next thirty seconds — pick one of Kennedy's proven structures rather than inventing a novel format under that kind of pressure, and match the letter's physical presentation (paper, color, enclosures) to the specific audience segment you're writing to.

## 3. Steps 7-13: Price, Technique Review, Drafting, and Rewriting for Strategy and Style

This middle stretch of the system is where diagnosis turns into copy. Step 7 is a dedicated chapter on defusing price objections before they form. Step 8 is Kennedy's own personal shortlist, boiled down from hundreds of techniques he tried over a career, of the handful that reliably work. Steps 9-11 are his drafting-and-editing method — write fast and loose, then make two distinct editing passes that ask different questions. The step that follows (its printed header is garbled in scanned copies of the book, but its content is unmistakably about handling reader objections head-on) teaches you to argue both sides of the sale on paper. Step 13 closes the arc by explaining why a technically persuasive letter can still fail if it doesn't manufacture urgency to act today rather than "later."

### Step 7: Beat the Price Bugaboo

Kennedy opens from a salesperson's-eye view: price is almost never the real reason a sale is lost, but handled carelessly it can stall a prospect before anything else in the letter gets a chance to work. His response is not to minimize price through discounting but to control the *frame* the reader uses to judge it. He offers several distinct reframing techniques, meant to be mixed as needed rather than used as a single formula.

**Compare apples to oranges.** Rather than let the reader benchmark your price against direct competitors on their terms, hand them a different, more flattering comparison. Kennedy's own example comes from a mail-order company selling audiocassette courses to dentists and chiropractors at a noticeably higher per-tape price than competitors' six-cassette albums. Instead of competing on cost-per-cassette, his copy compared the price of the tapes to the cost of attending the live seminar the material was drawn from — travel, lodging, and time away from the practice included — so the tapes looked like a bargain against a seminar rather than an overpriced tape set against cheaper tape sets. The general move: find *any* legitimate comparison point that isn't the obvious head-to-head one, and structure the price discussion around that instead.

**Sell bulk.** People instinctively read volume as value, independent of whether the volume is functionally useful. Kennedy traces this to one of the earliest book-offer sales letters, which sold a "Five-Foot Shelf" of classics by leaning on its sheer physical size, and gives his own borrowed version — describing a set of books by their shipping weight and cost to freight rather than by their content. For an information product, the bulk technique becomes a very long itemized list of everything included (he cites publishers like Boardroom Reports and Rodale as habitual users of this approach); for a physical product with no obvious "bulk" angle, his suggested workaround is to enumerate every underlying component of the benefit — his example is reframing a plain apple by listing every vitamin and mineral it contains and every health benefit each one delivers, turning "an apple a day" into an itemized case for nutritional bulk.

**Discuss the price paid to develop the offer.** This is a value-through-backstory technique: even if the cost of *creating* something has no logical bearing on what it's worth to the buyer, describing that development cost in detail makes the reader feel they're getting more. Kennedy illustrates this with two versions of copy for the same industrial packaging machine — a plain functional description versus a version that details the team of engineers who built it, months of lab testing, and a specific dollar figure spent on R&D before the machine ever reached a customer. Both describe an identical product and identical benefits; the version that dramatizes what it cost to build reads as more valuable.

**Make the parts worth more than the whole.** Borrowed explicitly from carnival and county-fair "pitchmen" who build enormous perceived value into each small attachment of a kitchen gadget until the sum of the parts dwarfs the bundle's actual price, this technique itemizes every component of an offer with its own individually-priced value, then totals them against the much lower bundled price. Kennedy's worked example is a medical-practice coaching program: seminars, a marketing kit, a monthly tape club, and several bonuses are each assigned a specific dollar value, summed to several thousand dollars of "hard value," and then contrasted against a bundled fee that's a fraction of that total — with time-limited bonuses stacked on top to add even more perceived overage.

**Conceal the price.** Presenting a price as small recurring installments rather than one total figure — "three small monthly installments of $X" — is a device Kennedy notes was already standard in car dealership advertising (grouping inventory into "$99 a month," "$149 a month" tiers) well before direct-response marketers adopted it broadly. The technique doesn't hide that a price exists; it changes the unit the reader mentally computes against.

**Three letter formulas that transcend the price question.** Beyond individual reframing lines, Kennedy offers three reusable macro-structures whose real function is to keep the reader's attention on something other than the dollar figure entirely:

- *Problem-Agitation-Solution.* Grounded in the observation that people act more readily to escape pain than to pursue gain, this formula states the reader's problem plainly (using only as much proof as needed to win agreement), then deliberately intensifies the emotional charge around that problem — tapping fear, anger, embarrassment, or guilt until the reader is genuinely uncomfortable — before finally introducing the product as the relieving solution. Kennedy credits sales trainer Cavett Robert with the vivid instruction that selling life insurance requires making the customer mentally see the hearse at the door. His paraphrased illustrations include a shoe-company letter that walks an aging reader through a future of foot pain and mobility loss before offering better shoes, and a retail-theft-prevention letter that opens with a supermarket owner's shock at discovering his delivery driver had bought a house as expensive as his own — funded, it turns out, by years of stolen goods — before the agitation escalates into a direct challenge to the reader's own blindness to employee theft.
- *Fortune Telling.* Capitalizing on the durable public fascination with predictions, this formula frames the product as insight into what's coming next for the reader's industry or life, and structures the letter around specific, often dramatic forecasts. Kennedy points to authors John Naisbitt and Doug Casey, who each built entire public careers by publishing predictive material, and notes that this same device was widely recycled across totally unrelated product categories (from home generators to gold coins) in the run-up to the Y2K date change — his broader point being that a durable structural formula like this one can be reused across decades with only cosmetic updates.
- *Winners and Losers.* This formula opens with a stark statistical split of a population into a small successful minority and a much larger unsuccessful majority (Kennedy's stock version cites Social Security-era data on retirement outcomes: roughly 1 in 100 ending up wealthy against the large majority ending up dependent or worse), then positions the product as the specific difference-maker that moves the reader from the losing group into the winning one. He notes The Wall Street Journal built a famous long-running letter around a narrower version of the same structure — two same-starting-point college graduates whose only later difference is that one reads the paper — and gives his own early example for a lawn-care business built around two otherwise-identical neighbors whose lawns diverge based on which one used professional lawn treatment.

**Apply:** Before drafting price copy, pick a deliberate reframe rather than defaulting to a plain number — a comparison point that isn't the obvious competitor, an itemized bulk or parts-value breakdown, a backstory about what the offer cost to create, or a payment-term restructure — and consider building the whole letter around Problem-Agitation-Solution, Fortune Telling, or Winners-and-Losers so the price question never gets asked in isolation.

### Step 8: Review Winning Copywriting Techniques and Tactics

Kennedy frames this chapter with a story about a Depression-era door-to-door vacuum salesman handed a binder of 299 memorized sales techniques, who after thirty days in the field had winnowed it down to the three or four that actually closed sales. He treats his own career the same way: after testing hundreds of techniques pulled from a large personal library of marketing and copywriting books, this chapter is the short list he still relies on.

Several of these overlap substantially with material already covered elsewhere in this skill, so they're summarized briefly here with a pointer rather than re-explained in full:

- **Intimidation / "taking a position."** Kennedy's umbrella term for a set of scarcity- and exclusivity-based copy moves, credited loosely to the psychology in Robert Ringer's *Winning Through Intimidation* — the idea that a seller who visibly doesn't need the sale has more leverage than one who visibly does. Under this heading he lists several specific variants: **limited number available** (hard scarcity — respond after the stated cutoff and the order is void); **most will buy** (a bandwagon-effect line implying a groundswell of other buyers already moving); **you will buy only if...** (challenging the reader's ego by implying the offer suits only a discerning few); **you can buy only if...** (gating purchase behind a qualifying step, like a multi-hour information session or an application); **only some can qualify** (an approval-based exclusivity play, his example being American Express's Gold Card positioning); and **strong credibility copy** (overwhelming a reader's latent doubt with a dense, specific credibility statement — a track record stated in hard numbers). The bandwagon and exclusivity variants here map directly onto the Cialdini-derived scarcity and social-proof material already covered in `whitman-cashvertising.md` §2 (the CLARCCS section) and `legends-library.md` Volume 15 — Kennedy's contribution is less the underlying psychology than a set of ready-to-drop-in phrasing patterns for each variant.
- **Demonstrate ROI.** Kennedy frames a strong ROI argument as effectively "selling money at a discount" — his image is offering real $1,000 bills for $50 each, which anyone would rush to buy once convinced the bills were genuine. Applied to copy, this means stating the payoff (income gained or costs saved) as an explicit multiple of the price, and optionally softening an aggressive ROI claim by conceding it might only be half right and still leaving the reader ahead — a hedge he says reads as more credible than an unqualified claim.
- **Ego appeals.** Positioning a purchase as a status marker rather than (or in addition to) a functional one. His illustration is a fax-machine sales letter built entirely around the reader's fear of being seen as behind the times by clients and peers rather than around the machine's functional convenience. This is close in substance to Whitman's "Ego Morphing" principle in `whitman-cashvertising.md` §2 — Kennedy's framing emphasizes anxiety about falling behind a peer group, where Whitman's emphasizes aspirational identity-borrowing, so the two are complementary angles on the same lever rather than duplicates.
- **Strong guarantee.** Kennedy argues that rising consumer skepticism (which he ties to a string of public trust failures across politics, religion, and sports through the decades) calls for *stronger* guarantees, not weaker ones, contrary to some commentators' claims that guarantees were losing relevance. He lists several ways to present one: the plain money-back guarantee, ideally worded with warmer language than "satisfied"; refund-plus-keep-the-premium (the reader keeps a bonus gift even after refunding the core purchase); redundancy (stating the same refund promise two or three times in different words, for emphasis); the free-trial framing (identical mechanics to a money-back guarantee, presented as risk-free trial instead); and, for a especially strong offer, making the guarantee itself the letter's lead rather than a supporting element. His most aggressive variant is guaranteeing the *letter* — paying the reader a fixed sum (or an equivalent charitable donation) if they feel their time was wasted after reading it — which he reports using himself with strong measured results (a small, bounded downside payout against a much larger revenue lift) and which he's confirmed through split-testing increases both readership and response. This whole technique overlaps directly with Secret #33 in `whitman-cashvertising.md` §4, including the same underlying logic that a stronger guarantee signals seller confidence; Whitman's chapter adds the research point that longer guarantee windows counterintuitively produce fewer actual returns than short ones, which is worth cross-referencing before finalizing guarantee wording.

**Apply:** Treat this as a checklist to run against a draft, not a menu to use all at once — pick one intimidation variant, one ROI or ego angle if relevant to the offer, and the strongest guarantee you can actually stand behind, rather than layering all of them into a single letter.

### Step 9: Write the First Draft

Kennedy treats this step as deliberately unglamorous: once the diagnostic and technique-selection work from the earlier steps is done, the instruction is simply to write, without editing as you go and without worrying about length or grammar. He reports that his own first drafts routinely run two to three times longer than the letter that eventually ships, and considers this the most efficient method he's seen among copywriters, precisely because it separates the generative work (getting every possible persuasive idea onto the page) from the judgment work (deciding what survives), rather than trying to do both simultaneously.

**Apply:** Write the full first draft in one pass without self-editing, expecting it to run long — the cutting and shaping is explicitly deferred to the next two steps, not this one.

### Steps 10-11: Two Distinct Editing Passes — Strategy, Then Style

Kennedy's rewriting method is built on a genuine two-pass distinction, and the difference between the passes is worth holding onto: a **strategy** rewrite asks whether the letter is built the right way — its length, its structure, its pacing, and how it's engineered to move different kinds of readers through it — while a **style** rewrite, covered separately in Step 11, asks whether each sentence and paragraph reads well once the structure is already right. Doing them in this order matters: restructuring after polishing sentence-level style wastes the polish.

#### Step 10: Rewrite for Strategy

Kennedy opens by confronting the standard objection to long sales copy — "who's going to read all that?" — with a direct answer: the people who are actually going to buy. He cites Ogilvy & Mather research indicating that readership drops steeply in the first fifty words of body copy but then drops off much more gradually between fifty and five hundred words, and uses this to argue that shortening a letter to a length a *disinterested* reader would tolerate is a wasted effort, since that reader was never going to respond regardless of length. His operating principle is to write for the buyer, not the non-buyer, and to include everything a genuinely interested prospect needs to decide — even if that requires many pages — rather than trimming to an arbitrary target length. He reports campaigns where adding pages to an already-working letter measurably increased response further, contrary to the intuition that shorter always converts better.

From that premise, Step 10 offers several concrete structural moves:

- **Write and rewrite without a page-count restraint.** Kennedy compares arbitrary length limits to a newspaper editor cutting a story to fit a column inch, and argues a sales letter should never be trimmed to fit a predetermined length — it should be exactly as long as it needs to be to make the sale, the way an in-person sales presentation would run as long as it takes.
- **The Double Readership Path.** Because readers split roughly into impulsive and analytical temperaments, Kennedy recommends structuring one letter to serve both simultaneously. The impulsive reader skims via bold subheads, captioned images, and highlighted call-out lines — an "impulsive path" that alone carries enough of the offer to prompt a response — while the analytical reader (who often starts by skimming the same path) is drawn in to read every word of the full surrounding copy for the facts and detail they require to feel informed. Building both paths into a single letter means the same document can convert readers of either temperament.
- **Internal repetition ("tell 'em, tell 'em again, tell 'em a little differently").** Because Kennedy argues that people accept new ideas as true mainly through either repetition or shock, and a sales letter can't ethically manufacture shock, he recommends restating the same core promise multiple times through different vehicles within one letter — a straightforward statement, a worked example, a narrative "slice of life," a testimonial, a quoted endorsement, and a numbered summary — so the same claim lands on the reader from several angles without reading as literally repetitive.
- **The "yes sequence."** Borrowed from a hypnotherapist's description of building agreement momentum, this move structures early questions or statements so the reader can easily say "yes" to each one, establishing a habit of agreement before the letter asks for the harder yes of a purchase decision. Kennedy suggests implementing it through question-based subheads or paragraph openers that the reader will naturally agree with.
- **Teaser copy at page breaks.** A formatting rule paired with a copy technique: never end a printed page on a completed sentence, since that gives the reader a natural stopping point; instead, break mid-sentence or mid-thought, and optionally add a short "teaser" line at the bottom of the page previewing what's coming next. Kennedy notes a teaser line is functionally just a headline for the next page and should be written using the same headline principles from Step 6.

**Apply:** Before touching individual sentences, check the letter's bones — is it as long as the offer actually requires (not as long as an arbitrary format allows), does it serve both a skimming and a deep reader, does the core promise recur in several different forms, does the early copy build a yes habit, and does every page break tease the reader into the next one?

#### Step 11: Rewrite for Style

Once the structure is right, the style pass asks whether the writing itself reads easily and persuasively at the sentence level. Kennedy's core standard is what he calls being "reader friendly" — plain, conversational, easy on the eye — and he offers several concrete techniques to get there:

- **Readability mechanics.** Short, punchy sentences (including deliberate sentence fragments), and short paragraphs of only three or four sentences, are what he says keep a reader moving without friction.
- **Use the first paragraph as an extended headline.** Kennedy treats the opening paragraph as having one job only — earning permission to read the second paragraph — rather than trying to make a sales argument immediately. He demonstrates this with a real letter opening that buried its strongest point (a bullish silver-market signal) under throat-clearing about internal memo protocol, and his rewritten version, which leads immediately with the strongest claim and compresses the throat-clearing to a single attributed line.
- **Be entertaining, not funny.** Outright humor, in his assessment, is difficult to execute well and risky when it fails, but a letter can and should avoid being flatly dry. He contrasts a lighthearted, self-deprecating opening about frequent business travel against a dead-serious rewrite of the identical offer, and argues the livelier version is simply more pleasant to keep reading — his point being that the enemy of good copy is boredom, not length.
- **Appeal to the senses.** Because the subconscious mind processes more than pure visual/logical information, Kennedy recommends writing passages that let the reader feel temperature, sound, or physical sensation, not just see a description — his example is describing the discomfort of being the last person working alone in a cold, dark office as a way into selling a faster computer.
- **Use "big impact" words and phrases.** Kennedy keeps a running personal file of vivid, charged phrases pulled from novels, television, and advertising to drop into otherwise plain copy for bursts of color, citing a passage from crime novelist Raymond Chandler as the kind of writing he mines for inspiration (without needing to write at that literary level throughout an entire letter — just borrowing isolated charged phrases).
- **Let your own personal style show.** Kennedy admits this is the one technique he can't reduce to a formula — some letters read as coming from a real individual and others don't, and he says he can usually identify a given writer's letters in his own mail purely by that quality. His instruction is simply to sell in print the way you would sell in person, rather than adopting an impersonal "corporate" voice.

**Apply:** Read the strategically-sound draft aloud and cut anything that isn't short, plain, and conversational; make sure the first paragraph alone would earn a stranger's next thirty seconds; and inject at least one sensory passage and a few charged phrases rather than settling for flatly correct prose.

### Answering Questions and Objections (the step between "Rewrite for Style" and "Spark Immediate Action")

This step (its header didn't survive OCR cleanly, but its content sits squarely between Steps 11 and 13) tackles the objections a reader is silently forming while reading, on the theory that an unaddressed doubt quietly kills a response even when everything else about the letter is sound.

Kennedy contrasts this with live selling, where a salesperson only has to answer the specific objections a given customer actually raises, gets immediate feedback on whether an answer landed, and can use sequential-agreement tactics (he describes "draining the objections" — listing every objection a prospect can think of before answering any of them, then using their agreement that a fully-answered list would justify buying to lock in the sale) to convert a skeptic. A sales letter has none of these advantages: it has to preemptively answer *every* plausible objection for *every* reader, with no feedback loop telling it when it's said enough.

Kennedy's own position, argued against clients and agency colleagues who worried that raising objections plants the idea in a reader's head, is that skepticism should never be underestimated — if a doubt is going to occur to a reader at all, hiding it doesn't prevent the doubt, it only prevents the answer. His preferred structural device is a copy block that names the fact that only a small percentage of recipients will respond, frames that as personally troubling to the writer given how much genuine benefit the product delivers, and then introduces a short, specific list of the exact reasons someone might say no — each one then answered directly. He describes a lower-effort alternative used more commonly in the industry: a straightforward "Frequently Asked Questions" page appended to the letter, with anticipated objections rephrased as questions.

Whichever format is used, Kennedy specifies that a strong objection-answer should generally combine three elements: a direct answer to the stated concern, a verifying testimonial, case history, or story that proves the answer is true in practice (not just asserted), and a restatement of or reference back to the offer's guarantee or free-trial terms, so every answered objection also re-anchors the reader in the fact that the risk is already covered.

**Apply:** Before finalizing a letter, list every objection a skeptical reader would silently hold (not just the flattering ones you'd want to admit), and answer the real ones directly, each with a stated answer, a piece of proof, and a pointer back to your guarantee — either woven into the letter as its own set-up block or appended as an FAQ.

### Step 13: Spark Immediate Action

Kennedy closes this stretch of the system by pointing out that even a letter that survives delivery, gets opened, gets read, and successfully answers every objection can still fail commercially if it doesn't convert that agreement into action *now*. He argues the largest group of non-responders isn't people who were unpersuaded — it's people who were persuaded, intended to respond "later," and then never did. Nearly all of a letter's real revenue, in his account, comes in close to immediately after mailing; a slow "trickle" of later responses exists but is commercially insignificant by comparison.

He frames the goal with a mentor's vivid test case: imagine the letter being read by someone in the middle of a brutal winter storm, and ask whether the letter is compelling enough to get that person out of a warm chair and out into the snow to mail a response rather than waiting until the storm passes. Modern response channels (toll-free numbers, credit cards) lower the physical bar, but the psychological bar — beating the temptation to set the letter aside as a "conditional yes" — is the same.

Kennedy lists seven specific urgency-generating devices:

1. **Limited availability** — a real, honestly-stated cap on quantity of the core product, a premium, or a discount, which creates competitive pressure to act before the supply runs out.
2. **Premiums** — Kennedy considers a well-chosen bonus gift, offered specifically for a fast response, more reliably effective at driving immediate action than a plain discount, reporting response lifts as large as 50% from adding the right premium. He walks through a Las Vegas casino-resort mailing (from Bob Stupak's Vegas World) that combined a strict, dated limited-availability claim (a fixed number of paid-for vacation packages) with a strong premium (a "free" companion vacation) mailed only to a stated fraction of past guests, and reports it converted him personally as a reader despite having no intention to buy that day — a result he attributes to four factors working together: trust in the company, genuine liking of the core product, belief in the stated scarcity, and desire for the specific premium offered.
3. **Deadlines** — the most basic urgency device, usable alone or stacked with any of the others. Kennedy cites an insurance agent whose cold-mail response rate roughly tripled after simply adding a rubber-stamped response deadline to an existing "free road atlas" offer that had previously carried no time limit.
4. **Multiple premiums** — once a single premium is shown to lift response, Kennedy recommends testing a second, larger premium tied to a bigger order, citing a cleaning-chemicals company where adding a second, higher-tier premium option didn't change the overall response rate but increased average order size substantially, because more buyers opted into the bigger order to earn the bigger premium.
5. **Discounts for fast response, penalties for slow response** — a tiered-pricing structure (common in the seminar and events industry) where responding earlier is explicitly cheaper than responding later or at the point of purchase, applicable to advance product orders, event tickets, and subscription renewals alike.
6. **Sweepstakes and contests** — inherently deadline-driven by their entry cutoff, and used well beyond publishing-industry sweepstakes mailers, though Kennedy flags them as an expensive tactic that has to earn back its cost through the response lift it generates.
7. **Ease of responding** — reducing the physical friction of replying: toll-free numbers (ideally staffed broadly, not just business hours), preaddressed reply devices, and, even at the time of writing, fax- and web-based response options. He notes one counterintuitive finding — postage-paid return envelopes often don't lift response enough to justify their added cost — and recommends testing that specific element rather than assuming it always helps.

**Apply:** Pick at least one honest urgency mechanism (never a fabricated scarcity claim) and state it with a specific number or date rather than vague language, then separately audit how easy the actual act of responding is — a persuasive letter that ends in friction loses the immediate responses it worked to create.

## 4. Steps 14-20: The P.S., Checklists, Graphic Enhancement, and Pretesting

This range covers the back half of Kennedy's drafting sequence — the point where a sales letter has real content on the page and the work shifts from "what do I say" to "does this land, does it look easy to consume, and does it survive contact with a real reader." Two of these steps deserve special attention: Step 14 (the P.S. as mandatory, high-leverage copy) and Step 19 (Pretest, a genuinely reusable no-cost QA method).

### Step 14: The Creative P.S.

**What Kennedy says to do:** Never submit a sales letter without a postscript. Kennedy treats the P.S. as a required element, not an optional flourish — a letter isn't finished until one has been written and it has been made to work as hard as any other line in the piece.

**His reasoning:** Reading behavior doesn't move top-to-bottom the way a writer imagines it will. A large share of readers jump to the end of a letter before working through the body — some to check who signed it and decide whether it's worth their time, others simply because they read endings first out of habit (the same instinct that has some people read a mystery's ending before buying it, or eat dessert before the meal). Kennedy's point is that this "skip to the end" habit isn't a problem to fight — it's free real estate. A P.S. that recaps the core offer or promise can pull a reader who was about to discard the letter back up into the body copy. And even for readers who did start at the top and read straight through, the P.S. functions as a second headline: one more high-visibility chance to restate the promise, sharpen the offer, or add a reason to act now, positioned at the exact spot where attention is either leaving the page or lingering on it.

**Paraphrased application:** The technique isn't "add a sign-off line" — it's "treat the P.S. as a compressed version of your strongest selling argument, written to work as a standalone unit for someone who has read nothing else." A weak P.S. just says goodbye. A working one restates the single biggest benefit, attaches a deadline or bonus, or poses the exact question the letter has been trying to answer, so that a reader arriving there cold still gets the pitch.

**Apply:** After drafting any long-form page or email that a reader might scan bottom-first (long-form landing pages, cold emails, renewal notices), write a P.S. that stands alone as a mini-pitch — restated core benefit plus a reason to act now — rather than a generic closing line. Treat it as copy to be tested, not boilerplate to be appended.

### Step 15: Check the Checklists

**What Kennedy says to do:** Once several drafts exist and the surviving one is marked up past readability, stop and run it against a checklist of proven techniques before continuing. He positions this as a deliberate "midcourse correction" roughly halfway through the overall writing process — a checkpoint, not a final review.

**His reasoning:** Kennedy draws the comparison to airline pilots, who rely on checklists specifically because experience doesn't prevent forgetting routine steps under pressure — he notes his own real-world lapse of walking away from a running car as a small example of exactly this failure mode. A sales letter drafted over multiple sittings accumulates the same risk: proven moves (a benefit stated early, a guarantee reinforced, an objection answered) quietly get lost in revision. A checklist pass catches those omissions before the letter goes further, when fixes are still cheap.

**Note on overlap:** This skill already carries a comparable QA tool in more fully-specified form — see `whitman-cashvertising.md` §5, the 46-point pre-flight checklist built from Whitman's *Ca$hvertising*, organized into headline, opening line, body copy, credibility, and layout zones. Kennedy's own checklist content in this section is thematically the same territory (a mid-draft and pre-ship pass against known-good technique) rather than a materially different tool, so it isn't rebuilt here a second time.

**Apply:** Treat this step as a scheduling instruction, not a new checklist to memorize: build in an explicit mid-draft checkpoint — after the core argument is on the page but before final polish — and run the draft against `whitman-cashvertising.md`'s 46-point checklist (or Sugarman's trigger list) at that point, then again at final pre-ship.

### Step 16: Use Graphic Enhancement

**What Kennedy says to do:** Once a draft exists on the page, work with whoever is setting type or laying out the piece to add graphic devices that make a long letter look fast and easy to get through — even when it isn't actually short.

**His reasoning:** Kennedy's read on the audience is blunt: the consumer reading direct mail is increasingly impatient and unwilling to do work — the same impulse that made the remote control, the microwave, and passive exercise equipment popular. A page of unbroken paragraphs signals effort required, and effort required is where readers quit. Graphic devices change the *appearance* of the reading task even when the word count hasn't changed — they let a reader's eye sample the letter's shape before committing to read it in full, and that sampling either invites them in or lets them see the shape of the argument at a glance.

The specific devices Kennedy names as his favorites, paraphrased rather than listed verbatim, fall into a few families:
- **Structural markers** — bullet points and numbered lists that break a run of prose into scannable units.
- **Typographic emphasis** — underlining, boldface, and mixed typefaces used to make key phrases visually louder than the surrounding text.
- **Handwritten-feel touches** — simulated handwriting in margins or next to the P.S., and a mimicked yellow-highlighter overprint, both meant to suggest a real person marked up the page for the reader personally.
- **Visual breaks** — rows of asterisks or similar characters used as section dividers, and photographs with text wrapped around them.
- **Navigation aids** — a generous number of subheadings, so a skimmer moving down the page gets a mini-outline of the argument even without reading full paragraphs.

Crucially, Kennedy insists these choices should not be delegated entirely to a typesetter or designer. The copywriter has to be the one deciding what gets emphasized and how, because the point of these devices isn't decoration — it's adding what he calls "voice inflection" to flat text, the visual equivalent of a speaker's tone, pauses, and emphasis. A designer optimizing for aesthetics and a writer optimizing for which sentence needs to hit hardest are solving different problems, and the writer's problem is the one that sells. He also recommends keeping a running swipe file of well-designed mail pieces specifically so those choices can be shown to designers and typesetters rather than just described.

**Apply:** When formatting long-form copy (landing pages, sales emails, PDFs), treat bolding, subheads, bullets, and pull-quotes as copywriting decisions to make yourself before handing a draft to a designer — mark up which two or three lines per section must visually dominate, rather than leaving emphasis choices to whoever builds the page. Keep a swipe folder of well-designed comparable pieces (see `modern-swipe-file-2026.md` in this skill) to hand off alongside the copy.

### Step 17: Rewrite for Passion! Edit for Clarity!

This step is really two passes in sequence: inject emotional energy first, then cut hard for clarity. Treat them as separate subsections.

**Putting passion in — what Kennedy says:** Rewrite the letter with the goal of amplifying its emotional, "unpredictable" side, even if the underlying offer is logical or the audience is presumably rational. His reasoning is that buying decisions are driven by emotion first and rationalized with logic afterward — he credits speaker Zig Ziglar's term "emotional logic" for this pattern — and that this holds true even in technical, business-to-business fields. He points out that the most effective salespeople, even in dry technical industries, tend to be warm and enthusiastic rather than purely analytical; a sales letter, being ink on paper instead of a live person, has to work harder to convey that same enthusiasm because anything that feels appropriately expressive on the page will read as comparatively flat by the time it reaches the recipient. A letter that reads as purely factual — what he calls a "cold fish" letter — underperforms almost without exception, and he frames plain boringness, not any single technical flaw, as the number-one failure mode in marketing writing generally.

**The passion exercise:** Kennedy's suggested drill is to write as though composing a letter meant to persuade a reluctant-but-tempted romantic partner to drop everything and take a spontaneous trip together — leaning into bold, vivid, sensory language, anticipating the other person's objections and answering them, and building genuine desire rather than listing facts. The exercise is meant to prove that anyone can locate an emotional register for their subject, no matter how dry the underlying product seems.

**Paraphrased example — the "boring" B2B business:** Kennedy recounts a seminar attendee who ran a specialty electronic-parts distribution business and initially insisted his industry — selling parts, by part number, to manufacturers — offered no emotional angle at all, since customers supposedly cared only about price and on-time delivery. After working through the exercise, that business owner produced a letter, printed on bright paper, built around a headline promising the reader a long list of things they could enjoy after work once they never again had to worry about a late part shipment — followed by a substantial list of after-work possibilities, some funny, some ordinary, alongside a straightforward pitch of the company's inventory depth and delivery guarantee. Mailed to a small batch of non-customers, it produced a strong reply rate and converted into a meaningful volume of new, long-term accounts. Kennedy's takeaway: even a genuinely unglamorous product can be pitched through the *feeling* of owning or using it, or the relief and time it creates, rather than through its literal specifications.

**Aggressive editing — what Kennedy says:** After the passion pass, edit hard. The goal is not to shorten the letter for its own sake but to cut every word or phrase that fails to advance the sales argument — brevity is a side effect of that clarity pass, not the target itself. His illustrative fix takes a hedging, self-congratulatory sentence about competitors and guarantees and tightens it down to a direct claim: "Our many imitators can't match..." The shorter version reaches the point faster and reads with more confidence, and Kennedy notes this kind of editing can take multiple passes across several sittings rather than a single sitting.

**Apply:** Draft the emotional layer of a piece deliberately before editing it — write one version that overstates the feeling behind the benefit (relief, pride, freedom, status), then run an aggressive edit pass that removes every sentence not directly carrying the argument forward, checking each cut sentence against "does this make the ask stronger" rather than "is this too long."

### Step 18: Compare Your Draft to Examples

**What Kennedy says to do:** Lay the current draft side by side with strong existing examples and check it against them directly, rather than judging it in isolation. He recommends using several comparison pieces, not just one, so that the draft's particular strengths and weaknesses become visible by contrast rather than being averaged out against a single reference.

**His reasoning:** A writer deep in their own draft loses the ability to judge it against an objective bar — comparison against genuinely strong outside examples restores that bar. Kennedy poses this as a short set of direct questions to ask of the draft relative to the comparison pieces: does it flow as smoothly, is it as compelling, does it speak to its intended audience as precisely, is it as well-structured, is it as easy to follow, and does it drive a reader to action as effectively?

**Apply:** Before calling a draft final, pull two or three strong comparable pieces — not one — and read the draft immediately after each, asking Kennedy's five questions (flow, persuasiveness, audience fit, structure, action-drive) rather than a generic "does this feel good" pass. This skill's `legends-library.md` and `modern-swipe-file-2026.md` are the natural comparison sources for this pass.

### Step 19: Pretest

**What Kennedy says to do:** Before committing to any paid mail test — which even at modest volume can be a real expense — run the draft through a set of no-cost pretests. Kennedy is explicit that this step has, on occasion, revealed a letter needed to be scrapped entirely, and more often surfaces a handful of fixable issues before the letter ever reaches a paying test audience. He offers three specific, low-cost pretest methods.

**1. Read the letter aloud (to yourself).** A letter should flow conversationally whether read silently or aloud. Any phrase that trips up the reader's own tongue on a read-aloud pass — a "tongue twister" or awkward construction — should be fixed, since a letter is meant to read as easily as natural speech even though it's consumed silently.

**2. Read the letter to a small group of realistic prospects.** Kennedy describes a copywriter working in a blue-collar market who takes drafts to a neighborhood bar, buys a round, and reads the letter aloud to the group — not primarily for their stated opinions, but for an unstated "acid test": if people in the room start asking how they can actually get the product being pitched, the letter is working, regardless of what they say about it afterward when asked directly. Kennedy also describes a case where reading a direct-mail package (letter, catalog, response device) to a handful of typical customers surfaced a large number of unanswered questions the piece hadn't addressed; rewriting the letter to answer those questions in more depth measurably lifted response.

**3. Have a young child read the letter aloud to you.** Any word or phrase a child stumbles over is a candidate for simplification. Kennedy connects this to a broader point he calls the "Sophistication Trap" — the mistaken, expensive assumption that one's own customers are meaningfully more literate or sophisticated than the general population, even in business-to-business contexts. He cites period reporting on functional illiteracy rates among U.S. adults to support the claim that this isn't a fringe concern, and argues that even highly technical or professional audiences are still part of the same mass audience reached by television advertising written at a low reading level. His broader illustration is that some of the highest-paid direct-response copywriters in the industry write for outlets aimed at a mass, unsophisticated readership — and that studying the craft of those ads, not their subject matter, is a legitimate education in what actually moves a broad audience to respond. His conclusion, paraphrasing a line he attributes to P. T. Barnum, is that erring toward simpler language costs far less than overestimating how sophisticated a reader is.

**Apply:** Before any expensive test send (paid ad spend, a large email blast, a print run), run all three passes cheaply first: read the copy aloud yourself and flag anything you stumble on; read or send it to three to five people who resemble the real audience and watch what they *do* (do they ask how to buy, sign up, or reply) rather than only what they say; and default every phrase toward the simpler of two options rather than the more sophisticated one, on the assumption that the real audience skews less technical and less patient than the writer expects.

### Step 20: Bring Your Letter to Life

**What Kennedy says to do:** Once the draft has been through passion-editing, clarity-editing, comparison, and pretesting, produce it in exactly the form it will actually be sent — full formatting, final layout, ready for the printer or send button. He frames this as a dry run or dress rehearsal: the point where the letter stops being a working document and becomes the actual artifact a prospect will receive.

**His reasoning:** Seeing the piece rendered in its final form is itself diagnostic — problems in pacing, density, or visual weight that were invisible in a plain draft often become obvious once the letter looks the way it will look in the reader's hands. Kennedy frames this step as satisfying, but its practical value is that it's the last point at which a formatting-level flaw can be caught before real cost (printing, postage, ad spend, send volume) is committed.

**Apply:** Before shipping any finished piece, generate a fully-formatted preview exactly as the recipient will see it (rendered email, built landing page, formatted PDF) and read it in that final form, not the working draft — this is the point to catch layout-driven readability problems that plain-text editing can't reveal.

## 5. Steps 21-28: Production, Editing, Second Opinions, and Mailing

By this point in Kennedy's system, the letter itself — headline, offer, copy, structure — is done. Steps 21 through 28 cover everything that happens *after* the writing: turning a manuscript into a physical mailed object without losing what made it work on the page. Kennedy treats this stretch with the same discipline he brings to the writing itself, on the theory that a brilliant letter that arrives damaged, looks like junk mail, or gets rushed to press with an uncaught error never gets a chance to persuade anyone.

### Step 21: Change Graphic Enhancements

**What Kennedy says to do:** Sit down with the typesetter, layout artist, and printer as a group and walk through every graphic device the letter uses — soliciting their ideas, not just handing off execution. This is also the point to finalize any enclosures riding along with the letter: coupons, certificates, product photos, reply cards.

**His reasoning:** Whatever gets chosen here has to be something you're genuinely certain about and that will land with your target reader, because changes made *after* this stage are expensive and frustrating to unwind — the cost of a wrong call rises sharply once production is underway.

**Apply:** Before locking graphics or enclosures, run one review pass with everyone who touches production at once — not sequential one-off approvals — and treat "we can fix it later" as a cost estimate, not a reassurance.

### Step 22: Edit Again

**What Kennedy says to do:** Read the now-typeset, formatted letter again as if for the first time, hunting for what only becomes visible once it looks real: a headline that doesn't stand out from the body copy, a hard-to-read color choice, a page break landing in the wrong place, a typeface that undercuts the tone.

**His reasoning:** Nobody enjoys this step, but it catches problems that survived every earlier pass simply because the letter didn't look finished yet — formatting itself introduces new failure modes that plain-draft editing can't surface. He explicitly suggests this is a good moment to re-run an earlier checklist pass against the newly typeset version, since formatting changes can quietly reintroduce problems a checklist would have caught.

**Apply:** Review the production-formatted version as its own draft, not a formality — a typeset page surfaces different errors than a manuscript does (contrast, hierarchy, color legibility) — and skipping this because "the words already got approved" misses exactly what this step is for.

### Step 23: Mail a Mock-Up

**What Kennedy says to do:** Assemble the single best mock-up of the entire mailing — letter plus every enclosure — exactly as it will go out, and mail it to yourself.

**His reasoning:** The point isn't to proofread it again; it's to receive it, cold, sitting in your own ordinary stack of mail, the way a prospect will. Kennedy names three specific things this test exposes that no desk review can: whether the piece's size or construction will get roughed up or damaged in transit, whether it holds up next to the rest of the mail you get on a normal day, and — if the letter is designed to feel personal — whether it actually reads that way once it's sitting in a pile, or whether it just looks like the rest of the junk mail. Given the work already invested in the letter, he treats skipping this real-world test as a false economy.

**Apply:** Physically produce and receive the final mailing package yourself before committing to a full run — a screen preview or printer's proof can't tell you how a piece behaves once it's competing with a real mail stack (or, for digital work, a real inbox).

### Step 24: The Cool Off

**What Kennedy says to do:** Once the mock-up arrives, do nothing with it for three to five days.

**His reasoning:** The instruction is deliberately anticlimactic because the problem it solves is psychological, not procedural. The longer and more closely you've worked on a letter, the more likely you are to have fallen for it — to see what's good about it and go blind to its flaws — and by this stage you're also likely impatient to be done with what has been, admittedly, a long system. A short cooling-off period restores the distance needed to judge the piece honestly rather than protectively.

**What makes this step distinctive** is Kennedy's own admission against it: he confesses that under real deadline pressure he sometimes skips the cool-off, and several of the other late-stage steps along with it — and he believes his finished work suffers a little whenever he does. He doesn't frame the cool-off as mandatory in every case; he frames it as a luxury of extra time that pays for itself in judgment, and his instruction is conditional — if you have the time, take it. That's a narrower claim than "always wait": it's "this specific trade-off is worth making when you can afford it," offered by someone who has knowingly made the opposite trade and named the price he paid for it.

**Apply:** Build 3-5 days of enforced silence into the schedule between "mock-up in hand" and "final sign-off" whenever the calendar allows it — not as padding, but as the specific mechanism that separates your judgment of the piece from your attachment to having made it. When the deadline genuinely doesn't allow it, treat that as a known, named risk to final quality, not a costless shortcut.

### Step 25: Get Second Opinions

**What Kennedy says to do:** Get second opinions, but "within certain very definite limits" — his phrase — and the limits are the substance of the step, not a footnote to it.

**His reasoning:** He opens by naming the trap: everyone is quick to spot how unqualified everyone *else* is to give an opinion, and almost no one applies that same skepticism to their own. He illustrates with a deliberately absurd data point — surveys putting something close to 70% of licensed drivers in the "above average" bracket, a statistical impossibility — to make the point that confidence and competence are only loosely correlated, and that most of the opinions available to you, unfiltered, are at best useless and at worst actively harmful to the letter.

**His bounding rule for whose opinion counts:** the more experience a person has specifically in direct marketing, the more their feedback is worth weighing — not general business acumen, not good taste, not seniority, but direct-response experience specifically. His concrete recommendation for finding such people is to build a standing network rather than solicit opinions ad hoc: seek out a local Direct Marketing Club and attend its meetings, find peers inside national, state, and local trade associations who are themselves aggressive, progressive marketers, and — his most specific reference — study Napoleon Hill's *Think and Grow Rich* for its "mastermind group" concept as a blueprint for assembling that kind of ongoing creative alliance. He also positions the book itself, and a paid personal-critique service he offered readers at the time, as stand-ins for that network for a reader who doesn't have one yet.

The step is narrower than "show it to people and see what they think." It's: identify the small set of people whose direct-marketing track record actually qualifies their opinion, build a standing relationship with a few of them before you need them, and weight everyone else's feedback accordingly — including your own, absent that specific experience.

**Apply:** Before circulating a draft for feedback, name who on the list has actual direct-response or conversion-copy track record versus who has general opinions about writing — and weight the two groups differently rather than averaging every comment equally. If no one on hand qualifies, treat that as a gap worth fixing before the next project, not just this one.

### Step 26: Give It the Final Review

**What Kennedy says to do:** Find a quiet, undistracted place and read the letter through one more time, on its own, away from the production noise of the preceding steps.

**His reasoning:** This is the last chance to tinker before the letter is out of your hands entirely — a final, dedicated read with no other task attached to it.

**Apply:** Schedule one uninterrupted, distraction-free final read of the piece in its exact final form — separate from any editing session — as the explicit last gate before it leaves your hands.

### Step 27: Go to Press

**What Kennedy says to do:** Hand the piece to the printer, but don't hand over control of it. Stay personally involved through proofs and blue lines, especially on a complex or high-volume job.

**His reasoning:** Kennedy draws a sharp line between two categories of changes that come up at this stage: printer errors (PEs) and author alterations (AAs). AAs — deciding to rewrite something after the fact — are expensive at this stage, and he's explicit that after the exhaustive review process the system already put the letter through, there's little reason left to be rewriting paragraphs; he reserves AAs for a genuinely glaring, damaging mistake, not stylistic fussing like moving a comma relative to a quotation mark. PEs are the opposite case, and his instruction there is to push back hard and without apology: if the color, paper stock, photo quality, or any other spec doesn't match what you ordered, say so and don't accept a suggestion to leave it "as is" or switch to whatever's more convenient for the printer. His reasoning is direct — you're paying, you set the specs because they serve the letter's effectiveness, and you bear the cost if the piece underperforms, so the decision stays yours. He also flags a specific billing trap: corrections for the printer's own errors shouldn't cost extra, but he notes it's common for a few such charges to slip onto the final invoice anyway, framing it as printers testing whether you're paying attention — so check the bill.

**Apply:** Personally review proofs/blue lines (or the digital equivalent — a staging build, a final export) on anything complex or high-volume rather than delegating sign-off, hold the line on spec deviations that are the vendor's fault, reserve last-minute content rewrites for genuinely damaging errors only, and audit the final invoice line by line for corrections that shouldn't have been billed.

### Step 28: Mail!

**What Kennedy says to do:** Before pulling the trigger, take one more pass on list selection and mailing method — the logic being that if reviewing everything else has made you second-guess either one, now is still the time to change it, not after the postage is spent.

**His reasoning and tactics, split by scale:**

For **narrow, small-quantity mailings** (his rough marker: under roughly a thousand recipients) aimed at tightly selected segments, he recommends techniques that reinforce a personal feel and improve the odds of surviving the recipient's first junk-mail sort: real stamps rather than metered postage, typed or hand-addressed envelopes rather than printed labels (and if labels are unavoidable, compensating with bold teaser copy instead of trying to fake a personal look), and rubber-stamped markings like "urgent" or "requested information enclosed" to add a person-to-person cue. He also raises non-postal delivery — courier or express services — as a reliable way to guarantee a piece reaches the right person and gets opened, and unconventional packaging as an attention-forcing device, citing an example of audio players tucked inside stuffed toys and delivered by courier to a small list, which drove response and follow-on business out of proportion to the list size.

For **large-volume mailings**, where individual-level personalization isn't practical, his guidance narrows to a few specific levers beyond the copy itself: avoid impersonal address lines like "Occupant" or "Postal Patron," which he says reads instantly as junk mail and depresses response — real-name addressing is usually worth its added cost, and where it truly isn't feasible, an imaginative descriptive address (naming the household or role directly, rather than a generic title) is a middle option. Where the envelope can't be made to look personal at all, he suggests the opposite strategy: openly declaring on the outside that there's an offer inside, with teaser copy strong enough to earn its own open. He also raises borrowing a recognizable celebrity's name or image on the envelope as a credibility and attention device for mailings where that fits the brand. His closing note for this step is that different audiences call for different combinations of these tactics — the job is to actually analyze the target rather than default to a favorite technique, then commit to the choice.

**Apply:** Match mailing/delivery tactics to list size and segment rather than using one playbook for every send — small, high-value segments justify personalization tactics that don't scale; large sends need the few levers (real names over generic titles, strong teaser copy) that still move response at volume. Treat this as one more explicit decision point, not an assumed default.

### Closer: "Congratulations!"

Kennedy closes by stating plainly that these are the same 28 steps he personally runs every time he writes a sales letter, and the same ones he teaches in his own seminars — not a simplified version for the book. He calls the sequence close to a "miracle system," pointing to people with no advertising background and no particular writing talent who followed the steps in order and produced letters that performed. The claim underneath the closer is really about the earlier steps and this one together: the system's value isn't any single trick, it's following the full sequence — including the unglamorous production tail — rather than stopping once the copy itself feels finished.

**Apply:** Treat "the copy is done" and "the piece is ready to send" as two different milestones with real work between them — the second is where letters that read brilliantly on screen actually succeed or fail in the real world.

### Why this tail belongs in a copywriting reference

Steps 1-20 of Kennedy's system are persuasion craft: researching the prospect, shaping the offer, building the argument, writing and testing the words. Steps 21-28 are almost entirely operational — production coordination, proofing, cooling-off periods, printer logistics, postage tactics. It's fair to ask why a copywriting reference covers proof-checking and stamp choice at all.

Kennedy's implicit answer, carried through this whole stretch, is that copy quality and delivery quality aren't separable in practice: a letter with a flawless headline and offer is worthless if it arrives torn from an oversized envelope, gets misread as generic junk mail because of a label and a metered stamp, or ships with a printer's color error nobody caught. The production tail is where a technically excellent piece of persuasion either reaches its reader intact and credible, or doesn't reach them as intended at all — which is why it belongs in the same system rather than a separate "print production" concern. For a working copywriter, the steps that never mention a headline are still the steps that decide whether the headline gets read.

## 6. The Most Versatile Sales Tool of All

This section argues that a well-built sales letter isn't a single-purpose direct-mail artifact — it's a general-purpose business tool Kennedy applies across roughly eight use cases, several of which sit outside classic direct-response mail entirely.

**Lead generation, without the volatility of other channels.** He contrasts letters against in-person cold calling (expensive, high turnover) and against media advertising, whose results swing on uncontrollable factors like page position or what's airing opposite a commercial slot. A tuned letter campaign, by comparison, tends to produce a consistent, repeatable response rate per unit mailed — a plannable lead source rather than a lottery ticket.

**Apply:** If a lead channel's results are noisy and hard to forecast, that's itself a reason to test a letter-based version of the same offer — consistency, not just volume, is the selling point.

**Supporting telemarketing rather than replacing it.** Kennedy describes businesses that send a letter before the cold call and see meaningfully better results than the call alone. The letter gives the caller a legitimate reason to be calling and gives the prospect something concrete to reference mid-conversation — useful whether the call's goal is an appointment or a close.

**Apply:** If a team's cold-call connect rates are poor, a short pre-call letter referencing an upcoming call can turn a cold call into a warm one.

**Driving foot traffic to a physical location.** He cites a recurring dealership mailing and, at more length, a deli/restaurant that built its local business from nothing with a "free lunch" letter mailed to nearby offices — accepting a loss-leader giveaway because the profit on drinks, sides, and desserts, plus the speed of building a client base compared to slower media buys, made the trade worthwhile.

**Apply:** A letter-driven traffic offer works especially well for a business with a fixed local radius and a low-cost item that can absorb being given away to seed higher-margin add-on sales.

**Business and personal persuasive correspondence, generally.** Kennedy's broadest claim in this section is that a letter selling a tax-savings service, a letter selling a self-improvement program, and a letter making a case to a boss, a school, or an elected official are fundamentally the same act: persuasion in print. He argues nearly all business communication carries a persuasive intent, acknowledged or not, and that the letter-writing discipline in the book applies to any of it — not only to campaigns explicitly labeled "direct mail."

**Apply:** Treat internal memos, board updates, and even personal asks (to a landlord, a school, an official) as sales letters with a non-purchase call to action — headline, problem, credibility, and a specific ask still apply.

The throughline across these uses is that Kennedy doesn't treat "the sales letter" as a format tied to postage — it's a persuasion structure that happens to also work by mail, which is also the bridge into Section 7's sequence idea and Section 8's channel-portability argument.

## 7. The Million-Dollar Secret: The Power of a Sequence

This is the book's most distinctive strategic claim, and Kennedy presents it as the single idea most responsible for his own financial success: **the sale is almost never won by one letter.** The biggest and most common mistake marketers make, in his framing, is the "one-shot mailing" — sending a single letter once and judging the offer a failure if it doesn't convert.

His alternative isn't Madison Avenue's version of repetition either — he's dismissive of slow, expensive brand-building measured loosely by market share or recognition over years. Instead, his model is a **tight, deliberately timed sequence run over roughly a 45- to 60-day window**, built to create recognition and generate real, immediate response quickly rather than diffusely. He illustrates it with a recurring seminar example — a multi-letter campaign written in the voice of a fictionalized Italian-restaurant host — that he says he used for years in front of a combined audience of well over two million people.

The mechanism: successive letters in the sequence don't just repeat the first message, they escalate it. The example sequence opens with a first letter making an offer, then follows with a "second notice" that opens with an attention-getting physical gimmick, restates the same core problem and pushes it further, expresses mock disappointment that the prospect hasn't responded yet, and then **sweetens the original offer** rather than just repeating the ask. Kennedy explicitly credits this notice-escalation structure to the collection industry's practice of first-notice/second-notice/third-notice dunning letters — a debt-collection cadence adapted into a sales cadence.

His reported result pattern: the second and third letters in a sequence, combined, often produce as much response as the first letter alone — sometimes more. He presents this as a stable, close-to-universal structural effect rather than something specific to one category of product; a sophisticated audience (his own example: CEOs buying costly computer systems) responds to the same sequence *structure* even when the surface tone is dialed down from a playful, informal style. Style is swappable; the multi-touch structure underneath is what does the work.

**Apply:** Before writing a single "best" version of an offer letter, plan the sequence: what does message 2 say if message 1 gets no response, and what does it add (urgency, a gimmick, a sweetened offer, a deadline) rather than merely repeat? Budget the campaign as a 45–60 day arc, not a single send date.

**Apply:** When a first-touch offer underperforms, resist rewriting it from scratch — write the follow-up that acknowledges the non-response, raises urgency, and adds value to the same core offer, mirroring a notice-2/notice-3 structure rather than a fresh pitch.

## 8. "High-Tech" Sales Letters (What's Dated, What Still Holds)

Kennedy wrote this closing section for a book whose first edition dates to 1991, at a moment when he treats broadcast fax, fax-on-demand, and the Internet as genuinely new tools that needed explaining to his readership. Read today, the specific technologies are almost entirely obsolete, but the chapter is worth keeping for one structural claim that has aged well.

**What's obsolete — treat as historical artifact, not guidance:**
- **Broadcast fax and fax-on-demand as primary channels.** He describes buying fax-number lists the way one buys mailing lists, and praises fax-on-demand as a way to deliver material instantly without printing or postage costs. Both are now marginal-to-defunct as marketing channels; his specific advice to keep faxed letters to one or two pages, or to worry about photos not faxing clearly, has no modern application.
- **Auto-responder framing as novel technology.** He describes preprogrammed multi-day email sequences as a new capability worth explaining from scratch. The mechanism is now baseline infrastructure in any email platform, not something to introduce as a discovery.
- **The claim about average website visit length**, offered as evidence that long copy works fine on the web, reflects an early-web reading pattern that doesn't describe modern browsing behavior; modern web copy has to earn attention in seconds before a reader will commit to depth.
- **The author's fax number and an early publisher web address**, given as contact information, are purely of their era.

**What still holds — the durable principle underneath the dated tech:**

Kennedy's real argument in this chapter isn't about fax machines or auto-responders — it's that the persuasion structure of a strong sales letter is medium-independent. His summary of the point is close to the chapter's thesis: a strong sales letter is a strong sales letter "regardless of the delivery medium." He extends this with a memorable comparison between carving a message in stone, hand-lettering it, and posting it to a website — headline, problem, agitation, credibility, offer construction, and a clear call to response don't change because the delivery pipe changed.

He also flags a caution that has arguably become more relevant with time: people who sell the *technical* execution of a channel (in his day, early web and email vendors; today, ad-platform specialists and marketing-automation vendors) often have real expertise in the mechanics of their channel and little to no grounding in persuasion. His advice is to take their technical advice on the channel at face value while independently vetting anything they say about messaging or offer — the two skill sets aren't the same, and channel expertise doesn't imply copy expertise.

**Apply:** When adapting a proven direct-mail structure (headline → problem → agitation → credibility → offer → guarantee → urgency → call to action) to a new channel — SMS, a landing page, a push notification, an in-app message — port the structure deliberately rather than assuming the new channel needs its own persuasion logic from scratch.

**Apply:** When a platform or automation vendor recommends a messaging change, treat it as a hypothesis to test against direct-response principles, not as authoritative simply because it came from the channel expert.
