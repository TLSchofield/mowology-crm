# Claude Hopkins — My Life in Advertising, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of *My Life in Advertising* (Harper & Brothers, 1927), Claude C. Hopkins' memoir, built from the public-domain OCR text (pre-1929 US publication, unrestricted). Hopkins is widely credited as the father of reason-why copy and of testing advertising like a science; he ran the copy department at Lord & Thomas and built or rescued the advertising behind Schlitz, Palmolive, Pepsodent, Quaker Oats' Puffed Wheat and Puffed Rice, Van Camp's, Goodyear, and a string of early automobile brands. He wrote this book in 1927, near the end of his career, as a favor to "those who will follow" — not a manual, but a chain of first-person case studies told the way he'd tell them over dinner: problem, gambit, result, moral.

His actual manual, *Scientific Advertising* (1923), states the same principles as compressed rules and is **not available this round** — this file is not a substitute for it. What follows captures Hopkins' philosophy *as it shows up in practice*, campaign by campaign, plus the general principles that recur often enough across the book to count as his creed. Nothing here is copied from the text; quotes under 15 words appear sparingly and only where the period phrasing itself is the lesson.

**A caution built into the source**: Hopkins wrote this as an old man reflecting on his own legend, and he was known to round his numbers upward. Where a figure looks suspiciously clean or is impossible to verify from outside the book (a specific dollar profit, a specific redemption count), it's flagged in place rather than repeated as fact. Treat his self-reported outcomes as "he said it worked, dramatically" rather than as audited results.

Use this reference alongside `sugarman-handbook.md` for long-form persuasive copy, and alongside `legends-library.md` (Volume 1 there is the short version of this).

## 1. Campaign Case Studies

Technique only, paraphrased; no ad copy is reproduced beyond a short phrase in quotes.

| Campaign | The problem | What Hopkins did | Result (as reported) | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bissell Carpet Sweeper** (his first success, ~1890s) | A ten-year durable good, one-dollar profit per unit, no way to advertise it profitably by ordinary means; dealers were overstocked and indifferent. | Rather than push the sweeper itself, he sold *scarcity and variety*: sweepers built in twelve named woods, "never to be offered again"; later a run finished in rare vermilion wood shipped from India. He wrote to dealers offering the run as a limited privilege, conditional on the dealer signing a display agreement — positioning Bissell as doing the dealer a favor, not asking for an order. | Reportedly sold a run of 250,000 sweepers in three weeks; Bissell went on to dominate the carpet-sweeper trade. | A commodity nobody can advertise on function can often be advertised on a manufactured scarcity or variety story instead — offer it as a privilege, not a pitch. |
| **Swift & Co. — Cotosuet "World's Largest Cake"** (~1898) | Cotosuet was an undifferentiated compound-lard product chasing an established rival (Cottolene); newspaper ads alone produced no traction and Hopkins was given weeks to show results. | Built a towering cake made with Cotosuet instead of butter as a live retail-store event, advertised in advance, with demonstrators sampling slices and a guess-the-weight contest that required a purchase to enter. Repeated the stunt city to city, using press coverage from earlier cities to pre-sell bakers on carrying it. | Drew large crowds (a reported 105,000 visitors to one store in a week) and put Cotosuet on a profitable footing in every city visited. | When there's no product argument to make, a dramatic, sample-driven event beats any amount of "dignified" copy — Hopkins argued no argument competes with a live demonstration. |
| **Dr. Shoop's Remedies, Racine** (early 1900s) | A line of medicines sold only through door-to-door agents, with the agent channel dying and no path onto drugstore shelves. | Devised the **druggist-signed guaranty**: the local druggist who took the customer's money personally signed a promise of refund if the remedy failed, converting an unknown mail-order promise into a face-to-face local one. | Reportedly built a business large enough to expand from house-to-house distribution into national newspaper advertising. | A guarantee is only as strong as the trust behind the guarantor — route it through someone the buyer already trusts, not through the seller. |
| **Liquozone** (~1904–1909) | A four-times-failed proprietary germicide; the company was insolvent (net worth reported as roughly $45,000 *below* zero) after four different ad men had tried and failed over four years. | Proposed buying the first 50-cent bottle for every inquirer (i.e., the company paid, not the customer), followed by a druggist-signed guaranty on a larger six-dollar order. Financed the initial run by pre-selling drugstore orders by letter and assigning those orders to the ad agency in place of cash. | Hopkins reports roughly 18 cents cost per inquiry against roughly 90 cents in resulting sales, and describes turning the company profitable within a year, later expanding into Europe. *(These specific profit and headcount figures are Hopkins' own recollection decades later and are not independently verifiable — treat as illustrative, not audited.)* | Removing all risk from the *first* step of a transaction (you pay, not them) converts a "prove it to me" prospect into a taker almost automatically — the guarantee only has to hold on the *second*, paid step. |
| **Schlitz Beer** (~1900s, while Hopkins worked for the J. L. Stack agency) | Schlitz was in fifth place among brewers, and every brewer was shouting the same unprovable word — "Pure" — in ever-larger type, with no effect. | Toured the brewery like a customer would never be allowed to, and had every routine, unremarkable step of the process explained: filtered-air cooling rooms, sterilized pipes, a mother yeast cell refined over 1,200 experiments, deep artesian wells, months of aging. Every brewer did some version of this. None had ever *said* it. He wrote the process up as the story of Schlitz's purity. | Hopkins reports Schlitz moved from fifth place to near parity with the leader within months; he calls it one of his defining campaigns. | The most reusable move in the whole book: find the routine, industry-wide practices your own client is too close to see as remarkable, and be the first competitor to say them out loud — the fact doesn't have to be exclusive to become associated with you. |
| **Early automobile advertising** (Chalmers, Hudson, Overland, Reo, Mitchell, Studebaker, ~1908–1915) | A fast-changing category where buyers had no reliable way to judge one manufacturer's engineering claims against another's, and "best in the world"-style copy was already worthless. | Repeated moves across several accounts: named a real, credentialed engineer as the face of the car (Howard E. Coffin for Chalmers, then Hudson) rather than the company; used exact, specific figures instead of superlatives ("our profit is 9 percent," itemized part costs); for Reo, had the founder sign the ads personally under the name "My Farewell Car." For Mitchell, built a full campaign around an "Efficiency Expert" and drove a surge in sales — into a car whose engineering did not hold up, producing widespread returns and a ruined name. | Hudson and Reo campaigns are reported as durable, multi-year successes; the Studebaker "quantity production lowers cost" campaign became, in his words, the new standard note for auto advertising; Mitchell is presented explicitly as his cautionary tale. | Specific, checkable figures and a named human expert beat brand-name boosting every time people are choosing between similar mechanical products — but advertising that outsells the product's actual quality only accelerates the wreck. |
| **Goodyear — "No-Rim-Cut" tires** (~1910s) | Goodyear's engineers were describing their new "straight-side" tire construction to customers in manufacturing language nobody outside the factory cared about; the ad budget was small and tires were seen as unadvertisable. | Reframed the construction detail as a customer outcome and coined a name that stated the benefit directly: "No-Rim-Cut Tires, 10% Oversize." Later ran a campaign naming every dealer who stocked at least $150 of inventory, making non-participation visibly costly to a dealer's competitors. | Reports Goodyear's ad budget grew from about $40,000 to nearly $1,000,000 a year and the brand took the lead in tires; he says he eventually lost the account when the company drifted into "institutional" (boastful, plant-touring) advertising he refused to write. | A coined name that states the customer benefit directly can function as a complete ad by itself — and naming participating dealers publicly is a cheap, self-reinforcing way to force distribution. |
| **Palmolive Soap** (from 1911) | An unremarked-on toilet soap inside a laundry-soap company that had otherwise been advised against advertising at all; no one at the agency had yet registered the strength of a beauty appeal. | Ran ads building interest in the beauty story, then followed with a coupon good at any store for a ten-cent cake — explicitly framed as "we will buy this for you," not "free," because a bought item signals the advertiser's own confidence while a free one cheapens the product. The coupon mechanic also forced every dealer to stock the product before the ad ran. | Local test in Benton Harbor, Michigan (reported cost around $700) is said to have paid for itself before the bills were due; Palmolive became, in his account, the best-selling toilet soap in the world. | "We will buy it for you" outperforms "free" because it reframes the offer as the advertiser's confidence rather than a giveaway — wording a sample offer is not a cosmetic choice. |
| **Van Camp's** (pork and beans, then evaporated milk, ~1910s) | On pork and beans: 94% of housewives baked their own, and every existing canned-bean ad simply said "buy my brand." On evaporated milk: a rival brand had held a market (New York) for years purely through housewife familiarity. | For beans: attacked home-baking itself (time, digestibility, uneven results) rather than competing brands, then ran the audacious headline inviting readers to "try our rivals, too" — since the case against home baking applied to every canned brand equally. For milk: ran a coupon good for a free can, pre-notified grocers of expected demand so they'd stock in advance, and executed it as a single-day national push. | Claims 97% New York grocery distribution secured in three weeks before the ad ran, and roughly 1,460,000 coupons redeemed from one Sunday insertion. *(These precise redemption figures come only from Hopkins' memory and should be read as illustrative of scale, not as an audited count.)* | When your product isn't actually better than direct competitors, attack the *real* competing behavior (home baking, brand inertia) instead — and inviting comparison to rivals can read as more confident than attacking them. |
| **Puffed Wheat & Puffed Rice** (Quaker Oats, ~1910s) | Two low-momentum, undistinguished cereal products with declining sales that the manufacturer already considered close to failures. | Renamed "Wheat Berries" to "Puffed Wheat" to pair the two products under one story; raised prices to fund the campaign; built a personality around the inventor, Professor A. P. Anderson; and coined the deliberately strange, ridiculed-at-the-time line "foods shot from guns" to describe the puffing process, alongside visuals of the grains blown up to eight times normal size. Also corrected an early mistake of mailing free samples indiscriminately, restricting samples afterward to readers who'd clipped a coupon in response to the story. | Reports it became the most successful cereal campaign of its time and the category's biggest earner; the newspaper-vs-magazine misallocation and the promiscuous-sampling phase are both presented as costly early errors he caught and fixed. | Curiosity-driven, even ridiculed-sounding language can outperform safe description if it's paired with a concrete, visualizable mechanism — and a sample given to someone who didn't ask for it is close to worthless. |
| **Pepsodent** (from ~1915) | A technical dental-film product with no obvious lay explanation, priced at double the going rate for tooth paste, in a field Hopkins initially didn't think could be explained to ordinary buyers. | Found a single findable idea in the dental literature — a filmy coating on teeth — and built the whole campaign around beauty and reward (attractive people, whiter smiles) rather than prevention or disease. Keyed every ad with a coupon so results came back appeal by appeal; used those results to discover, by trial, that framing the free sample around "free" (which worked in food) actually *cut* response by roughly three-quarters in a hygiene product, where "free" read as commercial rather than beneficent — so he rewrote around service instead. | Hopkins calls it the largest success of his career, reporting national dominance within a year and worldwide distribution within four; he states he personally made roughly a million dollars from an early equity stake. *(Personal profit figures again come only from his own account.)* | Coupon-keyed testing doesn't just optimize a winning approach — it can reveal that a technique proven in one category (free samples, in food) actively backfires in an adjacent one (hygiene); never assume a rule transfers without a new test. |

## 2. General Principles

Extracted across the whole book, not stated by Hopkins as a numbered list.

1. **Reason-why, not assertion.** Every claim needs the mechanism behind it — not "pure" but *what makes it pure and how you know*. This is the throughline of nearly every case above; a bare superlative ("best in the world") gets discounted, while a specific, checkable fact ("9 per cent profit," "150 times its volume in lather," "10% oversize") gets believed at face value because readers assume advertisers can't lie about numbers in a reputable publication. *Apply:* before writing a benefit claim, write the sentence that proves it; if you can't, the claim is too thin to use.
2. **The common-fact-as-claim move (the Schlitz principle).** A fact true of your whole category can still become *your* exclusive property if you're the first to say it plainly — imitators afterward only reinforce the association. *Apply:* audit your client's "boring," unremarkable operational habits before reaching for a differentiator that doesn't exist.
3. **Sampling is a science, not a giveaway.** Free samples handed out unrequested ("cast on the doorstep") are reported as consistently ineffective across several campaigns; a sample earned by clipping a coupon, after reading a story, converts far better because the reader has already invested attention. Buying the item for the reader ("we will buy") outperforms calling it free. *Apply:* gate every sample behind a small action, and test the exact wording of the offer — "free" is not a neutral word.
4. **Institutional and vanity advertising is close to always a mistake.** Hopkins repeatedly frames "boasting" — about plant size, history, or the founder's pride — as advertising's most common and most expensive failure mode, and reports losing accounts (Goodyear among them) specifically because clients wanted to brag rather than sell. *Apply:* if a line exists to make the client feel good rather than to move the reader to act, cut it.
5. **Test everything through a keyed, trackable mechanism.** Coupons, exclusive local test cities, and letter-based offers all served the same purpose for him: turning "I think this will work" into a number within weeks, at low cost, before committing large budgets. The Pepsodent "free" discovery and the Quaker Oats sampling correction both came directly from this discipline. *Apply:* before a national or full-budget rollout, find *some* mechanism — a coupon, a code, a small paid test market — that gives you a number, not an opinion.
6. **Prevention doesn't sell; cure and reward do.** People will act to fix an existing problem but rarely to avoid a hypothetical future one; Hopkins says he never once pictured "the afflicted" in Pepsodent ads, choosing beauty and attractiveness over decay and disease. *Apply:* if your product is preventive, find the way it can be framed as delivering an immediate, positive reward rather than avoiding a future loss.
7. **Write for the average reader, not the sophisticated one.** He credits his own lack of formal education and years of door-to-door canvassing as advantages, arguing that literary style and "fine writing" read as an effort to sell and trigger resistance, and that the great majority of buyers distrust polish. *Apply:* draft in plain, short sentences and treat any line a copywriting peer would call "beautiful" as a warning sign, not a compliment.
8. **Service framing beats self-interest framing.** "Buy my brand" and "insist on the genuine" are, in his account, close to worthless because every reader recognizes a purely selfish appeal and discounts it; framing the same offer as help to the reader (a free trial, a guarantee, advice) gets action. *Apply:* rewrite every "buy X" line as "here's what X does for you," and cut any phrase whose only function is protecting the brand's ego.
9. **Skepticism toward cleverness and humor.** He states flatly that money-spending is serious to the reader and that frivolity or amusement-seeking copy has never built a lasting success in his experience, contrasting it with the never-forgotten sincerity of a direct, service-based appeal. *Apply:* if a line exists mainly to be clever or funny rather than to move the sale forward, question whether it survives a cut.
10. **Become a fast, hands-on expert on the product before writing.** Repeatedly — the brewery tour for Schlitz, the incubator catalogs for Racine, weeks of research before Pepsodent, canvassing door to door before Van Camp's — Hopkins immersed himself in the operational reality of a product before drafting a word, on the theory that the useful, specific fact is usually hiding in a detail the manufacturer considers too ordinary to mention. *Apply:* interview the people who make the product about what they think is *boring*, not just what they're proud of.
11. **Never try to sell the same customer twice.** He argues repeatedly against paying to win the consumer and then paying again to win the dealer or jobber — pick one point of attack (usually the end consumer) and let the rest of the channel follow the demand you create. *Apply:* map who actually needs to be persuaded for a sale to happen, and refuse to fund persuasion at more than one link in that chain.
12. **A completed story in one reading, not a series.** He assumes most readers see any given ad once, like a news item, so every ad should be a complete argument rather than one installment of a drip campaign; the winning appeals discovered by testing should be folded into *every* ad, not spread across a rotation. *Apply:* before running a campaign as a "series," check whether it depends on the reader seeing multiple ads — if so, each individual ad is probably underperforming on its own.
13. **Habits can't be profitably created by paid advertising — only redirected.** His stated biggest mistake was trying to grow Quaker Oats by converting non-oatmeal households; he later concluded that new eating and hygiene habits are built by free editorial coverage and general culture, not paid ads, and that paid advertising only wins when it offers *existing* users of a category a better way. *Apply:* before funding a campaign aimed at non-users of a whole category, ask whether you're trying to create a habit (usually unprofitable) or convert within an existing one (usually workable).

## 3. Problem → Hopkins Tool

| Symptom | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Product is identical to every competitor's | §1 Schlitz — find the common, unstated industry fact and say it first |
| Client wants to describe manufacturing/engineering details | §1 Goodyear — translate the detail into a named customer benefit |
| Superlative claims aren't landing | Principle 1 — replace with one specific, checkable figure |
| Free trial/sample isn't converting | Principle 3 — gate the sample behind an action; test "we will buy" vs. "free" |
| Client wants to brag about the company/plant/history | Principle 4 — cut it; that's institutional advertising, and Hopkins lost accounts over it |
| No budget for a full launch | §1 Liquozone, Palmolive — run a cheap, keyed local test first |
| Selling a preventive product that isn't moving | Principle 6 — reframe around reward/beauty, not avoided disaster |
| Copy feels "too polished" and isn't converting | Principle 7 — simplify to plain, short, unliterary language |
| "Buy our brand" language isn't working | Principle 8 — rewrite as a service or benefit to the reader |
| Ad campaign relies on the reader seeing several installments | Principle 12 — fold your best-tested appeals into one complete ad |
| Trying to win over people outside the category entirely | Principle 13 — check whether you're funding habit creation (usually a loss) |
| Selling to consumers *and* paying dealers/jobbers to push it | Principle 11 — pick one link in the chain and let the rest follow demand |
| An account or claim's real number is hard to verify | Recall the note at the top — treat Hopkins' own self-reported figures as illustrative, not audited |
