# John Caples — Tested Advertising Methods, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of John Caples' *Tested Advertising Methods* (5th edition, revised by Fred E. Hahn), built from the user's own copy. This is a currently-copyrighted book — paraphrase is mandatory throughout: no verbatim reproduction of his headline examples or case studies beyond short quoted fragments, direct quotes under 15 words and rare. Caples ran split tests on headlines and copy for decades at BBDO (he wrote "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano") and this book is the accumulated, testable result — the direct ancestor of the specificity/testing material in Hopkins and Starch already in this skill, but focused entirely on what actually WON in head-to-head tests.

Use this alongside `legends-library.md` Volume 4 (the short summary — this file is the full treatment) and `starch-advertising-principles.md` (the historical testing-methodology companion).

## 1. The New Advertising Strategy, the Most Important Part of an Ad, and Headline Rules

### Chapter 1 — The New Advertising Strategy

Caples opens on a hard split he saw across the whole industry: advertisers who test, and advertisers who don't. The testers — mail-order sellers, catalog houses, department stores, anyone whose response is countable — treat every ad as a hypothesis to be proven against real orders. The non-testers run on opinion: what the client likes, what the art director thinks looks modern, what a committee voted was "tasteful." His argument is not that untested advertising never works — plenty of it does — but that nobody running it actually knows *why*, and so nobody can reliably repeat the win. He credits the raw discipline to earlier mail-order thinking (he quotes a contemporary's line that the only justification for advertising is sales, sales, sales) and spends the rest of the book trying to drag general/brand advertising toward the same standard.

He illustrates the danger of opinion with two stories from his own early career: an artist who wanted pen-and-ink sketches in furniture ads because they felt "modern," and a copywriter who banned headlines from perfume ads because "headlines would spoil the effect." Caples took both as settled rules until he started working with keyed, measurable mail-order copy — and discovered that most of what passed for advertising wisdom around him was really just someone's private taste, sometimes traceable to something as arbitrary as an art director's fiancée liking blue.

**Apply:** Before accepting a creative "rule" from anyone — including a past version of yourself — ask what test produced it. If the answer is a preference rather than a result, treat it as a hypothesis to check, not a constraint to obey.

His prescribed sequence for planning a campaign has three stages: first, strip out every existing opinion, theory, and assumption about what will work. Second, run small-scale tests of individual ads, mailings, or commercials against each other within a single medium to find the strongest one. Third, once you know your best performer in a given channel, expand the test to compare whole classes of media — newspaper against magazine against direct mail — against each other, so you're learning both which specific piece wins and which channel category is worth the investment. He's explicit that this groundwork takes real time, and that the time is worth spending before a single dollar goes into full rollout.

**Apply:** Treat campaign planning as three separable questions — which variant wins, which of the winners scales, which channel is worth scaling into — rather than one intuitive leap to a finished campaign.

The chapter's signature data point is his own finding that one mail-order ad, run in identical space in the identical publication with equally strong art and copy, outsold a competing version by roughly nineteen and a half times. The only variable was the appeal — the core promise the headline and copy were built around. Everything else about the two ads was comparable. Caples uses this to argue that finding the right appeal dwarfs every other decision in the ad-making process; he says he'd rather have a rushed piece of copy built on the correct appeal than a beautifully finished, expensively illustrated ad built on the wrong one, and that he'd happily spend eleven months of a twelve-month production calendar hunting for the appeal and leave only the last week to actually write the ad.

**Apply:** When results across supposedly-equal ads diverge by an order of magnitude, look first at the underlying appeal or promise, not the execution — headline polish, photography, and layout are secondary next to getting the core offer right.

He extends the same test-first mindset to every other decision an advertiser makes, not just the appeal: which media to use, what time of year to run, where physically to place the ad on the page or in the schedule, and which specific ad element (headline, subhead, illustration, copy block) is pulling its weight. Every element, in his framing, belongs in the ad because testing showed it worked — not because it looked right to the person approving it. He acknowledges some situations genuinely resist pre-testing (a single-run ad, a very small budget, or the long-term cumulative effect of a repeated slogan) — in those cases his fallback is to borrow conclusions from advertisers who *have* tested comparable situations, rather than falling back on personal judgment by default. He's contemptuous of the common failure mode where the final call on an ad campaign's "three W's" — where, when, and what to say — lands with whoever is most senior in the room, whether or not that person has any actual advertising expertise.

**Apply:** When a live test isn't feasible, don't default to whoever has the most authority in the room — default to the nearest available analogous data, even if imperfect.

Caples also lays out the practical mechanics of testing that make the whole philosophy actionable: track sales lift directly where you can (retail point-of-sale, before/after comparisons); use test cities or test regions to trial a new appeal on a subset of the market before a national rollout; and — his most durable contribution — the key-coded response. A distinct code appended to the reply address or coupon (department number, source letter, date) lets you trace every inquiry or order back to the exact ad, medium, and date that generated it, with zero ambiguity even if the same ad runs in a dozen places. He suggests using sequential letters (A, B, C…) for successive revisions of the same ad rather than arbitrary numbers, purely so a code on its own tells you how many iterations preceded it. He calls the key number one of the most important structural inventions in the whole discipline, on the order of what an entirely new diagnostic instrument does for medicine — because it converts "I think this pulled well" into an exact, comparable count.

**Apply:** Any time you run more than one version of anything — a subject line, a landing page, an ad — give each version its own trackable code from day one, even for a "small" test, so results are attributable without guesswork later.

On the relationship between inquiries and sales: Caples cites Daniel Starch's large-scale analysis of inquiry data across many firms and many years, which found that the ad pulling the most inquiries in a properly controlled test is *also*, in the overwhelming majority of cases, the ad that produces the most eventual sales — which licenses using early-stage response (coupon returns, leads, calls) as a fast, cheap proxy for the slower, more expensive sales outcome. He flags two exceptions worth watching for: an ad promising something free with no real qualification tends to inflate response with low-value inquiries, and a small population of habitual "coupon clippers" will respond to almost any free offer regardless of interest in the product. His fix is to keep any premium or free-offer element identical and equally prominent across every version being tested, so that response counts stay comparable to each other even if the raw numbers are somewhat inflated across the board.

**Apply:** Early funnel metrics (clicks, inquiries, leads) are a legitimate stand-in for revenue in a fast test — as long as the offer and its prominence are held constant across every variant, so you're not accidentally testing "who has the flashiest freebie."

### Chapter 2 — The Most Important Part of an Advertisement

Caples devotes a disproportionate share of the whole book — four of eighteen chapters — to headlines, and defends the imbalance directly: whatever the illustration does, most readers decide whether to engage with an ad based on the headline alone, exactly the way a newspaper headline decides whether anyone reads the story beneath it. He treats the headline as functioning like a telegraphic pitch competing for a reader's instant of attention against everything else on the page and in the surrounding editorial content.

He brings in Don Belding's estimate that the headline alone accounts for somewhere between half and three-quarters of an ad's total selling power, and Belding's observation that in an average big-city paper, a single headline is competing against several hundred news stories and dozens of other headlines and feature articles simultaneously — all in the same fraction of a second it takes a reader's eye to move down the page.

He then reaches for one of the book's most quoted case studies, credited to Bruce Barton: a correspondence-school ad that headlined a former student's earnings as a flat, large sum performed far worse than a revised headline crediting a smaller sum tied to a single early sale, made shortly after finishing the course. Caples' read on why: nearly every reader could picture earning the smaller, more specific amount for themselves; almost none could picture the larger, vaguer one. The lesson generalizes past this one ad — a benefit sized within the reader's imaginable reach out-pulls a benefit so large it reads as somebody else's story.

**Apply:** When quantifying a benefit in a headline, favor the number a typical reader can picture achieving personally over the single most impressive number you have — the credible, personal-scale figure usually outperforms the eye-popping outlier.

Caples reinforces the point with a matched pair of headlines tested against each other for the same book on English usage, same layout, same copy underneath — one asking readers if they're afraid of making mistakes, the other framing it as "do you make these specific mistakes." The second version, which implies there's a checklist waiting in the copy and invites readers to test themselves against it, drew substantially more response. His diagnosis: the winning version promises free, specific, self-diagnostic information and taps curiosity about how the reader stacks up against other people's blunders; the losing version merely signals "sales pitch incoming" without offering the reader anything to look forward to reading.

**Apply:** Where two headlines otherwise say the same thing, prefer the phrasing that implies a concrete, specific payoff waiting in the body copy over the phrasing that reads as a general pitch.

He closes the chapter arguing that headline-writing deserves the lion's share of an ad's production time, not the leftover minutes after the copy is polished — his own practice, once he started working with traceable results, was to spend hours or days getting the headline right, at which point the copy underneath tends to come quickly and naturally. His formulation: a weak headline dooms even excellent copy to go unread, while a strong headline makes the copy-writing job comparatively easy. He also gives a workflow caveat: if you don't yet know the product or offer intimately enough for the headline to come first, write a rough draft of the copy first to surface the real selling point, then build the headline from that discovered point, then rewrite the copy to flow from the finished headline. He cites David Ogilvy's account of testing over a hundred headline candidates on colleagues before landing on his famous quiet-Rolls-Royce line as evidence that even legendary copywriters didn't trust their first draft.

**Apply:** Don't leave the headline for the last ten minutes of a session. If the offer isn't fully internalized yet, draft the body copy first purely to locate the real selling point, then invest the bulk of your remaining time in the headline itself.

### Chapter 3 — Headlines That Attract the Most Readers

Caples works through ten historically strong headlines (several drawn from mail-order courses, an etiquette book, a hearing-aid ad, a real-estate training course, and a self-improvement offer) to extract what they have in common. Rather than reproduce his specific headline text, the underlying qualities he identifies are:

- **Self-interest** — a direct offer of something the reader wants, present in every one of the ten examples he analyzes. He treats this as by far the dominant quality.
- **News** — announcing something genuinely new (a new course, a new device, a new plan), present in about half his examples.
- **Curiosity** — making the reader want to know more, present in several examples but never carrying a headline on its own; he's explicit that curiosity alone succeeds far less often than it fails.
- **Implied ease or speed** — a suggestion that the benefit arrives quickly and without much effort (a number of days, a single evening, "free"), present in a majority of his winning examples and one he flags as a meaningful multiplier on top of the other three.

He walks through why self-interest so reliably beats pure curiosity: a curious reader will read on *if they have time*; a reader offered something they actually want will *make* time. He also notes the practical asymmetry that a huge fraction of any ad's audience reads only the headline and never gets to the copy at all — which means a headline built purely on mystery, with no logo or company name doing supplementary work, delivers zero message to that large group, while a self-interest headline delivers its core promise even in isolation.

He adds a fifth quality that cuts across the other four: **believability**. A headline promising too much, too easily, collapses under its own implausibility — and he flags specific figures (a stated number of days, a stated dollar amount) as the most reliable device for keeping an aggressive promise credible, because a precise number reads as evidence rather than exaggeration.

**Apply:** Score a candidate headline against all five qualities — self-interest, news, curiosity, speed/ease, and believability — rather than optimizing for just one. A headline with self-interest plus a believable specific number will usually beat a cleverer headline built on curiosity alone.

Caples also uses this chapter to warn against trusting the judgment of advertising juries and award panels over test results. He gives several reasons juries reliably favor the wrong headline: they read the copy before judging the headline (so an obscure headline gets "explained" to them, unlike a real reader who never gets that context); they're motivated to elevate the reputation of the advertising craft rather than to maximize a client's sales; they over-reward polished writing when blunt, valid language usually outsells eloquent, vaguer language; and they judge in a conference-room context where none of them are actually in the market to buy the product being advertised. He cites an exercise in which the same set of mail-order ads with known, verified sales results were shown to members of over a dozen advertising clubs, asked to predict which ads had performed best — and roughly half of those expert predictions were wrong. His conclusion is blunt: expert opinion without a test is roughly a coin flip, and should never be trusted as the final word over actual response data.

**Apply:** Never let a subjective "which do you like better" review override a real test result — and be equally suspicious of your own confident guess about which version will win, since expert intuition on this specific question has a documented track record of being wrong about half the time.

### Chapter 4 — Right and Wrong Methods of Writing Headlines

Caples flips the lens and analyzes ten headlines that tested as outright failures — pulled from ads for a public-speaking course, a business training course, a party-games book, a book of literary excerpts, and several life-insurance offers. His diagnosis of what they share: every one of them relies on curiosity alone with no self-interest payoff attached; none of them delivers any news; and a majority of them lean negative — dwelling on embarrassment, worry, or failure rather than offering an upside.

From the contrast between his ten winners and ten failures, he distills five explicit rules:

**Rule 1 — Lead with self-interest.** Make the headline promise the reader something they actually want. He calls this the most fundamental rule in the chapter and notes it gets violated constantly despite being obvious once stated.
**Apply:** Before finishing any headline draft, check whether a stranger skimming only that line would know what's in it for them. If not, revise before touching anything else.

**Rule 2 — Push real news hard.** If there's something genuinely new — a new product, or a new application of an existing one — put that news in the headline itself, prominently, rather than burying it in body copy.
**Apply:** A genuine "first" or "new" claim belongs in the headline verbatim, not saved as a reveal in paragraph two.

**Rule 3 — Don't lean on curiosity alone.** Curiosity paired with self-interest or real news is a strong combination; curiosity by itself is, in his words, rarely enough — and he calls this the single most commonly broken rule in the whole industry, citing issue after issue of contemporary magazines relying on mystery-only headlines that go nowhere.
**Apply:** If you strip the intrigue out of a headline and nothing self-interested or newsworthy is left underneath, add one of those elements or replace the headline.

**Rule 4 — Favor the positive framing over the negative one.** Where a headline could dwell on a fear, an embarrassment, or a worry, or instead on the relief or gain of avoiding it, choose the cheerful and constructive angle when you have the choice.
**Apply:** Rewrite a fear-based draft headline into its positive mirror (what's gained by acting, not just what's avoided by acting) and test whether it reads stronger.

**Rule 5 — Suggest a quick, easy path to the benefit.** Just be careful — per the believability point from Chapter 3 — not to push this so far that the promise stops sounding credible. He gives a direct cautionary example: a headline promising readers they could work less and earn more underperformed because, however universally desirable that outcome is, it read as too good to be true.
**Apply:** Pair every "fast and easy" claim with something concrete (a specific timeframe, a specific mechanism) that makes the ease believable rather than suspicious.

Beyond the five core rules, Caples lists thirteen additional field-tested pointers, condensed here:

1. **Write from your own buying motive.** Ask honestly what argument would actually get you, personally, to spend money on this product, then build the headline around that real reason rather than an assumed one.
**Apply:** If you can't articulate why you personally would buy the thing, don't expect a headline built on guesswork to convince anyone else.

2. **Don't sacrifice completeness for brevity.** A longer headline that finishes its thought beats a short one that doesn't say enough — he shows several successful headlines running well beyond a dozen words because the full idea needed the space.
**Apply:** Judge headline length by whether the idea is complete, not by an arbitrary word-count target.

3. **Avoid the "dead" headline** — language so stiff and formal it reads like a plaque inscription rather than something a person would say to another person.
**Apply:** Read the headline aloud; if it sounds like a corporate motto rather than a conversation, rewrite it in plainer language.

4. **Avoid the "too smart" headline** — wordplay so clever that the reader's reaction is admiration for the pun rather than any interest in the product.
**Apply:** If a headline's main effect is "that's clever," not "I want that," cut it regardless of how proud you are of the wordplay.

5. **Avoid the meaningless headline** — vague phrases that could be attached to any product because they say nothing specific about this one.
**Apply:** Test whether a competitor's name could be substituted into your headline without anything sounding wrong. If so, it's not doing its job.

6. **Signal useful information is inside.** Headlines framed as advice or instruction ("advice to X," "how to improve Y") pull readers who want that information regardless of brand loyalty.
**Apply:** Where the copy genuinely teaches something, say so plainly in the headline rather than only in the body.

7. **Put your actual hook in the headline, not buried in the copy.** Caples' example is an ad for an overseas-market advertising consultant whose one relevant detail (the overseas-market service) appeared only in the last line of body copy — meaning readers who wanted exactly that service would never discover it, because the headline never signaled it.
**Apply:** Identify the single most specific, targeting detail in your copy and check that it — not a generic framing around it — is what appears in the headline.

8. **Layer curiosity on top of self-interest, not instead of it.** He shows how a plain self-interest headline can be strengthened by adding an unexplained twist (an accident, a strange event) that makes the reader want the mechanism explained, while the underlying benefit promise stays intact.
**Apply:** Once you have a solid self-interest headline, try adding one unexplained, curiosity-generating detail about *how* the benefit was achieved — without removing the benefit itself.

9. **A pure statement of fact, with no offer, rarely pulls readers into the copy** — because the reader can already predict exactly what a manufacturer-name-dropping fact-headline is going to say next ("use our brand"), so there's no reason to read on. He notes this style still has some value purely for readers who only ever see the headline.
**Apply:** If a headline is simply a factual claim ending in a brand name, ask whether a reader can already guess the rest of the ad — if so, it won't earn a read past the headline.

10. **Remember the logotype is functionally part of the headline.** The same headline can carry entirely different meanings depending on which company's name sits beneath it, since readers instinctively read the two together.
**Apply:** Draft the headline and the accompanying brand/logo placement as one unit, and sanity-check what the headline means once the reader's eye reaches the company name.

11. **The illustration does the same disambiguating work as the logotype.** A headline that's ambiguous on its own can be made completely clear by pairing it with the right image — his example is a two-word headline about overweight men that only makes sense next to a picture of a reducing belt.
**Apply:** If a headline is short enough to be ambiguous in isolation, make sure the accompanying image resolves the ambiguity rather than adding a second one.

12. **Avoid the "hard-to-grasp" headline** that requires real thought to parse — readers give an ad a single involuntary glance, not a moment of analysis, and will simply move on rather than puzzle out an obscure line.
**Apply:** If a headline needs to be read twice to understand, it has already lost most of its audience — simplify it until the meaning lands on the first pass.

13. **Never run an ad with no headline at all**, on the theory that it looks sophisticated or modern. Caples compares this to opening a shop with no sign — a handful of passersby might wander in, but most of the potential audience never learns what's being sold. He allows one narrow exception: an unmistakably strong, self-explanatory product photograph (his example is a beautiful shot of the product itself with the brand name beneath it) can occasionally substitute for headline copy.
**Apply:** Default to always running a headline; treat "the visual speaks for itself" as a rare exception you have to justify, not a routine option.

Caples also gives concrete craft advice on *producing* headlines, not just judging them. Write far more candidates than you think you need — a dozen at minimum, twenty-five if time allows — because the odds of landing a genuinely strong headline rise with volume, the same way a contestant submitting many entries in a contest improves their odds over someone submitting one. Let the list sit overnight if possible, then review it fresh, deliberately trying to adopt the mindset of a distracted, uninterested reader flipping past your ad rather than the mindset of the person who wrote it. Before finalizing, show the shortlist to someone who has never seen it before and trust their fresh reaction over your own — you're too close to your own draft to catch an unintended double meaning or an ambiguity that would be obvious to someone encountering it cold; he gives an example of a line about a Biblical hero "dropping" his defeated opponent that unintentionally conjured a completely different mental picture than the writer intended.

**Apply:** Generate a batch of headline candidates (aim for a dozen-plus) rather than polishing your first idea, sleep on the shortlist, and get a genuinely fresh pair of eyes on the finalist before you commit — your own familiarity with the draft is actively working against your ability to judge it.

Finally, on handling headlines too long to set in one uniform size: Caples recommends against printing a long headline entirely in one flat size, which he says reads as a dull gray block with nothing to catch the eye — instead, emphasize the words that actually carry meaning (not just whichever word happens to fall first or last) in larger or bolder type, so that even a reader who only registers the emphasized words gets the real message. He shows paired right/wrong examples where the wrong version bolds throwaway words like "the secret" or "at last" while the right version bolds the substantive words like "taller" or "dry hair" that actually communicate the offer. He also stresses that this decision shouldn't be left entirely to a designer working from a typed page in isolation — the writer needs to be involved in choosing which words get the emphasis, since a designer optimizing purely for visual balance may bold a word for its shape rather than its meaning.

**Apply:** When a headline runs long enough to need visual hierarchy, put the emphasis on the words that carry the actual selling meaning — and make that choice yourself as the writer rather than delegating it to layout.

## 2. Thirty-Five Proven Headline Formulas, and Finding the Right Appeal

### Why a formula list works at all

Caples opens this chapter by pointing out that formulas already govern other kinds of commercial writing — the structure of a pop song, the beats of a screenplay, the shape of a television drama — so there's no reason headline writing should be exempt. The 35 formulas that follow are patterns he watched succeed repeatedly across decades of mail-order and retail advertising, tracked by actual coupon and sales response rather than by anyone's opinion of them. He's careful to frame the list as a springboard rather than a cage: if none of the 35 fits, that's a signal to invent a new formula or bend an old one into a new shape, not a reason to stop. He also makes a stronger claim than most headline advice does — that writing a strong headline is more than half the job of writing the whole ad, because a weak headline means nobody survives long enough to read the copy beneath it, no matter how good that copy is. The formulas apply equally to magazine and newspaper headlines, the teaser copy printed on a direct-mail envelope, and the opening line of a broadcast commercial.

Use this list alongside `schwartz-breakthrough-advertising.md` section 4 ("38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline") — the two are not duplicates of each other. Schwartz's 38 are refinements you apply to a claim you've already chosen, to make it feel bigger, fresher, or more pulling. Caples' 35 are structural starting templates — mostly built around specific opening words or offer types — meant to hand you a complete, usable headline shape when you're starting from a blank page. Reach for Caples' list first when you need a headline fast; reach for Schwartz's list once you have a rough headline and want to sharpen it.

### Group one: news formulas (headlines 1–8)

Caples leads with news-style headlines because he found they reliably pull attention and sales — readers are drawn to the announcement of something new even when they have no immediate need for it, a pattern he traces back to the proclamation-style advertising of the ancient world.

1. **Open with "Introducing."** Signal that what follows is a first appearance — a new brand, a new product line, or a genuinely new category — spelled out large enough that the announcement itself is the visual hook.
2. **Open with "Announcing."** A close cousin of #1, often stronger when the item being announced is an improvement or a new offering from an already-known company; it can also be recast as "[Company] announces..." or folded into a phrase like "an important announcement to homeowners."
3. **Use other words that carry an announcement feel.** Words like "Presenting," "Finally," "Just published," or "Good-bye [old way of doing things]" all borrow the same psychological jolt as "Introducing" and "Announcing" without repeating them verbatim — useful once you've already used the first two formulas in an earlier ad in the same campaign.
4. **Open with "New."** The simplest and most durable of the news words — used either as the entire opening beat or repeated across several claims in the same headline to stack up a sense of comprehensive change.
5. **Open with "Now."** A slightly softer news cue than "New," often used once the product itself is no longer brand-new but a fresh use, format, or availability is being announced (paperback editions, new routes, new time-slots).
6. **Open with "At last."** Implies that the reader has been waiting, whether or not that's literally true, and works even when the actual words don't appear — Caples notes you can get the same effect with "Finally" or with a headline built as an incredulous claim ("Imagine a car so precisely engineered..."). It can also be moved to the end of the sentence as a question: has a cure been found, at last?
7. **Put an actual date into the headline.** A specific day, month, or year injects urgency and news value even in copy that isn't strictly time-limited — "Beginning June 1," "One day only, Sunday August 6th," or a bracketed year dropped into an otherwise evergreen claim.
8. **Write the whole headline in news-report style**, as if it were a headline from the front page rather than an ad — discovery-style phrasing ("Discovered — a new way to..."), or a plain declarative statement of fact rather than a sales pitch. Caples highlights one real headline that layered four separate news cues into a single line ("Just published... a new booklet announcing a new series...") as proof that these formulas can be stacked rather than used one at a time.

### Group two: price formulas (headlines 9–13)

Caples treats price as one of the single most decisive factors in any sale, backing this with readership research showing that readers will skip past large display type just to find the small print where the price sits. He notes these formulas fit local retail and broadcast advertising more naturally than national magazine campaigns, where a single price can't cover every market.

9. **State the price directly in the headline**, especially when the price itself is the selling point — a surprisingly low number, or a number framed as a comparison ("would you spend $5 to feel like a million?").
10. **Emphasize that the price has been cut.** Show the reduction itself — a "was/now" comparison, a specific dollar or percentage discount — rather than simply stating a low price with no reference point.
11. **Lead with an unusually generous merchandising offer**, of the kind that may not be profitable on its own but is designed to get a new customer trying the product for the first time — an introductory multi-unit deal, a steep first-order discount, a small-dollar trial quantity.
12. **Lead with easy or deferred payment terms.** Where an installment or delayed-payment plan exists, put it in the headline itself rather than burying it in body copy — Caples' research found this converts otherwise-lost sales because it removes the immediate cash objection.
13. **Lead with a free offer.** The free element can be a trial period, a sample, an informational booklet, a premium gift tied to purchase, or some combination of these. "Free" doesn't have to be the literal first word of the headline, but wherever it appears it needs strong visual or verbal emphasis — unless you're deliberately testing a version where the offer is held back as a surprise.

### Two formulas built on giving the reader something before you ask for anything (14–15)

14. **Offer the reader information of real value**, structuring the ad like a helpful article rather than a pitch. Caples breaks this structure into three movements: pure information with no sales talk, information gradually blended with sales talk, and finally straight sales talk — the idea being that you earn attention with real content before spending it on persuasion. Practical-advice and "do you make these mistakes" style headlines fall into this bucket.
15. **Tell the reader a story.** People already buy magazines and books to read stories, so a headline that promises a story rides that existing appetite for free. Caples cites his own legendary example — a headline built around a first-person narrative of self-improvement — as proof that a story headline can both drive massive readership and, when the audience is chosen precisely (his memory-course headline names "memory" directly), also drive the sale itself.

### Group three: key-word formulas (headlines 16–25)

Caples' insight here is double-sided: certain opening words don't just pull readers in, they also discipline the copywriter, because a headline that opens with "How to," for instance, forces you to actually deliver a how-to in the copy that follows — which happens to be exactly what a reader expects when they see those words.

16. **Open with "How to."** One of the most reliable formulas in the whole list; Caples reports that magazine editors independently discovered the same pulling power for article titles that mail-order advertisers found for headlines — readers are simply hungry to learn how to do things they already want to do.
17. **Open with "How"** on its own, without "to" — used for a slightly broader claim than a strict instructional promise, often framing the ad as an explanation of a mechanism or a transformation rather than a step-by-step guide.
18. **Open with "Why."** Invites the reader into a causal explanation — why a product works, why a problem exists — and works especially well when the "why" taps into something the reader already suspects but hasn't had confirmed.
19. **Open with "Which."** Puts the reader into an active, comparative decision-making frame — asking them to choose among named options, or to identify themselves among a list of symptoms or conditions. Caples notes the symptom-list variant does double duty: it's inherently engaging because readers like checking themselves against a list, and it lets the copywriter cover several complaints in one headline so more readers find themselves included.
20. **Open with "Who else."** Implies that other people similar to the reader have already gotten the benefit, and invites the reader to join them — a template that leans on social proof without needing an actual testimonial.
21. **Open with "Wanted."** Borrows the urgency of a classified or recruitment ad; it makes readers curious about what's being sought and, used well, implies real scarcity or demand for whatever role or service is being described.
22. **Open with "This."** A small word that does real work: it makes the headline feel concrete and specific rather than general, and it visually points straight at the product or illustration next to it.
23. **Open with "Because."** Leads with a justification or a reason, which pre-answers the reader's skepticism before they've had a chance to raise it.
24. **Open with "If."** Extremely flexible — it can pose a conditional scenario the reader recognizes themselves in, or set up a near-universal claim of the shape "if you think you can't afford this, you haven't seen [the financing/discount]," which Caples flags as adaptable to almost any product with a price objection.
25. **Open with "Advice."** Framing the headline as free advice rather than a sales pitch lowers the reader's guard, because it doesn't ask them to buy anything up front — it simply promises useful information, with the sales talk allowed to enter once they're already reading.

Caples adds that these ten don't exhaust the supply of usable key words — he points to headline-style words found in ordinary newspaper headlines and nonfiction book and article titles (words like "plain," "common," "principles," "what every ___ should know") as an open-ended source of more of the same pattern.

### Group four: format and delivery formulas (headlines 26–35)

The final ten formulas are less about a specific opening word and more about the overall shape, length, or stance of the headline.

26. **Write it as, or in the style of, a testimonial.** This can be an actual quoted testimonial from a customer or reviewer, or simply a headline written in the first person as if a satisfied user were speaking — both borrow the credibility of someone other than the advertiser making the claim.
27. **Challenge the reader to a test.** Frame the headline as a diagnostic the reader can apply to themselves or their situation right now — "can your kitchen pass the guest test?" — which converts a passive claim into an active, personally relevant question.
28. **Use a single word as the entire headline.** Works best for small-space ads where a single well-chosen word can be set in large type and still carry the whole message — Caples points to decades-long classics that sold trusses and used-textbook buyback services this way, on the strength of one attention-grabbing word alone.
29. **Use a two-word headline** when a single word can't carry enough meaning on its own but you still want the same stripped-down, high-impact format — pairing a category word with a benefit or a distinguishing modifier.
30. **Use a three-word headline** as a further extension of the same minimalist principle, tight enough to function almost like a slogan while still stating an actual claim or offer.
31. **Warn the reader to delay buying.** Because nearly every other ad on the page is urging an immediate purchase, a headline that tells the reader to hold off — until they've compared, read further, or checked a competing claim — stands out by contradiction; Caples also recounts a real case where a "don't buy" billboard campaign kept drawing foot traffic to a trade-show booth for years after the original advertiser had stopped exhibiting there.
32. **Have the advertiser speak in the first person, directly to the reader.** "I'll train you," "I guarantee," "why I offer you" — this creates a sense of a personal promise or personal stake rather than a corporate announcement.
33. **Address the headline to a named, specific person or group.** Naming the audience directly in the headline — car owners, allergy sufferers, a specific income bracket, "men and women who want to retire on a guaranteed income" — does two things at once: it pre-qualifies the reader as belonging to the intended audience, and it immediately promises a solution to something that group already cares about.
34. **Phrase the headline as a question.** A direct question pulls the reader into answering it mentally before they've consciously decided to keep reading, and it works especially well when the question touches something the reader is uncertain or curious about.
35. **Lead with a hard fact or statistic as the benefit.** A specific, quantified claim — a percentage, a comparison figure, a dollar saving — carries more weight than an unquantified adjective, and can be combined with formula 26 by putting the statistic in a named person's mouth as a testimonial.

**Apply:** Treat this as a lookup table, not a checklist to work through top to bottom. Start from what you already know about the product and the offer — is there real news, a strong price, a story worth telling, a fact-based edge? — and pull the two or three formulas whose *shape* already matches what you have, rather than forcing an "Introducing" headline onto a product with no actual news, or a testimonial headline when you don't have one. When a formula produces a flat first draft, don't discard the formula — try a different key word from the same family (swap "How" for "Why," or "This" for "Because") before abandoning the structural idea entirely. And once you've found a formula that measurably wins for a given product, mine it the way Caples describes in the appeal section below: write several variations on the same winning shape rather than moving on to a completely different approach.

### Finding the right appeal: what an appeal is, and why it outranks execution

Chapter 6 opens with an anecdote from Fred Hahn's own early career: a supervisor told him that essentially all successful advertising rests on sex, greed, or fear. Hahn later expanded this to four categories, all of which — this is the point he stresses hardest — describe a benefit to the *reader*, never a boast about the *advertiser*:

- **Sex appeal**, understood broadly as love, affection, and friendship, not narrowly as the physical.
- **Greed**, meaning everything money can buy, material and emotional alike.
- **Fear** — either of losing something already held, or of failing to gain something hoped for.
- **Duty, honor, and professionalism** — doing right by the people you serve rather than what's in it for you personally. Hahn illustrates this with his own experience promoting a fire-safety educational film to fire departments: when he tested whether firefighters would rather receive a popcorn popper or a set of knives as a thank-you gift, the fire chiefs were furious at the suggestion that a safety program needed a premium at all — the appeal that actually worked was children's safety, not a gift.

Caples adds a warning here that recurs throughout the book: the most common reason advertising fails is that the advertiser is too busy being proud of their own product ("the world's best seed") to say what's in it for the buyer ("the world's best lawn"). He also flags that the old blunt-force money headline ("You can make big money easily") no longer works on a skeptical modern reader, who assumes it means grinding labor or a low-value pitch dressed up. The fix isn't to abandon the money appeal — people still want money as much as ever — it's to make the claim more credible: he contrasts a guarantee-sounding headline with one that inserts the single word "plan" ("how a man or woman of 40 can *plan* to retire in 15 years"), which promises a strategy rather than a result the advertiser can't actually deliver.

**Apply:** Before drafting any headline, name the underlying appeal in one sentence, phrased entirely from the reader's side of the transaction. If your draft sentence describes what the product *is* or what the company *did*, it isn't an appeal yet — keep rewriting until it describes what the reader *gets*.

### The list of consistently effective appeals

Caples supplies a working list of appeals that have kept producing results across the categories he tracked: making more money, saving money, retirement security, better health right now, health-care security, security in old age, professional advancement, prestige, enjoyment, easier chores, more leisure time, comfort, losing weight, and freedom from worry. Alongside these, he calls out several broader psychological pulls that show up across very different product categories: the wish to belong to an "in" group (sells everything from apparel to what to eat and where); the universal appetite for a bargain (used by everyone from discount retailers to luxury car dealers); the wish to be noticed and found attractive (cosmetics, swimwear, sportswear); and the wish to outdo the neighbors, which he notes is usually more effective when implied through the imagery and positioning of an ad than when stated outright — nobody wants to be told directly that the point of the product is to make the people next door jealous. He also singles out the money appeal as unusually flexible: a clothing manufacturer's best-performing claim was that better clothes could help command a better income, and a business-book publisher found "eight books that *can* help you make more money" outperformed the same claim with "will" in place of "can," because the softer, more plausible verb held up better under scrutiny.

**Apply:** When brainstorming appeals for a specific product, generate at least three candidates from this list before writing a single headline, and actively look for the ones that sound almost too plain — Caples' repeated finding is that the obvious, believable appeal usually beats the clever one that only sounds good in a meeting.

### The ranking method: how Caples actually found the winning appeal

Caples doesn't just assert that the appeal matters most — he shows the actual analysis that convinced him. He had run eleven full-page magazine ads for one client over a year and had coupon-reply counts for each. When he laid the eleven ads in order from fewest replies to most, they split cleanly into two clusters: a low-performing group topping out at 263 replies, and a high-performing group starting at 502 and running as high as 867. Going back through the ads, he found that every ad in the high-performing group had one specific, strongly stated appeal built directly into its headline — and no ad in the low-performing group had that appeal in its headline at all. Crucially, the losing ads weren't appeal-free; they simply used a variety of other appeals that happened to be the wrong fit for that audience and product. One losing ad had even featured the winning appeal, but only in a small subheading rather than the headline itself, and it still underperformed — which told Caples that having the right appeal in the copy isn't enough; it has to be carried by the headline specifically.

From this he generalized a repeatable method for any advertiser sitting on a set of results: lay the ads or mailers out in strict order of performance, then build a comparison checklist across every dimension that might explain the gap — headline and subhead wording, size, and placement; illustration subject, size, style, and placement; overall layout and color; copy length and typography; the offer itself, including how the reader orders; the ad's physical size relative to the page; the publication or medium; and where in that medium the ad ran. Whatever quality shows up consistently in the winners and is consistently absent from the losers is the lever worth pulling harder in future work.

**Apply:** The next time you have more than a handful of past ads, emails, or pages with measurable response data, run this exact exercise before writing anything new — rank by result, then diff the winners against the losers across headline appeal first, before looking at design or format differences, since Caples' own data shows appeal is where the gap usually lives.

### Be your own guinea pig: judging appeals by imagining yourself as the reader

Caples offers a simpler, faster gut-check for when you don't yet have test data: picture two competing headlines for the same product and ask which one would actually move you. His toothpaste example contrasts a market-leadership claim ("more people buy this than any other brand") against a minor packaging detail (a cap that can't be lost) — the market-leadership claim wins even if it's presented in dull black-and-white type against a beautifully illustrated version of the weaker claim, because substance beats polish. He's careful to note this isn't a fixed law, though: change the weak claim slightly (a cap that flips open easily instead of one that merely can't be lost) and aim it at an audience with arthritis or grip difficulty, and the "weak" appeal can suddenly become the stronger one for that specific reader — which is exactly why testing beats guessing whenever real testing is possible. He runs the same exercise with two business-school ads, where the version citing specific before-and-after income figures for real students beats the version speaking only in general terms about the value of training — reinforcing that concrete, specific claims consistently out-pull vague ones.

Because choosing between concepts is also a political problem inside most organizations, Caples adds practical guidance for surviving the internal approval process: always give decision-makers an actual recommendation rather than a menu with no opinion attached, since that judgment is the expertise they're paying for; never show a rough, unfinished-looking version next to a polished one, since laypeople can't mentally finish a rough sketch the way an ad professional can; never present versions with mismatched levels of polish or editing; and if a decision-maker insists on running the version you believe will lose, propose an A/B split rather than fighting about it — and whichever way the test lands, keep your response professional either way.

**Apply:** Before running any concept past a stakeholder, silently rank the options yourself as if you were the target reader, and be ready to state which one wins and why in a single sentence — that stated recommendation is what turns a design review into a decision instead of a debate.

### Ideas that sound good in the room versus ideas that actually sell

Caples devotes real attention to the gap between what impresses fellow professionals and what a headline test proves in the market. His clearest example is a business-school ad built around a beautifully composed letter from a wife to her underachieving husband, which drew unusual praise from other advertising people — and then flopped completely, because the actual target reader didn't want to be lectured by a fictional spouse even in polished prose. He pairs this with a second example: an investment-plan ad that opened with several paragraphs of abstract, feel-good language about everyone deserving "the good things in life" before finally stating any facts; rewriting it to lead with the facts immediately produced better inquiries and sales. The throughline in both cases is the same: what an ad says outweighs how elegantly it says it, and appeals that sound clever or literary in a conference room are not a reliable predictor of what will actually move a buyer.

**Apply:** Treat praise from colleagues or clients as a warning sign to test rather than a stopping point — the fact that an ad impresses the people who already understand the product tells you nothing about whether it will move someone who doesn't.

### Appeals that created ill will

This is Caples' most cautionary material, and worth separating out on its own. He collects several real complaints from ordinary readers — a household product's ad featuring a grinning face people found simply ugly, a slogan a woman found so meaningless it actively annoyed her every time she saw it, a household insect-repellent card showing a winking woman captioned "I'm wise, are you?" that a reader found made no sense at all. His conclusion is that confusion breeds active resentment, not neutral indifference: readers get genuinely irritated by slogans and captions whose meaning isn't instantly clear, and that irritation attaches itself to the brand.

He's equally clear that a few complaints on their own aren't a crisis. The right response is to use complaints as a trigger to go check actual results rather than reacting emotionally to them, and then to gauge how widespread the ill will really is before deciding whether fixing it is worth what you might lose by changing an ad that's otherwise working.

He also draws a sharper distinction that cuts against the intuitive assumption that being liked and being effective are the same thing. An artist he knew praised a toothpaste campaign's elegant Acropolis-themed imagery at length — and then, asked what toothpaste she actually used, named a competitor entirely. The lesson: people carry both a conscious reaction to an ad's craftsmanship and a separate, unconscious reaction that only shows up at the point of purchase, and the only real test of an ad is whether it moves product, not whether people enjoy or even remember it fondly. He goes further still, citing a toy buyer's rule of thumb for selecting bestselling dolls: the buyer sorted candidate dolls into three piles — strongly liked, strongly disliked (even "actually ugly"), and neutral — and bought only from the first two piles, because dolls nobody had any reaction to, positive or negative, reliably failed to sell. Caples uses this to argue that noticeability itself, even when it tips into being disliked, usually beats blandness — echoing the line he attributes to a well-known copywriter, that the worst thing an ad can do is go unnoticed.

**Apply:** When feedback calls an ad "ugly," "confusing," or "annoying," don't treat that as automatically disqualifying — check whether it's actually being noticed and whether it's actually converting before you soften or pull it. But when the complaint is specifically that people don't *understand* the claim, treat that one differently and fix it fast, since confusion (unlike mere distaste) reliably produces resentment rather than sales.

### The specific appeal versus the general appeal

Caples closes the chapter with a clean illustration of concreteness beating polish at scale. A used-car dealer first ran handsomely designed institutional ads with vague headlines like "better automobile values" and "long life at low cost" — attractive layouts, careful type, and almost no customer response. Switching to specific copy naming actual makes, model years, and real prices, styled closer to a mail-order catalog than a brand ad, tripled the number of customers who showed up. Caples' point is not that layout and design don't matter at all, but that they are a distant second to what the ad actually claims — an elegant ad with a vague appeal will consistently underperform a plain ad with a specific one.

He closes the chapter with a compact four-step process for finding and then exploiting a winning appeal: first, choose your candidate appeals for a reasoned reason, not a hunch; second, actually test more than one; third, determine the winner strictly from results, not preference; and fourth, once you have a winner, milk it — run variations of the same core appeal across every headline and every medium you use, the way a financial advertiser took one winning "end money worries" headline and produced several more headlines built on the identical appeal, all of which performed well.

**Apply:** Once you've identified a winning appeal for a product — through Caples' ranking method above or through simple A/B testing — don't retire it after one good result. Generate three to five headline variations that restate the same core appeal in different words or formats (question, "how to," testimonial, fact-based), and let those compete against each other rather than searching for an entirely new angle.

## 3. Tested vs. Untested Advertising, Enthusiasm, Mental Hazards, the First Paragraph, and Copy Rules

### Chapter 7: Tested Advertising Versus Untested Advertising

Caples opens on a hard problem: most advertising can't actually be scored. A handful of dealer comments trickle in, maybe a few consumer letters, and that's it. If sales rise, a dozen explanations compete with "the ad worked" — sales force, season, some unrelated shift in demand. All that long-lived institutional advertisers like Wrigley's, Ivory Soap, and Campbell's really prove is that sustained advertising builds a business over years in some general sense — not that any specific ad pulled its weight.

This split creates two very different kinds of copywriting careers. Writers on accountable, response-driven work — mail order, department stores — must produce sales or lose the job; their work is judged daily. Writers on unmeasured "general" or institutional accounts can go years producing mediocre copy that nobody can prove is mediocre, because nobody is counting anything. Caples makes the contrast vivid with a small scene: a mail-order copywriter hopes out loud for a rainy Sunday, because he knows from experience that bad weather keeps people home reading the newspaper — and therefore reading his ad. An institutional copywriter would never think this way, because no one downstream is counting returns closely enough for weather to matter to them personally.

He follows with an anecdote about a friend whose father was launching a travel agency. The father arrives not wanting advice but validation for a "big idea" already fixed in his head — reviving a nostalgic sailing-ship illustration from an old college Christmas card. He isn't interested that most air travelers wouldn't connect a ship to a trip abroad, or that a national magazine would waste nearly all its circulation on readers outside his one city. Caples uses this as a caricature — extreme but not rare — of how untested campaigns get decided: by a client's pet idea, sometimes resting on nothing sturdier than a decades-old keepsake, rather than by evidence.

Mail-order advertising doesn't get to work this way, and that's why it looks different on the page. Because a mail-order ad must do the entire selling job by itself and its results are always tallied, every proposed ad clears three self-interested critics — copywriter, account executive, client — any one of whom will kill a weak idea rather than risk being blamed for a flop. That triple filter is why mail-order and direct-response layouts converge on the same traits: bold, legible headlines, easy-to-read type, copy dense with sales points, almost no white space (mail-order testing settled long ago that white space is wasted space). General advertising, insulated from that same daily accountability, keeps recycling expensive decoration, vague headlines, and strained "clever" copy — the same faults appearing in print decades apart, because nothing forces a correction.

He quotes John W. Blake's observation that general advertising is too often "a structure of opinion and unproven theories" resting on no real foundation, and Blake's practical fix: build some reply mechanism — even a "send for our free circular" line — into any campaign so brand-level spending has something to be checked against. A real case study backs this: a soft-drink maker whose loyal customers were aging out needed new buyers, so the whole plan pivoted to sampling. Careful testing and pruning ("breeding" the schedule down to the survivors) cut the cost per coupon from $1.20 to 24 cents across several seasons, while net earnings roughly quadrupled over three years, ending on a stable lineup of 15 proven ads.

A second case study — a bank's poster test — applies testing to attention itself. Candidate ads were displayed as posters in bank lobbies, with observers counting how many people walked past versus stopped to read. Results held steady across branches, and the spread between ideas was startling: one savings ad stopped 9 people out of 1,000 passersby, a similar one stopped 34. Adding a named, recognizable endorser (several posters quoted U.S. presidents) roughly doubled attention value versus the same words shown unattributed — stripping the name and photo from a poster cut its attention value in half. Caples's lesson: attention-value isn't the only measure of a good ad, but it's foundational. An ad nobody stops to read can't do anything for you, however good its argument is once someone reaches it.

The chapter closes on what Caples calls the "Philosophy of As If": treat every advertisement, even a purely institutional one, as if it were a mail-order ad. Give it a mail-order-style headline, mail-order art, mail-order copy density, mail-order typography, and — above all — a personal reason for the specific reader to act. Do that consistently, he argues, and the "as if" tends to turn into a reality of actual response. His two closing lessons: find some way to test your own advertising so you actually know what's working, rather than guessing; and when hunting for ideas, don't imitate the fancy, untested ads whose results nobody can measure — borrow instead from the ads that are proven daily (mail order, direct response, department stores), especially the ones that keep getting repeated, since repetition itself is evidence of a winner.

**Apply:** Before shipping any brand or "awareness" copy, build in some form of reply mechanism — even a soft one, like a lead-capture offer or a trackable link — so the piece has to earn its keep against real numbers rather than internal opinion. When judging your own draft, ask which of the three mail-order filters it would survive: would a skeptical account owner, a results-accountable manager, and a paying client all sign off, or is it coasting on the fact that nobody will ever check?

### Chapter 8: How to Put Enthusiasm into Advertising Copy

Caples opens with a story about an unremarkable young man — the son of a friend, no particular sales training — who delivered one of the most persuasive pitches Caples had ever heard, about a biomedical stock. What made it work wasn't skill; it was total, unfeigned belief. He'd bought the stock himself, sold it to his own friends, and was sure it would double in months. Caples later learned the founder had personally evangelized his young brokers for hours until they carried more conviction than years of sales training could produce. His point: enthusiasm sells as hard in writing as face to face, which is exactly why so many copywriters find an ad's opening the hardest part to write — a "cold brain" problem.

He gives three specific reasons starting is difficult. First, the brain behaves like an engine — it performs best warmed up, and you always sit down to write it cold, which is like trying to climb a steep hill in a car that hasn't started yet. Second, writers already know the opening is objectively the highest-stakes part of the ad — if it doesn't hold the reader, nothing after it matters — and that knowledge adds pressure rather than easing it. Third, in shops where a creative director hands the writer a fixed headline and layout before a word of copy exists, that scaffolding can feel like a constraint rather than a help; Caples notes the best writers treat it as a challenge to work within on the way to becoming creative directors themselves.

His fixes for cold-brain starts are practical rather than mystical. One: tell yourself explicitly that you'll probably discard the first few paragraphs anyway, and just start moving — the point isn't to write the opening, it's to get warm. Two: skip the beginning altogether and start writing in the middle of the argument; once you've warmed up, reread what you've produced, pull out the strongest paragraph, and use or rework it as your actual opening — repeating that scavenging pass three or four times as you go, letting the beginning assemble itself after the fact rather than forcing it up front. A third method, from a working copywriter he quotes: some writers simply don't enjoy the act of composing but are excellent editors, so they get anything down on the page fast, however rough, and treat the entire job as an editing pass afterward.

On burnout, he's blunt that it's real. He quotes a frustrated copywriter tasked with yet another "original" Christmas angle for the same cellular phone, having already covered every conceivable relationship permutation (business use, gifts between every combination of parent, spouse, child, and sibling) once a month for three years running. Caples's aside is sharp: writing three dozen ads for one product without ever testing which of them actually affected sales is exactly the kind of blind repetition that burns a writer out — there's no feedback telling you which angle was worth developing further. Some writers solve chronic burnout by changing jobs periodically. A gentler fix he recommends: stop writing toward an imaginary "average customer" you've addressed for years, and instead write as though telling a specific friend about something you personally just discovered and are excited about.

He illustrates this with a genuine letter — an association president wrote a friend describing an exciting site visit to Montreal ahead of a convention there, full of specific, unguarded enthusiasm about the hotel, the food, and the logistics — and only afterward realized it was good enough to send to the entire membership as-is. Caples's diagnosis: it works because it was written in the excited state, immediately after the experience, before the feeling cooled. He extends this to speech as much as writing: describe something exciting the same day and the account lands with real force; wait a week and the charge leaks out of it. His practical advice is to carry a notepad or recorder everywhere, even into the bathroom, so a spontaneous idea gets captured before it evaporates or gets talked over by someone else's competing idea.

He closes the chapter with four short copy samples modeling genuine enthusiasm: a retirement-income pitch built around the relief of removing a lifelong money worry; a Wall Street Journal subscription ad framed as a first-person turnaround story; a fruit-shipper's sensory description of pears ripening on decades-old trees; and a weight-loss offer's confident, guarantee-backed promise. None are stylistically fancy — what they share is a writer who sounds like they actually believe what they're saying.

**Apply:** When you're stuck on an opening, don't force it — draft the middle of your argument first (the concrete benefits, the specific proof), then mine that draft for the sentence that already has the most conviction in it and promote it to the top. If you have a genuine flash of enthusiasm about a product while researching or using it, write that reaction down immediately, in your own voice, before drafting the "real" copy — it will usually read stronger than anything you construct afterward from a cold start.

### Chapter 9: How to Avoid Mental Hazards

This chapter is short but distinctive, naming psychological traps a writer falls into before a word is drafted. The first and biggest is knowing the copy will be judged by a whole chain of internal critics — copy chief, account executive, advertising manager, sales manager, president — plus, in the writer's own imagination, random strangers glancing at it on the street. Each critic has a different, often incompatible, notion of good copy. Trying to satisfy all of them at once is, in Caples's image, like aiming one arrow at a dozen separate targets — it can't be done, and trying produces copy sanded down to please committee taste rather than sharpened to move an actual buyer.

His prescribed discipline is to separate two passes completely. In the drafting pass, banish every one of those critics from your mind on purpose — ignore them, forget them, and write purely toward what you believe strong, selling copy should sound like, at real speed and with real heat: get "steamed up," push the copy with the force of "a runaway locomotive." Only afterward, in a distinct, cold-blooded second pass, go back and cut whatever will actually draw objections from the real critics you had to ignore to write it in the first place. The reason this order matters, in his framing, is that writing with everyone else's preferences already loaded into your head at draft time produces something as correct — and as lifeless — as a schoolchild's essay.

He identifies two further mental hazards, structurally identical to the critics problem: the running list of things you're not allowed to say about the product (legal or client restrictions), and the running list of things you're required to say (mandatory policy language). Both operate the same way on a writer's head — if you let the "musts" and "can'ts" sit active in your mind while drafting, they strangle the copy before it exists. His fix is the same fix: put both lists completely out of your head during the hot draft, and reconcile the copy against them only in the cold, second pass.

To get into the right state for the hot pass, he recommends a specific trick — a form of deliberate self-hypnosis. Tell yourself, before you start, that the product is the best in the world and will produce real results quickly, regardless of how measured you'll be about the actual claims later. The justification: enthusiasm is genuinely contagious, transmitting from writer to reader the way an infection spreads; you can't transmit a feeling you haven't first generated in yourself. His instruction for the draft itself: write fast and furiously, as though racing to catch a plane. Expect the first paragraphs to look unusable — keep going anyway, because momentum eventually produces real selling copy, and some of what gets written in that state affects the reader emotionally in ways the writer didn't consciously plan.

He draws a sharp line here between two registers of copy. "Reason why" copy works on the reader's intelligence — it earns rational agreement, the kind of nodding-along a well-argued case gets. But it's emotional copy that reaches deeper, into the parts of the mind where love, hate, fear, and desire actually live, and it's emotional copy that supplies the one ingredient reason-why copy lacks on its own: action. Caples is explicit that the two aren't competitors — skillfully combined, rational argument plus genuine emotional pull is what actually gets a reader up out of the chair and moving toward the store.

He closes with a memorable image about sequencing: you can tame a wild horse into something useful, but no skill can put life into a horse that's already dead. Copy is the same — a piece written in real, white-hot enthusiasm can afterward be tamed into something both lively and acceptable, but no editing pass can resurrect copy that was cold and dutiful from its first draft. The order is not reversible: heat first, discipline second, never the reverse. He backs this with Anatole France's claimed habit of rewriting every paragraph five times, quoting France's own observation that the first four passes "sound as if anybody had written" them, and only the fifth begins to sound distinctly like himself — the enthusiastic first draft is a starting material, not a finished piece.

**Apply:** Physically separate your drafting and editing sessions for anything that needs real persuasive force — write the first pass without opening the brand guidelines, legal must-say list, or stakeholder feedback doc, then do a second pass specifically to reconcile the copy against those constraints. If a piece of copy feels flat despite technically covering every required point, the likely cause is that the critics and the compliance list were open in a mental (or literal) tab while it was being drafted — rewrite it once, fast, with all of that closed, and edit for compliance afterward rather than during.

### Chapter 10: How to Write the First Paragraph

Caples starts from an old definition of advertising copy — "salesmanship in print" — and his complaint that many writers have quietly forgotten it, opening with throat-clearing that loses a reader instead of holding one. He makes the case with a simple analogy: someone walking into a store to look at a TV set already has specific, practical questions in mind — how many channels, what's the reception like, is there real stereo sound, and what does it cost. A salesperson who greeted that shopper with "this is an age of beauty, charm, style" would seem obviously unhinged — yet that's the literal opening line of a real, expensive magazine ad for a television set that Caples cites. He pairs it with a second real opening paragraph praising "the modern woman's" refined taste and disdain for anything less than authentic — a paragraph that names no product, offers no reason to keep reading, and functions as a wall between the reader and whatever they actually want to know, rather than a bridge toward it.

The practical fix he's seen work at the editing level: a copy chief literally deletes the writer's original opening sentences and tells them to start further down the page, where real selling begins — like a pitcher needing a few warm-up throws before the pitch that counts, with the copy chief as the coach who can tell where "warmed up" starts, a call the writer is usually too close to their own draft to make. Absent an editor, set the draft aside a day or two and reread it cold; you'll often find you can cut the original opening entirely without losing anything essential. He passes along one editor's mechanical trick: make every draft description literally begin "This is a book about..." then delete that stock phrase once written — the words after it are reliably a stronger opening than whatever you'd have written unprompted.

The heart of the chapter is a study Caples ran on Reader's Digest: he transcribed the opening sentence of all 35 articles in one issue, then repeated the exercise across further issues, specifically to see how professional editors solve the same problem ad writers face — holding a reader who has already been stopped by a headline or title. The same small set of formulas kept recurring:

1. **Interrupting idea** — a startling or odd statement that breaks through the reader's background boredom, illustrated by an article on odor control that opened on a pair of skunks displayed at a trade show.
2. **The shocker** — a more jarring opening, close cousin to the interrupting idea, illustrated by a stark statistic about national drinking habits that contradicts what you'd expect from daily observation.
3. **News** — an opening that reads like the lead of an actual news story, announcing something newly happening.
4. **Preview** — a sentence that compresses, in miniature, what the whole piece is about to cover.
5. **Quotation** — opening on someone else's memorable words rather than the writer's own; his example is Daniel Webster's remark that of all his possessions he would keep the power of words above everything else, since words alone could recover the rest.
6. **Story** — opening on a specific person, place, and moment, told as a narrative. Caples flags this as the single most common pattern by far — appearing in over half of the articles he sampled, despite Reader's Digest being a nonfiction magazine — and connects it directly to the fact that many of history's most famous ads are themselves built as stories.

Across all six formulas, shared traits stand out: dense with concrete facts, compressed and telegraphic rather than padded, specific rather than general, curiosity-provoking, and light on adjectives. A companion pass on a later issue reconfirmed the same six formulas were still doing the same work decades later — evidence the pattern isn't a fad but a durable fact about how attention transfers from a headline into a body.

When none of the six formulas fits naturally, Caples offers a near-inviolable fallback: continue, in the first paragraph, the exact thought the headline already opened. A headline that has stopped a reader has revealed something specific that reader wants, and the safest way to keep them reading is to keep feeding that want rather than pivot to something else. He cites several real mail-order first paragraphs that do this tightly — a stammering-cure ad whose short opening promises a fast cure via the writer's own recovery story; a piano-lesson ad compressing its whole pitch (no drills, no teacher, popular songs by ear) into two sentences; a civil-service-jobs ad packing seven selling arguments into three short sentences. Against these he sets a real automobile-oiling-system ad whose ornate opening paragraph buried its genuinely strong mechanical benefits many paragraphs later — benefits thousands more readers would have seen had they not closed the ad first.

From all of this he distills three explicit rules for a first paragraph, based specifically on mail-order results:

1. **Make it short.** A long opening paragraph discourages readers before they've really started.
2. **Continue the thought the headline already expressed.** Don't introduce a new idea in the first paragraph after catching the reader with a different one in the headline — that's how you lose the very reader you just caught.
3. **State the reader's most important benefit or benefits in a few words, immediately.** Benefits are what make people keep reading in the first place — what do I get, what will it do for me — so don't delay answering that question past the first paragraph.

**Apply:** After finishing a draft, find the sentence where you actually start telling the reader something they want to know, and check whether everything before it can simply be deleted — it usually can. Read your headline and first paragraph back to back and ask whether the first paragraph answers the exact question the headline raised, or quietly changes the subject; if it changes the subject, you'll lose the reader the headline just caught.

### Chapter 11: Right and Wrong Methods of Writing Copy

This chapter catalogs 19 distinct styles of copy Caples observed in practice, sorted into three tiers: 13 recommended, 3 to use with real caution, and 3 to avoid outright. The organizing question behind the whole list is the same one running through the rest of the book — which of these styles have actually been shown, through tested results, to sell, and which merely look or sound impressive.

**Recommended styles:**

1. **Straightforward copy** — a plain, unadorned statement of facts in the clearest order, no stylistic dressing; e.g. a personalized-stationery ad that simply states what you get and what it costs.
2. **Story copy** — opens on a human-interest situation resolving into an implicit "buy this product"; the old confession-magazine formula that anticipates countless later TV beauty and health commercials.
3. **"You and me" copy** — the seller speaks to the reader directly and informally, as a good salesperson would face to face; e.g. a mail-order letter selling mackerel fillets in a folksy fisherman's voice. If you can't fake that voice, record the actual product expert talking and use the transcript as a first draft.
4. **Imaginative copy** — heightens interest by describing the product experience in vivid, near-narrative terms; the marquee example is Bruce Barton's home-study-course ad, structured as an imagined cross-country trip meeting bank presidents, which ran unchanged for seven straight years.
5. **Factual copy** — piles up as many concrete facts as the space allows. Caples's own comparison of successful against unsuccessful retail ads found a direct relationship between fact density and sales — "the more you tell, the more you sell" — shown in a boots ad stacking one construction detail on another.
6. **Facts-plus-style copy** — genuine literary style paired with real selling substance, fitting for higher-end products; a Rolls-Royce ad's fast, clipped rhythm echoes the car's smoothness while every sentence still carries a concrete performance claim.
7. **Forthright copy** — increases credibility by candidly admitting weak points alongside strengths; a real-estate listing openly calls the property "neglected" and flags a sagging guest house before pivoting to genuine positives, reading as more trustworthy than uniform boosterism.
8. **Superlative copy** — bold, unqualified "best/greatest" claims, which work only when the writer genuinely has the facts to back them; shown via a collectors'-library ad and a garden-catalog ad, both anchoring superlatives to specific real detail.
9. **Signed copy** — a manufacturer or recognizable figure personally signs a statement about the product; a magazine-launch letter signed by its own publisher, written as a personal note, reportedly pulled about four times its own space cost.
10. **Title copy** — specific to mail-order books, where testing surfaces which titles reliably outsell others; short, three-to-four-word titles on a narrow set of perennial subjects test consistently well, a pattern Caples notes still shows up in modern bestseller lists.
11. **Teaser copy** — frames the ad as a challenge to, or near-rejection of, the reader rather than a direct pitch; e.g. a business-training ad headlined toward people who don't already consider themselves know-it-alls, working precisely because the framing is unexpected.
12. **News-page copy** — the ad visually mimics a real editorial news page while staying clearly labeled as advertising; good as an occasional change of pace but loses power if overused — one auto-accessories advertiser tripled coupon returns the one time he tried it.
13. **Competitive copy** — directly names and unfavorably compares a competitor. Caples flags this as the one category that flipped from "use with caution" in earlier editions to fully recommended here, reflecting a real shift in advertising norms; shown via a tire maker's side-by-side spec table against unbranded "mail order" tires.

**Use with caution:**

14. **Card copy** — an ad so minimal it could fit on a business card despite occupying a full page, like a jeweler's name and slogan in a plain box. Pleasant work, but historically unable to match the volume that denser, 200-word-plus mail-order copy produces.
15. **Clever copy** — attempts at cleverness usually land one of three ways: neither clever nor effective, with an obscure headline that repels readers; genuinely amusing to the few who read it end to end, while the tricky headline keeps that number small; or, rarely, both clever and persuasive (a self-deprecating cheap-suit ad whose joke headline still pulled real readership). Overall advice: avoid it — the odds run roughly a hundred to one against you, versus a hundred to one in your favor with plain, benefit-first copy.
16. **Humorous copy** — avoid for the same reason: only a minority of any audience has a strong sense of humor, and that minority splits into incompatible subgroups, so a joke landing for one reader falls flat or insults another — shrinking your effective audience. A rare safe example: a mild, low-risk cartoon-style tire ad.

**Not recommended:**

17. **Poetic copy** — ornate, self-consciously literary writing that showcases the writer's vocabulary rather than selling anything; a florid ad a copywriter wrote to sell his own services (which likely flopped), set against a plain, fact-dense promotion ad proven by being run for years.
18. **Affected copy** — overwrought, sophomoric language chasing an "intense" effect through extravagant phrasing rather than real thought, illustrated with a purple, adjective-saturated star-sapphire ad.
19. **Unbelievable copy** — claims exaggerated past what any intelligent reader could accept; advertisers leaning on this had, by Caples's time, largely been squeezed into the cheapest publications or out of business, illustrated with a stock-promotion form letter dense with capitalized superlatives.

He closes on the stakes of that last category with a story: a life-insurance agent spent ten years failing to sell one prospect a policy, only to have that same prospect walk in one day wanting to buy it — because he'd seen it in a printed ad, and print carried a trained-in credibility the spoken pitch never had. That inherited trust is exactly what misleading copy spends down: his example is a "free roller skates" offer aimed at children that turns out to require selling merchandise door to door first, creating a skeptic who, twenty years later shopping for a car, distrusts advertising a little more — making every other advertiser's job harder. Copywriters share a professional responsibility not to spend the public's trust in print for one short-term win.

**Apply:** Before finishing a piece of copy, check it against this list honestly: if it's leaning on humor, cleverness, ornate language, or a claim you can't fully back up, it's sitting in one of the discouraged categories regardless of how good it feels to write. Default to the plain, fact-dense, benefit-first end of the list (straightforward, factual, forthright) unless you have a specific, tested reason to reach for one of the riskier styles — and if you do reach for competitive or superlative copy, make sure you actually have the comparable facts on hand before you claim them.

## 4. Twenty Ways to Increase Selling Power of Copy, and Appealing to a Mass-Market Audience

This section covers three consecutive chapters of the book: the core twenty-item copy checklist, a shorter chapter on manufacturing drama for inherently boring products (plus a related mechanic — store coupon promotions), a thirty-two-item checklist specifically for maximizing inquiry volume, and the chapter on writing so a mass, non-specialist readership actually absorbs the message. Caples frames the twenty-item list as the accumulated, field-tested answer to "how do I make this ad pull harder," independent of any single headline formula or appeal — these are copy-level moves that apply once you already know what you're saying and are refining how you say it.

### The Twenty Ways to Increase Selling Power of Copy

1. **Write in present tense, second person.** Address the reader directly as "you" experiencing the benefit right now, rather than describing a hypothetical "he," "she," or "people" who might feel it someday. His paired examples turn a generic "people will enjoy a sense of security" into a direct "you enjoy a sense of security."

2. **Use subheads throughout long copy.** They let a skimmer absorb your story in outline form without reading every line, and they re-hook a reader who was about to stop partway through by giving them a fresh, specific reason to keep going. His skincare example uses four subheads that function as a complete mini-narrative on their own, separate from the body copy underneath them.

3. **Caption every illustration.** Readers are trained from childhood — by textbooks, newspapers, and magazines — to read the line under a picture even when they skip everything else on the page, so a picture without a caption wastes a nearly guaranteed reading opportunity; the caption should carry the brand name and the core promise, not just describe what's shown.

4. **Write in a simple, uncluttered style.** Every bit of effort a reader spends untangling sentence structure is effort not spent absorbing your actual idea, so the plainer and better-ordered the language, the more of the reader's attention reaches the message itself rather than getting consumed by the delivery. The ad's job is to spotlight the product, not the cleverness of the writer.

5. **Choose short, common words over long or Latinate ones.** Short words are read and understood instantly by virtually everyone, including educated readers, while long or "impressive" words slow readers down, and a reader who stumbles more than once or twice tends to give up and move on. As a practical bonus, short words are more space-efficient — useful when you're paying by the line.

6. **Lead with free information, follow with the pitch.** Open in an editorial, almost magazine-article voice that gives the reader something useful or interesting before asking for anything, because if the sales pitch runs first, many readers never make it to the part that would have hooked them. His business-school example opens like a feature story warning about a stalled career, with the actual offer arriving only after that hook.

7. **Write selling copy, not style copy.** Style copy leans on unsupported, flowery adjectives ("sculptured smoothness"); selling copy backs every claim with a specific, checkable fact ("made of olive and palm oils — no other fats whatever"). Advertisers who can actually trace their own sales results consistently gravitate to the second kind, which is itself a data point worth trusting.

8. **Arouse curiosity, but never fully resolve it in the ad.** Reveal enough of a situation to make the reader want the outcome, then withhold the outcome itself. He recounts losing his own interest in buying a novel after a review quoted so much of it that his curiosity was already satisfied — proof that an ad which gives away its own secret can talk itself out of a sale.

9. **Make claims specific rather than round.** An oddly precise number ("97,482 people") reads as documented fact, where a round one ("nearly 100,000") reads as a guess. His strongest illustration is a building-materials advertiser whose ad claiming a product tested "52.7% above" a quality standard drove strong, sustained demand — until industry pressure forced the figure to be softened to "over 50%," at which point response collapsed, showing that the specificity itself, not just the fact behind it, was doing the selling.

10. **Use long copy when your situation calls for it.** Every category of advertiser that can trace direct sales results — book and record clubs, correspondence schools, real estate, patent medicines — consistently uses long copy because they can't afford to run copy that merely reminds; he traces one mail-order seafood business from a 75-word handwritten note to a full package of letter, order form, and illustrated folder as its sales grew right alongside the length of its copy. The exception: when a picture alone can carry the entire sale, minimal copy (sometimes just a product name) outperforms an explanation.

11. **Write more copy than the space needs, then cut it down.** Copy reliably improves as it's compressed — the reduction from full page to half page strips out slack phrasing, and a version compressed to its tightest, most telegraphic form often ends up the strongest of all. He shows a retirement-income ad reduced in stages from three loose paragraphs down to one tightly packed sentence, each cut sharpening rather than weakening the pitch.

12. **Don't accidentally advertise the whole category.** A generic pitch for "owning a TV" or "enjoying coffee" sends an already-persuaded reader to any competitor's shelf; sell your specific brand's specific advantage instead, or fold both into the same ad. His example is a Jaguar direct-mail package built entirely around one particular car and the reader's relationship to it, never around the idea of car ownership generally.

13. **Apply mail-order rules to direct mail, with the letter's first sentence acting as its headline.** The same discipline around openings, subheads, and length carries over directly. The one direct-mail-specific trick is stuffing the envelope with several distinct pieces on different stocks (a teaser outer envelope, letter, folder, testimonials, clippings, order form, reply envelope) rather than one combined booklet — a reader who'd toss a single circular unread will usually still glance at each of several separate pieces before discarding them, multiplying your chances to catch interest.

14. **Calibrate overstatement against understatement.** Old-style, breathless copy ("easily learned, quickly grasped, right-up-to-the-minute...") has generally given way to plainer, more confident copy that can even reassure the reader it won't pressure them. The same restraint applies to testimonials — a modest, specific quoted letter reads as more credible than a headline boasting an outsized personal transformation — and to how a genuinely new feature should be introduced: stated plainly as news, not dressed up to sound better than it is.

15. **Drop slogans and taglines that are obviously untrue.** A claim like a candy being "on every tongue" collapses the moment a reader tests it against reality and reads as empty wordplay; a smaller, true claim beats a bigger, disbelieved one.

16. **Get real feedback, not polite feedback.** Most people soften criticism to avoid hurting your feelings, so instead of showing one piece of copy and asking whether it's good, show two side by side and ask which one is better — that framing forces a genuine comparative judgment instead of a reflexive compliment. Separately, work directly with the client rather than relaying copy through an account intermediary, since a message passed hand to hand picks up misunderstandings at every step.

17. **Never mention that a salesperson will call.** If a lead-generation offer is followed up with a phone call or visit, saying so in the ad itself can cut coupon returns by roughly three-quarters — readers who'd happily request information balk at inviting a sales call.

18. **Study how mail-order catalogs sell.** Large catalog retailers have spent decades refining a proven sales pitch for almost every kind of product; when stuck on how to sell something, see how a catalog approaches the identical category.

19. **Make every single ad a complete, self-contained sales talk.** Never assume a reader saw an earlier ad or will see a later one. His illustration is a financial-services rep who plans to pitch three strangers three different narrow benefits (income for a widow, college funds, a paid-off home) based on guesses about each one's situation — an absurd way for a live salesperson to work, yet exactly what an advertiser does by scattering different single arguments across different ads instead of packing every important selling point into each one.

20. **Close by telling the reader exactly what to do next.** After building attention and interest, don't leave the reader to supply their own next step — state the action directly, and give a concrete, genuine reason to act now (a price increase, limited supply) whenever one actually exists.

**Apply:** Treat this list as a copy-editing pass, not a drafting checklist — write your draft first, then run it against all twenty: cut any word a shorter one could replace, verify every claim has a specific fact behind it (not a round number or a mood), confirm the headline/subhead pair could be skimmed alone and still tell the story, make sure the ad doesn't quietly sell the whole category instead of your brand, and check that the close tells the reader what to do rather than assuming they'll figure it out.

### Bonus: Dramatizing Dull Products, and Store Coupon Promotions

Caples devotes a short companion chapter to the recurring problem of a genuinely boring product or service that still has to be made interesting. His consistent method is to borrow a narrative device from somewhere outside advertising and graft it onto the dull subject rather than trying to make the product's own attributes sound exciting. A statistics-analysis firm became the subject of a "Famous Wrong Guesses in History" series (his example dramatizes the doomed 1830s canal-building boom) rather than describing statistical services directly. Disinfectant brands borrowed manufactured medical urgency and even historical horror — one created the now-familiar "bad breath" anxiety around the clinical-sounding term for it, another ran a series built around plague imagery before introducing the product as science's modern weapon against germs. Cough drops got a Shakespearean "aside" treatment — letting the reader overhear what a character is really thinking, in his example a purchasing agent silently furious at a salesperson's cough — instead of describing the drops themselves. Paper towels and a chapped-hand lotion both used overheard or imagined embarrassment (workers complaining about cheap towels in a washroom; a woman hiding rough hands at a dance) rather than listing product attributes like softness or moisture. Cellophane got dramatized through an ironic comparison to nature's own "packaging" (a corn husk, a coconut shell) that flatters the mundane detail of transparency into an achievement. Even a grave vault was pitched through a completely unrelated physical-power image — a steam locomotive contained by steel — to dramatize simple material strength. And a beverage brand that had stalled out on straightforward nutrition claims turned its sales around only after switching to small human-interest scenes instead of just stating the product's vitamin content.

A related, more mechanical technique covered in the same stretch of the book is the small-space newspaper coupon used to pull customers into a retail store — a distinct, non-glamorous format that reliably works precisely because it removes friction: a clipped coupon in a pocket or purse becomes a physical reminder that's hard to forget, and it lets a self-conscious customer avoid a bit of awkward chat, since the coupon can be redeemed by producing it rather than asking for a discount out loud. Results improve further with clear restrictions and deadlines printed directly on the coupon (a redemption date, a one-per-customer limit).

**Apply:** When a product or offer feels inherently boring on paper, don't try to make its own features sound more exciting than they are — borrow a dramatic device from an unrelated field (a historical parallel, an overheard conversation, an ironic comparison, someone else's raw power or intensity) and use the actual product as the resolution or payoff of that borrowed drama.

### Thirty-Two Ways to Get More Inquiries from Your Advertising

Caples separates the goal of maximizing raw response volume from the goal of writing a generally more persuasive ad — some of these thirty-two moves genuinely make the ad better, and some simply increase replies as a mechanical side effect without improving the underlying pitch. They cluster into five practical groups.

**Surface the offer repeatedly, not just once.** State the offer directly in the headline (turn "How to retire on an income" into "Free booklet tells how to retire on an income"); make the word "free" (or an equivalent like "send no money") visually prominent and repeat it; restate the offer again in a subhead; show a picture of the actual booklet or sample — shrinking it to a thumbnail if space is tight, but having it professionally relettered so a tiny reproduction stays legible; and mention the offer once near the top of the ad in addition to describing it fully at the close, since the best-pulling ads typically do both rather than saving it for the end alone.

**Make the offer itself more attractive on its own terms.** Give a booklet or premium a genuinely compelling title, since it functions as its own mini-headline within the response mechanism; describe the offer in enticing, specific detail rather than a throwaway line; borrow credibility with a foreword from a recognized figure; include testimonials; and add a small extra incentive to sweeten an already-decent offer.

**Strip friction out of the act of responding.** Include a coupon, print its dollar value directly on it, and even work a bit of selling copy into the coupon's own text; print your address twice in the ad, since skimmers often miss it the first time; add a phone number, and — per this edition — a fax number, as parallel response channels; explicitly say "no obligation" to lower perceived risk; for sensitive offers, promise delivery in a plain, unmarked envelope; add a genuine urgency cue; and consider a postage-paid business-reply card, a fold-over coupon, or a free-standing insert format that lets the response mechanism be torn out and mailed as its own physical unit.

**Test and target relentlessly.** Test different offers against each other, separately from testing different ad executions; test which media actually perform for your specific proposition, then weight spend toward them; deliberately "skim the cream" by targeting the most responsive slice of a market rather than blanketing it evenly; test space size rather than assuming bigger always wins; remember long copy tends to outpull short copy for inquiry generation too; test seasonally, since response volume shifts meaningfully through the year; test position within a publication, since certain pages and positions consistently outperform others; and watch which ads your competitors keep re-running, since a mail-order advertiser's survival depends on repeating only what actually pays — their repetition is itself a signal of what's working.

**Track everything you run.** Key every ad — a department number in the address, or a short code like "RD-5" for a specific publication and issue — and keep a standing results file recording headline, size, cost, publication, date, position, and response count for every insertion, sorted by cost per inquiry from cheapest to most expensive, reviewed on a regular schedule so future budget follows documented performance rather than memory or guesswork.

**Apply:** Before adding any single inquiry-boosting tactic from this list, ask whether it also makes the ad more persuasive or only makes it easier to respond to — both are legitimate reasons to use one, but conflating them can lead you to over-invest in response mechanics (a fourth restatement of "free") at the expense of the sales argument that would have produced a better lead in the first place.

### Appealing to a Mass-Market Audience

This chapter is Caples' most concentrated treatment of readability, and it's worth reading as a companion to the Fog Index scoring method already covered in `sugarman-handbook.md` (Section 6, "Readability") — that file gives you the formula (sentence length plus percentage of three-syllable words, targeting roughly a fifth-to-eighth-grade reading level for a mass-market opening); Caples instead gives a series of head-to-head demonstrations of the same principle in practice, plus a few specific mechanical traps that a pure grade-level formula won't catch on its own.

1. **Simplicity is testable, not just a style preference.** Caples backs this with parallel evidence from outside advertising entirely: artistically ambitious films underperform at the box office more often than not, a broad-circulation general-interest magazine reliably outsells a literary one by a wide margin, and picture-driven tabloid newspapers have historically out-circulated denser, text-heavy ones. His point isn't an aesthetic preference for plainness — it's that plain writing consistently wins in head-to-head sales results.
   **Apply:** Treat a plain-language draft as the default hypothesis to beat, not a compromise — if a more "sophisticated" version doesn't test better, the plain one should run.

2. **Read the copy aloud before you run it.** This is Caples' own mechanical test, distinct from a Fog Index calculation: hand the draft to someone and have them read it out loud. Any sentence that makes them stumble — a question with no question mark until the end, a clause that needs re-reading, over-complicated punctuation — needs a rewrite, because a moment of reader confusion multiplied across a mass circulation is a real, if invisible, cost.
   **Apply:** Before finalizing long copy, have someone else read it aloud to you, cold, with no rehearsal — mark every stumble as a required edit.

3. **Distrust "style copy" that sounds impressive but is genuinely ambiguous about what it's claiming.** His soap example ("you'll find them always the same, all around the world, unchanging") could mean the product is sold everywhere or that it survives any climate — a reader can't actually tell which. He demonstrates the fix with three versions of the same $25,000-earner headline, where the plainest, least "written" version communicates the idea most directly.
   **Apply:** If you can't state in one sentence exactly what a line of copy is claiming, rewrite it in plainer language until you can — ambiguity that sounds sophisticated to the writer usually just reads as unclear to everyone else.

4. **Spell out words that have gone stale from overuse.** Terms like "exclusive" and "guaranteed" appear in so much advertising that many readers no longer register real content behind them — and plenty of readers never knew their precise meaning to begin with. His fix is to unpack them: "exclusive" becomes "cannot be obtained elsewhere," and "guaranteed" gets its actual replace-or-refund terms spelled out in the same sentence.
   **Apply:** Any time you use a heavily worn advertising word ("exclusive," "guaranteed," "premium"), immediately follow it with the concrete, literal fact that word is supposed to represent.

5. **Link consecutive sentences by echoing the previous sentence's closing idea at the start of the next one.** He shows the same two-sentence passage in two orders — one where the second sentence opens on a new subject, one where it opens by repeating the exact phrase the first sentence just ended on — and the repeated-phrase version reads perceptibly easier, because the reader's short-term attention is already holding that phrase when the next sentence begins.
   **Apply:** When two sentences feel disconnected even though the logic is sound, check whether the second one could open with the same words the first one closed on.

6. **Write for a plain, general vocabulary and don't assume shared cultural literacy.** His field-research anecdote — asking Ohio housewives door-to-door in the 1930s whether they knew what a "Pianola" was, and finding nine out of ten did not, despite it being a term he was certain everybody knew — is a caution against overestimating how universal even common-seeming words and references actually are. He lists specific words worth avoiding for a mass audience: "fastidious," "distinctive," "virtual," "veritable," "heritage," and phrase patterns borrowed from trade-journal or technical writing.
   **Apply:** Before finalizing copy meant for a broad audience, flag every word you'd only expect a reader with above-average education or specialized interest to know on sight, and test a plainer substitute for each one.

7. **Assume readers take images completely literally.** Two of his anecdotes make this point: bystanders on a bus discussing a radio ad's speedboat photo as though it were an ad for the boat itself, and a woman unable to even sound out the word "bland" from an oil brand's sign. His conclusion is blunt: if you're advertising a radio, however powerful, show a picture of the radio — not a metaphor for its power — because a literal-minded reader will read the picture as an ad for whatever it literally depicts.
   **Apply:** Before finalizing a layout, ask what a literal-minded reader would say the picture is an ad for — if the honest answer isn't your product, change the image rather than trusting the headline to correct it.

8. **Avoid unnecessary contractions and misplaced exclamation points at the level of individual letters.** Contractions force the eye to register a small apostrophe or risk misreading one word for another at speed (his examples: "We're" can misread as "Were," "He'll" as "Hell"). Exclamation points placed immediately after certain words can visually fuse with the preceding letters — his example is a headline ending in "How!" that visually reads as "Howl" at a glance.
   **Apply:** Read finished headlines and short lines at normal reading speed specifically looking for accidental words formed by contractions or exclamation points — not just for meaning, but for how the letters visually cluster.

9. **Make sure the headline and the illustration tell the identical story.** His own cautionary example is an early ad of his own — a hair-treatment ad whose conditional headline claimed he'd be jailed if the product didn't work, illustrated with the advertiser literally shown behind bars — a colleague read it as mocking the client, because the unconditional image outran the conditional headline. Since a picture registers faster than a headline, any gap between what the image shows and what the words actually claim becomes the reader's real, first takeaway.
   **Apply:** Read your headline as a plain statement of fact and separately describe what your illustration shows as a plain statement of fact — if the two statements diverge at all, fix one or the other before running the ad.

10. **A headline can almost always be improved incrementally, and the improvement is directly testable.** He walks through a run of his own split-run pairs: adding a targeting qualifier ("Car Owners!" ahead of a fuel-savings claim) lifted response 20%; adding a concrete benefit word ("Dry Up" ahead of "Hay Fever") lifted response 27%; adding an age qualifier to a retirement headline improved both response volume and the quality of the leads it generated; adding a further concrete promise to a cooling-appliance headline strengthened it; swapping one word for a more casual synonym ("Fix" for "Repair") lifted orders 20%; and extending two separate book titles with a resonant closing phrase outsold the shorter original title in both cases.
    **Apply:** Treat your first headline draft as a baseline to beat, not a finished answer — generate at least one variant that adds a targeting qualifier, one that adds a concrete benefit word, and one that swaps a stiff word for a more casual synonym, then test them against the original rather than assuming which will win.

11. **Study how magazine circulation departments rewrite article titles for cover blurbs, and apply the same three edits to your own headlines.** They routinely shorten a wordy title for punch, lengthen a flat title by adding a payoff clause at the end, or fully reconstruct a title around a different angle on the same story — evidence, in Caples' reading, that a first-draft headline is rarely the strongest version available.
    **Apply:** Put a finished headline aside overnight, then deliberately produce three alternates the next day — a shortened cut, a lengthened version with an added benefit clause, and a fully reconstructed angle — before deciding which one actually runs.

## 5. Layouts, Small Ads, Brain Teasers, and Seventeen Ways to Test

### Chapter 14 — Layouts and Illustrations That Attract the Most Readers

Caples opens with an anecdote he clearly treats as formative: a trained art director who judged her own work by fine-art standards — gallery-worthy composition, illustrations styled after old masters — got nothing but polite admiration from other art directors, and prize ribbons at commercial-art shows. Out of curiosity she started showing her layouts to non-specialists — cab drivers, clerks, stenographers — asking which one caught their eye. They kept picking the least "artistic" option, the one that looked like ordinary mail-order clutter. Once she ran that comparison at scale, the pattern held: attention and persuasion don't track with artistic polish, and sometimes the two pull in opposite directions. His broader point: fine art aims to blend in and please the eye (he uses park-bench green as the example — chosen precisely because it recedes into the background); an ad's job is the opposite of blending in, so a layout artist optimizing for beauty is often optimizing against the ad's actual purpose. This is worth reading alongside `ogilvy-on-advertising.md`'s illustration rules and `bly-copywriters-handbook.md`'s design section rather than as a rival theory — Ogilvy and Bly both land on similar reader-first, plainness-over-polish conclusions from their own testing. What Caples adds that's distinctly his is the *falsifiability*: he frames layout choices as questions with testable answers, not aesthetic judgment calls, and several of the rules below are stated as findings from readership surveys and sales tests rather than taste.

**Type as a tool, not decoration.** Headline type has one job: be big and bold enough that even a careless page-flipper can't miss it. Body type has a different, opposite job: disappear into the reader's habitual reading experience. Caples' practical test is to use whatever type style the reader already reads most often — ordinary newspaper- and magazine-article type — and to actively avoid anything that calls attention to itself: script, heavy italics, unusually thin or bold faces, or a designer's trick of squaring off copy into a neat block with even line-lengths (which he compares to a memorial tablet). None of that reads *as* reading; it reads as design, and design isn't why people buy magazines and papers. His rule of thumb for anyone who can't name type styles: tear a well-performing mail-order ad out of a publication and hand it to the typesetter as the model to match.

**Size and boldness of a headline change how important it feels — even with no news in it.** Caples shows the same wording ("Announcing New Models") set small versus set in a full-width, oversized face, and argues the larger version reads as a real announcement while the smaller one reads like an apology. This works even on headlines with no announcement content at all — pure benefit statements gain a manufactured "news" quality purely from scale. The one guardrail: past six or seven words, full capitals become measurably harder to read for most adult readers, so long headlines should drop to upper-and-lowercase rather than staying in all-caps just for consistency.

**Feature the load-bearing phrase, not random words.** For long headlines, Caples' technique is to set one meaningful chunk — never an isolated word that means nothing alone — in larger or bolder type than the rest, and to place that phrase at the *start* of the headline whenever the sentence structure allows it, since front-loading the emphasized phrase reads more naturally than emphasizing something buried mid-sentence.

**Pictures with attention value, from readership surveys.** Independent of any product, certain picture subjects reliably stop readers: brides, babies, animals, celebrities, people in odd costumes or situations, pictures that imply a story (a girl trying on her mother's hat), romantic pairs, catastrophes, breaking-news images, and calendar-timely images (Santa Claus in December). A related, durable finding: readers of a given picture's gender tend to look longer at ads depicting their own gender, because the picture functions as a label for who the product is for — a subtler version of audience selection working through imagery instead of words. Caples uses this to warn against a specific mistake: a "bathing beauty" picture used to stop male readers for a non-related product creates desire for the woman in the photo, not for the product next to her — high attention, zero transfer.

**Attention is not the same as sales.** This is the hinge of the whole chapter, and the piece most worth separating from Ogilvy's and Bly's general design advice: a picture can score well on a readership survey and still contribute nothing to sales, because attention value and relevance are independent variables. His fix is a short, testable list of picture types that have actually been tied to sales results, not just eyeballs: the product itself, the product in active use, the reward of having used it (a woman admiring the cake she just baked), the attainment of an ambition the product enables (a graduate receiving a diploma; his own classic "extra $50" correspondence-school ad), an enlarged detail that a photograph alone couldn't show (a magnified pen nib), and a genuinely dramatic image *directly tied to the specific claim* (a blindfolded man for a memory course, illustrating the claim rather than just decorating the page). The failure mode he singles out is agencies who get bored producing the obvious, on-target picture (happy travelers on a cruise ship) and reach for something "clever" instead (a ship's compass, a captain's hat) — forgetting that to a fast page-flipper, a compass photo reads as an ad for a compass, and that the people who've actually saved up for a cruise want to see themselves in the picture, not admire a prop.

**Photographs beat drawings for believability, almost without exception.** Caples backs this with a run of small personal anecdotes rather than a survey number: a verbal description and even a formal crayon portrait told him nothing real about a friend's nephew, but one candid snapshot did; an idealized painted resort brochure conveyed no real information, while a competitor's photographs let him picture the actual trip; airplane-luggage ads with photos gave him confidence the product would look like its picture, while illustrated or painted versions left him unsure. His summary line, adapting an old proverb: if a good picture is worth a thousand words, a good photograph is worth two thousand.

**Faces are the most space-efficient illustration you have.** A head, especially one looking straight at the reader, earns outsized attention per square inch — you can crop away everything but the face and still have a full illustration, which a landscape or group scene can't survive being shrunk to. That makes faces the default choice whenever copy is long and the illustration space is small (his example: a 60-line mail-order ad where the "picture" is barely postage-stamp sized). Faces do double duty on testimonial ads specifically: showing the actual person who gave the testimonial signals that they're real and confident enough to be pictured, and lets the reader glance back and forth between the words and the face while reading, which he says deepens engagement with the message.

**The logotype belongs at the bottom, in size, almost by default** — brand-building is a long-haul investment that compounds with repetition, which is why broadcast ads substitute vocal repetition of the name for the visual logotype's job. Caples lists the real exceptions rather than treating it as absolute: the name is already in the headline; a package shot with visible branding does the job instead; the product is a one-time purchase (a book, a course) where there's no repeat-purchase brand equity to build, so the space is better spent on selling copy; or the ad is deliberately built to look like editorial content (a cartoon, a news item) rather than an ad, in which case a visible logotype would break the disguise and cost the readership gain that disguise exists to buy.

**Pictures are not mandatory.** Some of the longest-running, highest-performing ads in Caples' own experience carried no illustration at all — an all-type headline-and-copy block that simply out-pulled every illustrated alternative tested against it over years. His closing discipline for the whole chapter: every illustration costs real money in space, so hold each one to a specific question — does this picture add enough sales value to be worth what it costs — rather than assuming a picture is always an asset.

**Apply:** before approving any ad layout, run each element through Caples' test rather than a taste test — does the headline type read as bold news, does the body type disappear into ordinary reading, and does the picture (if there is one) depict the product, its use, its reward, or its specific claim, rather than something merely eye-catching and unrelated.

### Chapter 15 — How to Make Small Ads Pay

Caples starts from a counterintuitive mail-order finding: space size requirements aren't universal — they're a function of how much explaining and trust-building the specific product needs. A reducing belt sold profitably in tiny single-column ads because the mechanism is visually self-evident (a picture of a belt pulling in a waistline plus a few lines of copy says everything needed); a hair-growing treatment *lost* money in the same small format because overcoming skepticism about whether it actually works required a long, evidence-heavy case with testimonials that a small space physically can't hold, so it only worked at full-page length. A third category — his U.S. School of Music correspondence course example — sold profitably at every size from full page down to one column-inch, because the offer needed neither heavy trust-building nor much visual demonstration. The lesson before any format decision: ask how much *proof and explanation* the specific claim requires, not just what your budget allows.

**Ten limitations of small ads**, condensed: they don't impress your own dealer network the way a full page does; there's no room for a long dealer-address list; no room for color; no room for an appetite-arousing food shot or similarly rich image; they can't move real volume quickly; they can't project bigness or importance; no room for landscape or lifestyle group scenes; no room to properly show a big-ticket item like a car or a refrigerator; no room for space-hungry editorial techniques like comic strips; and they don't get the best placement positions in a publication.

**Ten advantages of small ads**, condensed: the same total budget buys many more insertions, spread across time (Caples flags the real caveat here — small space costs proportionately *more* per column-inch than large space, so the frequency gain isn't a straight multiplication); a series of small ads can rotate through different products or different uses of one product; the same money can spread across many more publications instead of concentrating in one; you can split budget between big and small formats for flexibility; small ads still support booklet/sample/catalog offers and mail-order sales; they still generate sales leads; they can buy contextually-relevant placement (a wedding-ring ad next to the engagement notices); they can earn outsized readership through editorial-style techniques (small cartoons, news-style items); and they're the only profitable format for narrow, "limited-market" products. His argument for that last point is the chapter's sharpest idea: for a product like a corn remedy, there isn't enough margin to justify full-page ads, and a beautiful full-page ad is wasted on a reader who doesn't currently have a corn — but you can't predict *when* a given reader's corn will start bothering them, so a small, cheap, one-word trigger headline ("CORNS") running in every single issue beats an occasional big ad, because it's there the moment the need appears.

**Making the copy pay in less space.** Caples' method is telegraphic compression — writing as if every word cost real money, the way a cable did. His example: "We will be glad to mail you a copy of our free booklet on request" compresses to "Free Booklet," sometimes just "Booklet." His production tip: if you don't have a small ad to write, write the long version first and cut it down the next day, the same discipline the testing chapter below applies to any first draft.

**Headlines for small space.** A single meaningful word that reaches the right reader — "Accounting," "Deaf," "Loans" — is often the strongest possible small-ad headline, purely because it can run in large type without eating space. His hard rule: never force a one-word headline out of a meaningless word like "If" just to hit the word count; meaning always outranks brevity. He also lists compact, information-dense phrase patterns worth stealing as a checklist: precise numeric claims ("$250 a Week"), specific timeframes ("Learn in 6 Weeks"), longevity/authority markers ("Our 36th Year," "Since 1893"), risk-reversal ("Money Back if Not Delighted"), and urgency ("Write TODAY").

**Should a small ad carry a picture at all?** Caples treats this as a real trade-off, not a default yes: illustration space is space you could instead fill with selling copy, so include a picture only if it's genuinely necessary to show the product or explain its use, or if it's small and directly relevant (a nurse's head for a nursing course) — otherwise drop it and use the space for either more copy or a smaller, cheaper ad.

**Two incompatible small-ad styles — pick one.** "Ad style" leans into every direct-response convention at once: a stopper headline, dense small-print copy, a jammed layout, aiming to sell in spite of visibly being an ad. "Editorial style" disguises the ad as a cartoon, news item, or article to earn a readership boost — but that disguise only works if you *also* drop every standard ad convention (one-word headlines, coupons); mixing the two styles announces "this is an ad" and forfeits the readership advantage the disguise exists to buy.

**Measurement discipline applies at small-ad scale too.** Key every ad so responses can be traced to ad, publication, and season, then reallocate spend toward whichever combination is actually working; use split-run testing wherever the publication offers it (see Chapter 17 below for the mechanics). Caples closes the chapter reassuring agencies and publishers that a shift to small ads doesn't shrink an advertiser's total spend — it just spreads the same budget across more insertions and more publications — and that small-space advertising is often how small advertisers survive long enough to become big ones. He backs the format with a run of classified-ad result anecdotes from newspapers promoting their own classified sections: single-line ads for everything from snow tires to a used dog pulling dozens of calls for a few dollars in space cost, illustrating classified advertising's core advantages of low cost, geographic and category selectivity, and durability as a format.

**Apply:** before writing off small-space advertising as underpowered, check whether the product needs heavy proof/trust-building (go big) or is self-evident and narrow-audience (go small, go frequent, and pick one word if one word will do the audience-selecting).

### Chapter 16 — Ten Brain Teasers

This chapter is structured as a quiz, and it's worth preserving the format because the format *is* the lesson. Caples lays out twenty real, sales-tested ads — ten winners and ten losers — grouped into ten matched pairs, one pair per product category (a business course, a hair-growing remedy, life insurance, a piano course, a retirement plan, a financial newspaper, a book-review magazine, a dancing course, a nerve-treatment program, and a literary book set). Each pair was tested under closely matched conditions — same offer, same general format, no artificial "Free" emphasis given to one side — with results tracked all the way through to actual follow-up sales, not just replies. He frames the exercise as if the reader is a creative director choosing between two submissions per account, checks the reader's picks against the real winners, and only then reveals which ad in each pair actually won and why — deliberately withholding the answer key until after the guess, so the exercise forces a real prediction rather than pattern-matching to a stated rule.

Rather than reproduce all twenty ads verbatim, the pattern across the pairs is more useful than any single one, and a few are worth naming for what they isolate:

- **The business-course pair** isolated specificity: a headline built around two concrete dollar figures beat a vaguer headline that only claimed the training "pays financially" — the abstraction lost to the number.
- **The hair-remedy pair** isolated confidence versus hedging: a headline and picture that instantly flagged the right reader (a visual and verbal cue pointing straight at a self-conscious bald man) paired with copy that told a results-oriented story beat a money-back-guarantee headline that, by leading with the refund, accidentally implied doubt about whether the product would work at all.
- **The retirement-plan pair** isolated clarity versus cleverness: a plain, benefit-loaded headline promising a guaranteed income beat a poetic metaphor ("a vacation that lasts the rest of your life") that was trying to be memorable but ended up obscuring what was actually being offered.
- **The book-review-magazine pair** isolated precise audience-selection: a headline phrased as a pointed question aimed squarely at people afraid of being caught out in conversation beat a broader, warmer-sounding invitation that read more like a literary-club membership pitch and left the actual benefit unclear.
- **The dancing-course pair** isolated story over boast: a first-person account of turning an embarrassing mistake into new popularity beat a comparative claim about being more popular than other kinds of dancers — the personal transformation story out-pulled the abstract superiority claim.

The meta-lesson Caples draws across all ten pairs is consistent: the winning half almost always combines a specific, concrete promise of benefit with precise audience-selection (through wording, a picture, or both), and uses curiosity only as a *complement* to a stated benefit, never as a replacement for one. The losing half is reliably vague, curiosity-only, cleverness pursued at the cost of clarity, or — in the hair-remedy case — accidentally undermines confidence in its own product. He closes by pointing out that several of the losing ads were written by copywriters with long, successful track records — the exercise isn't a trick, it's evidence that intuition alone, even expert intuition, is not a reliable substitute for testing.

**Apply:** before spending money to test two headline candidates for real, run them through this chapter's pre-filter first — does each one carry a specific, concrete promise; does it select the intended reader precisely; and if it uses curiosity, is a real benefit attached to it or is curiosity doing all the work alone.

### Chapter 17 — Seventeen Ways to Test Your Advertising

Caples frames this chapter as the payoff of the whole book: whatever headline, appeal, or layout rule you've absorbed, new questions will keep coming up as you write new ads, and the only reliable arbiter is some form of test, chosen to fit the stakes, budget, and time available. Seventeen methods, in his order:

1. **Sleep on it.** Set new copy aside and reread it the next day with a cold, outsider's eye — errors invisible in the heat of writing become obvious a day later. Writing several candidate ads or headlines instead of just one improves the odds that at least one is strong.
2. **Have someone else read it aloud to you** — not the reverse. Reading your own copy aloud lets you unconsciously fix its weaknesses with emphasis and pacing you know to apply; listening to someone else read it cold exposes exactly where a sentence is unclear (they'll stumble) or an idea doesn't land (they'll read it flatly). Any available second reader works — spouse, boss, colleague.
3. **Opinion test by interview.** Show at least two options and ask for a preference — never show just one ad and ask for a verdict, since most people will politely praise a single option shown alone. Where possible, interview actual prospects rather than a general sample (show food ads to people who cook, not a random crowd), and scale up through more interviews, hired focus groups, or multiple cities as the budget allows. Caples is explicit about where opinion tests mislead: people won't vote for an appeal that reflects badly on themselves in front of an interviewer (a "bad breath" appeal will underperform in a stated-preference test even if it wins in real sales); people assume a "real" ad needs a picture, so all-type ads are undervalued in opinion tests even though they can win real sales tests; people tend to reward "clever" ads in a stated-preference setting even when simpler ads outsell them; and a three-way comparison can flip a two-way result through vote-splitting (his example: a child-photo layout beats a home-photo layout 60/40, but introduce a second child-photo variant and the child vote splits into two 30% shares, handing the "win" to the home photo) — the fix is to test in sequential pairs, never as an uncontrolled multi-way vote. Despite the flaws, opinion tests remain valuable: fast, cheap, sometimes the only method a deadline allows, and a chance to surface ideas or objections you hadn't thought to include.
4. **Mail-order sales test.** When the ad carries an actual coupon or purchase mechanism, every response is a real sales data point rather than a proxy. Choose a visible coupon when you want volume and every reply has value (an actual order, a sales lead, wide sample/booklet distribution, or survey data); choose a hidden offer when you specifically want fewer or higher-quality replies.
5. **Coupon-plus-sales-call test.** For higher-ticket items too expensive to sell by mail alone (insurance, office equipment, home improvement, correspondence courses), the coupon generates a lead that a sales rep then closes — giving a double-check: track lead volume from the ad *and* the conversion rate of those leads, which can also reveal that some publications produce more leads but of lower closing quality than others.
6. **Coupon test for samples/literature, without follow-up calls.** For consumer categories where phone or in-person follow-up isn't practical (beauty, household, drug products, most travel), either pretest new ad candidates in one publication that's representative of the full list before rolling out the winner everywhere, or key every coupon permanently and track cost-per-reply across the whole list on an ongoing basis.
7. **Hidden-offer test.** Use a hidden offer specifically when you want fewer replies, want to filter out professional coupon-clippers, or want proof that people are actually reading the copy rather than skimming for a giveaway. Caples treats roughly 100 replies per test ad as the useful target — too few makes the result statistically shaky, too many wastes fulfillment cost on a test that didn't need that much volume. He lays out concrete levers, in both directions, for tuning response volume: to raise replies, make the sample free instead of paid, offer a full-size item instead of a sample, bundle in a bonus gift (his "Kit" framing — a Bermuda vacation kit, a painter's kit — repeatedly multiplied response several-fold over the bare offer), describe the offer's actual contents attractively instead of naming it flatly, shift from a daily to a Sunday edition, move to a higher-circulation or additional publication, or simply run a bigger ad; to lower replies, reverse any of the above.
8. **Standing mail-order test.** Build a permanent key or tracking code into every ad running in every publication. Even when the resulting mail orders don't cover the ad's own space cost, the ongoing comparison data — this ad against that one, this publication against that one, this month against that one, this position against that one — is worth more over time than the orders themselves.
9. **Telephone-response test.** Direct readers to call a number, ideally asking for a specific named contact per version, instead of mailing in a coupon — this produces fast, trackable, live data and can route straight into a sales conversation. To cancel out variables like weather, alternate the two ad versions across a matched run schedule rather than running one after the other. His worked examples: an air-conditioner ad framed around "coolness" beat one framed around "humidity" by a wide margin (concrete sensory benefit beat a more technical/dry framing of the identical mechanism); a loan ad with a specific hard-sell dollar figure beat a soft-sell, philosophical framing by the same kind of margin (people already in financial trouble want money, not a meditation on borrowing); and differently-named phone contacts in help-wanted classifieds let an employer trace which copy, or even which day of the week, produced better response.
10. **Confidential mail test of an appeal.** When you need to test something sensitive or competitively exposed — a genuinely new appeal you don't want rivals to spot in a public test market — buy a list, split it, mail two creative approaches with keyed reply mechanisms (postcards, hidden offers), and read the results privately instead of running a visible newspaper test.
11. **Opinion test by mail.** A mail-based substitute for the in-person opinion test in point 3: send prospects a batch of rough ad sketches with a cover letter (Caples' example uses a "Research Bureau" framing) asking them to rank preference and return a marked card, useful when in-person interviewing isn't practical at the needed scale.
12. **Transit/bus-card test.** Buy two half-showings on the same route so both versions get identical exposure, run a different appeal with a distinctly keyed reply department on each half, and compare response over a fixed period — a way to get controlled, split-style testing in a medium that doesn't offer literal split-run printing.
13. **Sales test in selected/matched cities.** Slower and riskier than it sounds: established-product sales continue with or without the test ad, results lag behind actual advertising by the time it takes inventory to move from factory to wholesaler to shelf, and no two cities are ever perfectly matched, so hidden local factors can quietly bias the comparison. Caples' precautions: test full campaigns run for months, not single ads run once; deliberately overspend or intensify frequency during the test window so any real difference shows up faster; and measure with direct field counts of shelf inventory rather than waiting on factory shipment data, which lags too far behind real consumer response. New products are actually a better fit for this method than established ones, since there's no existing sales baseline to separate from the test signal — his example is an inventor personally counting drugstore-shelf units week over week as he rotated different local newspaper appeals, until one appeal cleared the shelf.
14. **Cooperative sales test.** Partner with one retailer or one retail chain: you pay for and run ads under their name, they report the resulting sales back to you. This concentrates traceable results in a small, cooperating set of stores instead of scattering them across many independent retailers with no reporting relationship, making the comparison far easier to read — Caples flags that this kind of co-op arrangement is legally regulated and needs a compliance check before being set up.
15. **Readership test by interview.** A field-reporter method for estimating what share of a publication's actual readers noticed or read your ad. To be reliable it needs real refinements: never interview friends (they'll flatter you into a false positive), page through the whole publication and ask open-endedly what the person noticed rather than pointing at your ad directly, and layer on follow-up questions about depth of reading and resulting purchase intent. It needs a large sample and, in practice, a professional research firm to be trustworthy. Its advantage is visibility into how your ad compares against everything else — including competitors' ads — in the same issue; its limitation is the same gap flagged in Chapter 14: high noticed/read scores don't guarantee sales, since attention and persuasion are separate variables.
16. **Readership test by mail.** The mail equivalent of point 15: send recipients an extra copy of the actual published issue along with a marking pencil, and ask them to underline whichever ads and editorial items caught their interest, returning the marked copy in a prepaid envelope.
17. **Split-run testing in publications** — the method Caples treats as the strongest tool in the whole chapter. Mechanically, certain presses print two copies of a page per cylinder revolution; putting a different ad version on each side of the cylinder means the two versions are interleaved evenly across the entire print run, landing simultaneously in every geography, on the same day, in the same weather, in the same position, surrounded by the same editorial content — eliminating the sequencing, position, and weather confounds that undermine city-to-city or day-to-day comparisons. Because of that control, split-run results can be quoted as an exact percentage difference rather than a rough "about twice as good," and the method is sensitive enough to isolate genuinely small mechanical changes — Caples names four such tests worth remembering as calibration points: headline placed above versus below the picture, a smiling versus a serious model in an otherwise identical photo, a single added word in an otherwise identical headline, and the presence versus absence of a subhead directly above a coupon — each producing a real, measurable shift in response. For testing more than two candidates at once, he describes two tournament structures: a bracket, where pairs are tested against each other and winners advance to face other winners until one ad remains; and a faster control-ad method, where a single fixed "control" is split-run against a new challenger every single day, letting an entire batch be ranked against one stable baseline in the time it takes to run that many days of tests (his example: 36 different headlines ranked across four newspapers in two weeks) — with a caution that pulling power can gradually wear down over a long sequential test, checked by periodically re-running an earlier pair to confirm the percentage gap has held steady.

Caples closes the chapter — and the book's main testing argument — by naming four variables that any single result should be attributed to before drawing a conclusion: copy, media, position, and season, any one of which can swing results by a wide multiple on its own even when the ad and its cost stay identical. His summary claim is that advertising will never be an exact science the way chemistry is, because it deals with unstable, unmeasurable human minds — but that testing is what lets an advertiser act on facts instead of opinion, guards against a manager's or agency's pet ideas, and keeps pace with a public whose taste keeps shifting even when a formula worked well a few years earlier.

**Apply:** match the method to the stakes — reach for the free, same-day tools (rereading cold, reading aloud, a quick opinion test) on early drafts; use coupon, hidden-offer, or telephone-response tests once a direct-response campaign is actually running; and reserve split-run testing, with a control ad and a tournament structure, for any claim, headline, or layout choice significant enough to be run at real, ongoing scale.
