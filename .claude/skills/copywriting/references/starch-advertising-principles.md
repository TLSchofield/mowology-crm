# Starch's Measurement Method — Advertising: Its Principles, Practice, and Technique, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Daniel Starch's *Advertising: Its Principles, Practice, and Technique* (1914), written while Starch was an economics and psychology professor at the University of Wisconsin — a decade before he founded the syndicated "Starch Readership Score," a magazine- and newspaper-ad-recall metric the research industry still runs today. This book is where the method started: one of the first serious attempts to treat advertising as measurable rather than a matter of taste or instinct. Where Hopkins or Sugarman (`legends-library.md`, `sugarman-handbook.md`) assume you can test an ad and know which wins, Starch worked out *how* — recall tests, ranking tests, keyed coupons, validated against real sales and inquiry counts. He is the direct ancestor of A/B testing, readership research, and modern CRO analytics. Nothing here is verbatim; quotes under 20 words appear sparingly.

Use this reference when you need to **validate a piece of copy before it ships** rather than just write it well, when you're deciding **what to actually measure** in a test, or when you want the historical grounding for why "does anyone read this" is a testable question with a hundred-year paper trail.

## 1. The Three Functions: Attention, Interest, Response

Starch defines advertising functionally: "the presentation of a proposition to the people ... in such a manner as to attempt to induce them to act upon that proposition." He then breaks any ad's job into three chief functions, performed in sequence: **attract attention**, **stimulate interest**, **secure a response**. A fourth, optional function — being remembered — rides along with the first two.

This taxonomy is his spine for the whole book: size, contrast, and borders are attention devices; headlines and illustration are interest devices; argument, suggestion, and the coupon are response devices. He's explicit the three overlap, but keeps them separate for analytical clarity.

*Apply:* before touching a draft, name which of the three jobs it's currently failing. A headline nobody notices is an attention failure; a noticed headline nobody keeps reading is an interest failure; interesting copy that doesn't convert is a response failure. Each failure mode has a different fix, and conflating them wastes edits.

## 2. Attention and the Magazine Test — the Direct Ancestor of the Starch Score

Starch's first move is to prove attention-value matters commercially, not just aesthetically. He tracked every full-page advertiser in two magazines from 1890 and checked who was still advertising twenty years later: of firms whose ads had been rated "good" for attention-value, 49% survived; of "poor" ones, only 16% — roughly triple the survival rate.

Then comes the method that matters most here: Starch's "magazine test." He handed a real issue of *Cosmopolitan* or *Everybody's* to 374 people, told them to read or skip as they liked but turn every page of the ad section, then after fifteen minutes took the magazine away and asked each person to write down every ad they remembered. He corrected each count by subtracting how often the same brand appeared in an unrelated "which brands are you familiar with" survey — isolating recall due to the ad's *construction*, not prior brand knowledge. Old Dutch Cleanser was mentioned 219 times in the recall test and 88 times in the familiarity survey, for a corrected attention-value of 131.

This is the literal ancestor of the Starch Readership Score he would formalize commercially in the 1920s: put a real publication in front of real readers, under realistic reading conditions, then measure what was actually noticed and remembered — not what a copywriter or client *believes* is memorable. The insight that made it rigorous rather than anecdotal was the familiarity correction: never credit an ad for a reputation it inherited rather than earned.

*Apply:* when judging whether a headline or hero section is "working," don't ask people if they like it — show it for a fixed, realistic exposure time, take it away, and ask what they remember. Discount recall by prior brand familiarity, or you're measuring reputation, not copy.

(Starch also blames period factors — hastier reading habits, exploding circulation, more firms competing for the same eyes — for attention getting harder to win between 1860 and 1914; historical color, but the same curve that runs from print skimming to feed-scrolling.)

## 3. Headlines: the Vital-Point Test, the News Test, the Sieve

Starch reduces headline-writing to three questions: what size of type, how many words, and what to actually say. On the third — the only one that survives into digital copy unchanged — he gives three tested devices:

**State the vital point.** The copywriter's job is to find "the most dynamic idea in the whole advertisement" and state it tersely. He contrasts weak, generic headlines ("It Pays," "A Paying Proposition") against a sharp one ("Earn Your College Expenses") built around who the ad is actually for. His maxim: the headline should act as "a sort of sieve for sifting out only the potential customers" — a headline that filters for fit is doing its job even if it repels the wrong reader.

**Use the news factor.** A live, timely news element reliably outperforms static claims. A "deaf aid" ad rewritten around one headline, "The Deaf Now Hear Whispers," quadrupled a manufacturer's business within a year with no other copy changed; a knock-down summer-house maker's inquiries jumped from ~150/week to 1,000 in the first week after swapping a platitude for "Build Your Own House in Two Hours."

**Appeal to instincts.** Personal ambition, social standing, curiosity, economy, comfort — headlines that hit a fundamental human motive outperform generic description, the same instinct Hopkins and Sugarman independently arrive at.

One micro-case: a mail-order ad headlined "Cold Feet" underperformed for years, until an agency changed one word — "Cold" to "Warm" — with the rest untouched, and orders started landing immediately. The headline should state the *benefit the reader wants*, not the *problem the reader has*.

He also warns against **blind or irrelevant headlines** — the "shout something scary, then pivot to the product" trick from patent-medicine advertising. His objection is measurable, not moral: in repeated-exposure testing, irrelevant words *lost* interest value with each successive viewing while relevant words *gained* it — novelty wears off, relevance compounds.

*Apply:* draft the headline last, once you know the single most dynamic claim. Test it against three filters: benefit not problem, reads as news, filters for the right reader. If a competitor's name could swap in and the headline still works, it isn't stating your vital point.

## 4. Illustration: Two Schools, and When Pictures Beat Words

Starch frames a real debate of his time — "reason-why" copywriters vs. "atmosphere and pretty pictures" copywriters — and refuses to pick a winner, citing psychologist H.L. Hollingworth's finding that readers split into two durable cognitive types: some think in vivid verbal/visual imagery and need no illustration at all, while others "thought in terms of sound and movements" and need a literal picture to form any clear idea of the product. E.K. Strong replicated this with 30 women rating soap ads and found the same split, concluding the ideal ad should contain "both cut and reading matter."

Where tested head-to-head, pictures win hard: the only keyed ad copy with no illustration cost $1.47 per inquiry versus an average of $0.68 for illustrated copy — more than double. Starch's four legitimate jobs for an illustration: show the product's actual appearance, show it in use, show a specific selling point in action, or lend an atmosphere words can't. His one hard rule for action shots: show the figure in a *natural resting position* mid-motion (both feet grounded, not one floating) — an unstable pose undercuts whatever it depicts.

*Apply:* don't treat "should this page have an image" as a house-style default. If the audience or product is unusually verbal/technical (engineers reading spec sheets, in Starch's own example), heavy text can outperform a hero image. For anything else, default to combining both, and judge an illustration by whether it does one of the four jobs above — not by whether it looks nice in isolation.

## 5. Repetition and Cumulative Effect

Starch lays out two competing laws of attention: stimuli that never change lose interest ("the law of novelty"), but stimuli with no meaning also fail to hold interest — familiarity is what makes repeated contact meaningful rather than just tedious. He confronts a live argument of his day: W.A. Shryer's keyed campaign data seemed to show *no* cumulative benefit from repetition — a subscription campaign's average cost-per-subscriber rose from $0.85 on first insertions to $1.91 on repeats.

Starch's rebuttal: of course *diminishing* returns show up — the easiest prospects convert first, so each repeat exposure fishes a smaller pool. That later exposures still convert *anyone* is itself evidence repetition works; a truly dead stimulus would drop to zero, not decay gradually. He also notes Shryer's data came entirely from one-time-purchase offers with no reason to compound, and warns against generalizing a null result from a bad-fit category.

He also reports on recurring mascots — the Gold Dust Twins (since 1883), the Cream of Wheat chef — used by roughly 10% of national advertisers, combining familiarity (aiding identification) with novelty (the surrounding copy still changes).

*Apply:* when a repeated campaign element (a recurring visual motif, a signature phrase, a mascot) seems to be "losing steam," check whether the decline is genuine fatigue (dropping to zero, novelty exhausted) or just diminishing-pool decay (declining but still nonzero, because the easy converts already converted). Only the first is a signal to change the creative; the second is a signal to change the targeting or the offer.

## 6. Argumentative ("Reason-Why") Copy: The Four Steps of a Deliberate Purchase

For high-consideration purchases, Starch models the buyer's thought process as four steps, shown hitting all four in a well-built Old Dutch Cleanser ad: (1) **desirability** — establish it's worth having; (2) **comparison** — differentiate against competitors; (3) **means of securing it** — where, how, at what price; (4) **decision and action** — the explicit close.

His sharpest rule concerns concreteness. He shows a real piano ad with every generic, could-apply-to-anything phrase intact and the product name blanked out; a group asked to guess the product from the stripped text guessed washing machines, bookcases, stoves, and encyclopedias — proof the copy said nothing actually about a piano. His fix: **use words that arouse concrete mental images**, matched to the sense the product appeals to — taste words for food, sound words for instruments, touch words for shoes. "The Michigan Apple, the finest in the world" fails this test; describing its color and flavor passes.

He's equally blunt about superlatives generally (see §11): "the finest," "the best" are structurally weak because they're non-falsifiable and available to every competitor — a specific, checkable fact reliably beats an unlimited superlative, a finding backed by a direct split test where fact-based copy outpulled assertion-based copy roughly two to one.

*Apply:* run the "blank test" — delete the product name and ask whether a stranger could guess the category from what's left. If not, you're in generality, not argument. Then confirm the copy supplies all four steps somewhere in the funnel, rather than stopping at desirability and skipping straight to a CTA.

## 7. Suggestive Copy and the Ideo-Motor Principle

Starch's other copy mode is "suggestive" copy — minimal argument, mostly image and name, working by association. He ranks four escalating types: bare name display, name plus elegant/aspirational surroundings, name plus the product shown in desirable use, and the direct command ("Take a Kodak with you").

His grounding is the **ideo-motor principle**: an idea, once impressed on the mind, tends to produce the corresponding action without deliberate will — his proof is that you brush dust off your sleeve the instant you notice it, with no conscious decision. Applied to advertising: a bare command like "Don't Be a Pump, Buy One" outpulls a passive claim because it taps a response tendency that skips reasoned evaluation.

He doesn't claim suggestive copy is *always* superior — it fits well-known or inexpensive commodities best — and cites a real Colgate contest where 60,000 respondents split almost exactly 50/50 on argumentative vs. suggestive versions of the same ad: neither mode dominates categorically.

*Apply:* for high-familiarity, low-risk purchases (a repeat SaaS renewal, an impulse add-on), suggestive/command copy earns its keep. For anything requiring real evaluation (first purchase, high price, unfamiliar category), argumentative copy is the safer default — but test it; Starch's own data shows the split can go either way per audience.

## 8. Keying: Advertising's First Tracking Pixels

Long before UTM parameters, Starch documents four period methods for attributing a response to a specific placement: asking readers to voluntarily mention the publication (unreliable); a hidden alphanumeric code in the coupon ("T.W. 5-14" = *Technical World*, May 1914); a varied street address or middle initial per placement; a department letter appended to mailing instructions ("Dept. A," "Dept. B"). One mail-order operator built a letter-plus-number system tracking up to 2,340 simultaneous placements by hand.

He also documents an early split test: two identical half-page ads, one with a coupon and one with a portrait, run months apart to avoid carryover. The coupon version pulled 83 inquiries at $0.90 each; the portrait version pulled 41 at $1.83 each — double the response at half the cost, credited to the coupon's visual novelty, its directly suggested action, and the friction it removed (the reader only had to sign it).

*Apply:* this is the ancestor of a dedicated landing page per channel, a unique promo code per placement, or a UTM-tagged CTA per variant. If you can't attribute a response to a specific placement, none of the other tests here are runnable — keying is the precondition, not an optional add-on.

## 9. The Order-of-Merit Test: Ranking Appeals Against Real Returns

This is the book's other direct ancestor of modern split-testing, alongside the magazine recall test. Starch's "order of merit method": write several versions of an ad, each built around a *different single selling point*, strip out all brand names in favor of fictitious ones (to remove brand-familiarity bias), and ask at least 25 representative readers — not "which ad do you like," but "to which firm would you go first, second, third" to actually buy — to rank the full set.

His worked example used nine clothing ads, each built on one appeal (fabric/style, price, sweatshop-free labor, firm age, style, "success," cheapness, a generic "best in men's clothes" claim). Ranked by 120 men, fabric-and-style won decisively; the vague "best in men's clothes" claim came in dead last for telling readers nothing and "sounding boastful." Repeated on shoe, soap, and breakfast-food ads, the same pattern held: men and women, and students and farmers, ranked the identical appeals in meaningfully different orders — an early, explicit case for segment-specific messaging over one-size-fits-all copy.

Crucially, he validated the method against real outcomes, not just internal consistency. Hollingworth ran the ranking test against five real lathe ads from the Bullard Machine Tool Company and found the test-predicted rank order matched the actual order of inquiries received exactly — the top-ranked ad had also pulled roughly 40 times more inquiries in the field than the bottom-ranked one. Strong found the same near-exact match testing three General Electric ads. This validation step — does the panel's ranking predict real market behavior — is what turns "people like this better" into "this will outperform," the same logical structure underneath a modern A/B test with a holdout metric.

Starch specifies five conditions for trustworthiness: at least 25 respondents (he found more didn't change results materially); drawn from the actual target audience, not a convenience sample; fictitious names to remove brand bias; the question framed behaviorally ("where would you buy") not aesthetically ("which is the best ad") — the latter measures likability, not persuasion; and the pieces formatted as they'll actually run, since layout contributes to the real effect.

*Apply:* when comparing copy variants pre-launch, don't ask "which do you like better" — ask the behavioral question ("which would get you to click / buy / sign up"), anonymize the brand, use a real sample size (25 is Starch's own floor), pull from your actual audience, and finish in production-realistic formatting. And treat the panel result as a hypothesis to validate against real conversion data the first chance you get, not as a final verdict.

## 10. Choosing Mediums: Four Criteria

Starch reduces medium selection to four testable questions, still portable to channel selection today: **quantity of circulation** (verified reach, not the publisher's own claim); **quality of circulation** (does audience composition match your buyer — he cites *Atlantic Monthly*'s subscriber breakdown by profession as the kind of data to demand before buying space); **quality of advertising carried** (does the surrounding content help or hurt trust — a truthful ad loses credibility next to a snake-oil cure-all, an early statement of brand safety); and **time/frequency of issuing** (does the cadence match the buying cycle).

*Apply:* before buying into any channel, demand the same four answers: verified reach, verified audience match, an honest look at what else runs there, and cadence fit with your buyer's decision cycle.

**Historical color worth knowing, not applying directly:** Starch found ad attention-value scaled *faster* than size (a double-page ad was recalled ~4.5x as often as a quarter-page, not just 2x) and that small, occasional advertisers dropped out of a publication within a year while large, consistent ones stuck around for eight-plus — an early argument that under-resourcing a placement can doom a good offer. All dollar figures and rate cards in the book (a $10,000 *Ladies' Home Journal* back cover, $700 million in total 1913 US ad spend) are period color only, useful for scale, not for modern budgeting.

## 11. Honesty as a Measurement Problem

Starch's ethics chapter is a mechanical argument, not just a moral one: he catalogs pages of real superlative claims ("the finest," "the best in the world") running side-by-side in the same magazine, contradicting each other to the same readers, and argues this is *self-defeating as copy* — readers learn to discount superlatives on sight, so the claim carries zero persuasive weight even for the rare advertiser telling the truth. His three-tier taxonomy — harmless exaggeration, false implication, gross dishonesty — treats trust as a shared resource: one dishonest advertiser measurably lowers the tested pulling power of every honest ad running alongside it.

*Apply:* treat unfalsifiable superlatives as a testing failure, not just an ethics one — "best in the world" can't be measured, so it can't be improved, and Starch's own data shows specific factual claims consistently outpull it.

## 12. Problem → Starch Tool

| Problem | Starch Tool |
|---|---|
| "Is anyone actually reading this page?" | The magazine test — show it under realistic conditions, take it away, ask what's remembered, correct for prior familiarity |
| "Which headline is stronger?" | The vital-point / news / instinct tests — does it state the single biggest claim as news, aimed at one instinct |
| "Should this section have an image or more copy?" | Check audience type (verbal vs. visual thinkers) before defaulting either way; combine both when unsure |
| "Is this campaign losing steam or just running out of easy converts?" | Check whether response is decaying toward zero (real fatigue) or decaying but nonzero (pool exhaustion) |
| "This copy feels generic" | The blank test — delete the product name, see if a stranger can guess the category |
| "Which of these three value props should we lead with?" | The order-of-merit test — rank behaviorally, with fictitious names, against a real sample of the actual audience |
| "We can't tell which channel/placement is working" | Keying — a unique code, URL, or coupon per placement, per Starch's four historical methods |
| "Is this the right channel for this audience?" | The four medium criteria: verified reach, audience match, surrounding content quality, cadence fit |
| "This claim sounds impressive but tests flat" | Check for unfalsifiable superlatives; replace with a specific, checkable fact |

## 13. Why Measurement and Persuasion Need Each Other

Persuasion-first thinking — Hopkins' reason-why, Sugarman's slippery slide, the instinct for what makes a reader keep going — tells you what a *good* ad looks like. Starch's contribution is orthogonal: he tells you how to find out whether your good-looking ad is actually good, by turning "I think this works" into a testable claim with a sample size, a control, and a real-world validation step. A brilliant headline that nobody notices and a forgettable headline that quietly converts are both invisible to instinct alone; only measurement tells them apart. Used together, the two halves of this skill do what neither does alone: persuasion supplies the hypotheses worth testing, and measurement tells you, honestly, which of your best ideas were wrong.
