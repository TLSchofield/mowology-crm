# David Ogilvy — Ogilvy on Advertising, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), built from the user's own copy. This is a currently-copyrighted book — paraphrase is mandatory throughout: no verbatim reproduction of his rules or ad copy beyond short quoted fragments, direct quotes under 15 words and rare. This book covers running an advertising agency as well as writing copy; this reference weights toward the copywriting-relevant chapters (writing ads, print, TV, direct mail, research, the legends profiles) and covers the agency-management chapters (getting clients, running an agency, predictions) more briefly, since that's outside this skill's scope.

Use this alongside `legends-library.md` Volume 2 (the short summary of Ogilvy's overall philosophy — this file is the full treatment) and cross-reference Hopkins, Caples, and the other legends already in this skill where Ogilvy discusses them directly (he does, at length, in his chapter on advertising's founding figures).

## 1. Overture: Ogilvy's Core Philosophy

Ogilvy opens the book by nailing down what he thinks advertising *is*, before he tells anyone how to make it. He is explicit that he does not treat advertising as a branch of entertainment or fine art — he treats it as a channel for delivering information that changes buying behavior. His own benchmark for a piece of copy isn't whether someone calls it clever; it's whether it moves the reader to actually buy. He illustrates the distinction with a line about two Greek orators: one, Aeschines, drew admiration for his eloquence; the other, Demosthenes, moved his audience to march to war. Ogilvy wants copy that marches people to the cash register, not copy that earns compliments on its style.

**Apply:** Before shipping any piece of copy, ask "does this make someone act" rather than "is this good writing." Those are different tests, and the second one is a trap.

He addresses his own credibility directly. By the time he wrote this 1983 book, two decades had passed since his first book, *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), and he was aware that some in the industry dismissed him as out of date. His counter-argument is that advertising's *fundamentals* — the psychological and behavioral responses of consumers — change far more slowly than the industry's fashions do. He points out that concepts credited to his generation, like brand image, were really articulated by Claude Hopkins decades earlier, and that the "Creative Revolution" people credited to him and Bill Bernbach in the 1950s had precedent in agencies from the 1930s. His claim: most of what worked when he wrote his first book still worked twenty years later, because people still respond to promises of value, beauty, health, relief, and status — everywhere, across time.

**Apply:** Don't discard a technique just because it's old. If a persuasion approach is grounded in a stable human motivation (status, relief from pain, value for money), assume it still works until data says otherwise — the "it's outdated" objection is usually aesthetic, not evidential.

He's openly contemptuous of a certain style of practitioner: agencies and creatives who chase novelty, resist being measured, and use flashy, insider-approved work to win admiration from other admen while quietly failing their actual clients. He notes these campaigns tend to impress at industry gatherings in the big coastal cities but don't hold up under scrutiny in more results-oriented markets. His retort to this crowd is simply that his own track record of sales results outperforms theirs combined.

On the question of whether he's imposing rigid "rules," Ogilvy pushes back hard — he says outright that he hates rules. What he offers instead, he insists, are *findings*: observed, tested patterns in how consumers respond to specific stimuli. His examples: research telling him that celebrity endorsers under-perform at persuading people to buy, or that dark type on a light background reads better than the reverse. He frames these as hints grounded in evidence, not commandments.

**Apply:** When you cite an Ogilvy "rule" to a client or teammate, frame it as "tested pattern, worth defaulting to" rather than "law" — that framing is truer to the source and easier to override with better data if you have it.

He justifies publishing his methods at all with an analogy: an 18th-century English family of obstetricians achieved much lower mortality rates than competitors because they'd invented an improved forceps — and kept it secret for their own advantage, until a medical student spied through a skylight and exposed it. Ogilvy frames modern professionals (unlike those obstetricians) as obligated to publish what they learn rather than hoard it, and positions this book as his own act of publishing.

Two other framing notes worth carrying forward: he says explicitly that he only writes about what he's personally practiced (he skips subjects like media buying or advertising in Japan because they're outside his direct experience), and he pitches the book at people who don't already believe they've mastered the craft — both newcomers and veterans still looking to improve their results.

## 2. How to Produce Advertising That Sells

This is Ogilvy's densest practical chapter — structured as if he's talking a first-day copywriter through the whole process. The throughline: most advertising fails not because it's poorly crafted but because it's built on no research, no strategy, and no big idea — and he wants you to do the unglamorous work first.

### The stakes: not all advertising is created equal, and some of it hurts

Ogilvy opens with a warning most copywriters underestimate: the emotional or stylistic gap between two ads can translate into an enormous performance gap, not a marginal one. He cites John Caples' claim of having seen one ad outsell another by nearly twenty times, same space, same publication, same production quality — the only difference being which appeal was used.

More provocatively, he argues that bad advertising doesn't just underperform, it can actively *reduce* sales. He cites two examples: a Ford marketing-research test where people who saw the company's ads in a magazine ended up buying fewer Fords than people who didn't see them at all; and a beer study where people who remembered the brand's advertising drank less of it than people who didn't. His conclusion is blunt — the advertising industry has a shared incentive to pretend all advertising works to some degree, and it's a myth. It doesn't.

**Apply:** Never assume "any exposure is good exposure." An ad with the wrong appeal, wrong tone, or wrong claim can actively depress purchase intent. Test the message, don't just ship it because it's on-brand or well-produced.

### Do your homework first

Ogilvy is emphatic that no one earns the right to write persuasive copy without first doing tedious, unglamorous research on the actual product. He describes personally spending three weeks reading about the Rolls-Royce before writing a word, which is how he found the now-famous detail about the electric clock being the loudest sound in the cabin at 60 mph — that became his headline, backed by roughly 600 words of supporting fact. For Mercedes, his team spent three weeks in Stuttgart interviewing the engineers directly, and the resulting campaign of long, fact-dense ads reportedly helped grow US sales four-fold. For a margarine account, ten days of reading corrected his own mistaken assumption about what the product was even made from.

**Apply:** Block real research time before writing — reading trade material, interviewing engineers or the founder, reading customer reviews — even (especially) when you think you already understand the product. The best headline is often a fact you didn't know yet.

He also insists you study what competitors have already done in the category (successes and failures both, to get your bearings) and then go straight to consumers: learn their own vocabulary for the category, which attributes they weigh, and what promise would actually move them. If you can't afford formal research, he says informal conversations with a handful of real customers can outperform copywriting built on guesswork alone.

### Positioning

Ogilvy defines positioning, in his own compressed phrase, as **what the product does and who it's for** — and notes that despite the term's popularity among marketers, there was little consensus on what it meant. He illustrates with his own choice on Dove: he could have positioned it as a detergent bar aimed at men with dirty hands, but instead positioned it as a moisturizing toilet bar for women with dry skin — a positioning he says was still working a quarter-century later. Similarly, a SAAB with no distinct profile in Norway was repositioned as "the car for winter," and within a few years won a local award for exactly that. He also credits Bernbach's team for positioning the VW Beetle not as a car with a funny shape, but as an anti-establishment protest against Detroit excess — which turned it into a cult object for anti-consumerist buyers rather than a punchline.

**Apply:** Before writing headlines, force yourself to state positioning in exactly the "what it does + who it's for" formula. If you can't fill in a specific "who," you don't have a position yet — you have a product description.

### Brand image

Ogilvy argues every single ad functions as a contribution to a cumulative brand personality, made up of the name, packaging, price, ad style, and the underlying nature of the product — and that this personality should stay consistent year over year, even though internal pressures (new agencies, new marketing directors eager to leave a mark) constantly threaten that consistency. He argues most products benefit from a "quality" image — he uses the phrase of giving them "a First Class ticket" — especially for products that are visible to other people, like liquor, cigarettes, and cars, because a cheap-looking ad rubs off on the product itself.

His clearest illustration is whiskey: he argues people choosing between competing bourbon brands aren't really comparing taste, they're buying into distinct brand images, and that image accounts for the overwhelming majority of what a distiller is actually selling. He cites a University of California psychology study: subjects given plain distilled water rated it as tasteless when told it was distilled water, but rated the same water as unpleasant when told it came from a tap — purely from the associative image of "tap water." He extends this to a thought experiment: give someone the same whiskey twice, telling them it's two different brands, and they'll report a real difference — they're tasting the image, not the liquid.

**Apply:** Every asset — visual style, price point, packaging, tone of voice — is doing brand-image work whether you intend it or not. Audit for consistency across touchpoints; a great headline undercut by cheap-looking design is self-sabotage.

### The Big Idea

Ogilvy states flatly that homework alone won't make you famous or rich — you need a genuinely big idea to cut through and get attention, arguing that maybe one campaign in a hundred actually contains one. Even as a prolific practitioner himself, he claims to have produced perhaps 20 in his career that qualify. His view on where big ideas come from is almost mystical: he says they well up from the unconscious mind, in advertising as in art and science — but only if that unconscious has first been "fed" with real information; an under-informed unconscious produces ideas that are irrelevant rather than useful. His practical advice for unlocking it: load your conscious mind with facts, then deliberately disengage rational thought — a long walk, a hot bath, even a drink — to let the connection surface.

He tells two illustrative stories: dreaming of two horses pulling a bakery delivery wagon down a country lane, which became the enduring visual for a Pepperidge Farm bread campaign decades later; and a colleague's idea to parade live bulls through Merrill Lynch commercials under a "bullish on America" tagline — which Ogilvy personally thought was weak, but which had already been approved and went on to run for years, well past the original executive's tenure.

He credits Albert Lasker with the observation that humility in front of a good idea is a scarce and valuable trait — implying most people, including experienced admen, are bad at recognizing a great idea when they first see it. Ogilvy adds that formal research is a poor tool for evaluating a Big Idea, because research can't forecast whether an idea will still be working three decades from now.

He offers a short checklist for judging whether an idea is actually big: did it stop me in my tracks the first time I saw it; do I wish I'd thought of it myself; is it unique; does it serve the strategy perfectly; and could it sustain a campaign for years. Campaigns that survive five years or more, he notes, are rare enough to be genuine superstars — he cites the Hathaway eyepatch (running since 1951), Dove's "doesn't dry your skin" promise (since 1955), American Express's "Do you know me?" campaign, and Leo Burnett's Marlboro cowboy (running 25+ years at time of writing) as examples that kept performing across recessions, competitive shifts, and changes in personnel.

**Apply:** When evaluating a creative concept, run it against the five-question checklist above rather than a gut "I like it" / "I don't like it" — and don't kill a strong idea just because it feels risky or because research can't yet prove it'll last.

### Make the product the hero

Ogilvy's position: there's no such thing as a boring product, only writers who haven't engaged with it — and he says every one of his own bad campaigns traces back to a product he personally wasn't interested in. For "parity" products — commodity goods where competitors have equal access to the same manufacturing and research tools — he argues the honest job of advertising is to differentiate through clearer, more persuasive explanation of real virtues, and through the style of execution itself. He's unapologetic that this counts as legitimate "added value," not a con.

He also passes along a related idea from his partner Joel Raphaelson: instead of trying to prove your product is *better* than competitors', it may be enough to convince the consumer your product is *positively good* — because if the consumer feels certain about yours and uncertain about the alternative, they'll default to yours. This reframes advertising for parity categories: state your own honest merits clearly and confidently rather than attacking or out-claiming competitors.

**Apply:** For a commodity or parity product, don't force a false differentiation claim. Instead, write the clearest, most confidence-building statement of what's genuinely good about your product — clarity and conviction can outperform a manufactured superiority claim.

### Repeat your winners

Ogilvy argues a proven-good ad should keep running until it demonstrably stops selling — not until internal stakeholders get bored of it. He cites research showing readership doesn't decline across at least four repeated showings in the same publication. His memorable framing: you aren't advertising to a fixed audience that's already seen your ad, you're advertising to a constantly-refreshing "moving parade" of new prospects entering the market — a good ad functions like radar continuously catching new buyers as they arrive. He tells the anecdote of Henry Ford complaining to a copywriter that a campaign had run "forever," and the copywriter replying that, from the audience's perspective, it hadn't actually run yet — Ford was just tired of seeing it in internal meetings. Ogilvy's fix for that internal fatigue: measure selling effectiveness at regular intervals and let the data — not boredom — decide when to retire a campaign.

**Apply:** Don't rotate out a converting ad just because your internal team is sick of looking at it. Track performance on a schedule and retire creative only when the metrics say fatigue has set in, not when stakeholders say so.

### Word of mouth

Ogilvy notes that occasionally a campaign breaks into the culture on its own — a jingle becomes a hit song, a spokesperson becomes a talk-show guest — but is candid that nobody, including him, has found a reliable way to engineer this on purpose.

### Down with committees

He's scathing about committee-driven creative work. His view: campaigns loaded with too many stakeholder objectives and negotiated compromises end up accomplishing nothing, and he repeats the line that you can search every city park and never find a statue erected to a committee. He walks through why the process is slow by nature: brand managers and account executives have a structural incentive to prolong strategy debates because arguing about strategy *is* their job; research teams take months on simple questions; and copywriters, once they finally get to the desk, often spend more time in brainstorm meetings than actually drafting. He cites (with dry humor) that the average agency campaign takes about 117 days to produce — comparing that gestation period to a hyena's (110 days) and a goat's (151 days) to underline how absurdly long it is relative to the actual writing time involved.

**Apply:** Protect the copywriter's actual drafting time from process. If a "campaign" is taking months of committee debate before a word gets written, the bottleneck is organizational, not creative.

### Ambition and the pursuit of knowledge

Ogilvy pushes back on complacent copywriters who never consider that a harder swing could double a client's sales, urging people to aim explicitly at the "immortals" of the craft rather than settling for adequate work. He pairs this with a demand for actual expertise: quoting a royal surgeon's answer to "what makes a great surgeon" — not manual skill, but knowing more than other surgeons do — and applying the same standard to advertising. He's contemptuous of copywriters who refuse to study the craft's accumulated findings and rely purely on "intuition," comparing it to trusting a surgeon who never read an anatomy textbook with your gall bladder.

He credits George Gallup's research work at Young & Rubicam in the 1930s — where he systematically tracked ad readership and found certain techniques consistently outperformed others — with giving that agency a measurable performance edge once a sharp art director applied the findings. He describes similar research from other named researchers of that era: finished-dish photography reliably beat raw-ingredient photography, recipe cards reliably performed well with a female readership, and photos with a "story" element reliably outperformed static ones (which is what led Ogilvy to put an eyepatch on the model in his own Hathaway shirt ads). His broader point is that these performance factors have stayed remarkably stable over decades, but that much of the industry has simply stopped bothering to track and apply them — he notes finding dozens of ads in a single magazine issue still using light-on-dark reversed type, long after research showed it hurts readability.

He also quotes J. Walter Thompson's longtime head Stanley Resor admitting that despite spending "hundreds of millions" of client money annually, the agency had done almost nothing to systematically identify what actually worked until a late-career effort to do so — implying most of the industry still operates this way.

**Apply:** Build (or maintain) your own running file of tested findings — what wins and loses in your specific category/channel — rather than re-deciding from instinct on every project. Treat accumulated internal data as a competitive asset, the way Gallup's readership research was.

### The lessons of direct response

Ogilvy holds up direct-response advertisers as the most honest teachers in the industry, because they're the only ones who know, to the dollar, whether a given ad actually sold anything — everyone else is guessing amid confounding variables. He lists specific places where direct-response practice diverges from general-advertising convention and argues direct response is usually right: general advertisers favor short 30-second commercials, but direct responders learned two-minute spots sell more; general advertisers buy expensive prime-time slots, but direct responders make more sales running late at night; general advertisers favor short print copy, but direct responders consistently use long copy. His recommendation: every copywriter should spend their first two years in direct response, because he claims he can tell instantly, from any general ad, whether its writer ever had that grounding.

**Apply:** Before assuming "shorter is better" or "prime placement matters most," check what direct-response data says for your channel — long copy and off-peak placement often outperform intuition-driven defaults.

### Creativity vs. selling — the core tension of the chapter

This section is arguably the chapter's real thesis. Ogilvy states that his own most successful ad, by results, was an unglamorous, fact-driven piece for Puerto Rican industrial development — it won no creative awards but drove real investment to the island. He contrasts what people *like* in advertising (beautiful visuals, clever copy) against what actually sells, arguing these two things are frequently different, and that readers judging ads by their own aesthetic taste routinely forget to ask whether the ad would make *them* buy.

He leans heavily on a quoted passage from Rosser Reeves of Ted Bates, who challenges the reader directly: if your business and your family's future depend on the outcome, do you want "fine writing" and framed masterpieces, or do you want the sales curve to reverse and start climbing. Ogilvy pairs this with Benton & Bowles' agency maxim that if it doesn't sell, it isn't creative — endorsing it outright.

He's dismissive of "creativity" as an industry buzzword, noting the word doesn't even appear in the (then-current) Oxford dictionary, and quotes Reeves calling originality "the most dangerous word in advertising" because pursuing novelty for its own sake chases something as insubstantial as swamp gas. Ogilvy even invokes Mozart's claim that he never deliberately tried to be original. As evidence that industry-awarded "creativity" doesn't track business results, Ogilvy cites an analysis showing that of 81 television commercials honored as all-time classics at the Clio awards, 36 of the responsible agencies had since lost the account or gone out of business entirely.

**Apply:** When a stakeholder pushes back that an ad "isn't creative enough," reframe the question: is it selling? An effective, plain, fact-driven ad that moves the sales curve beats an award-caliber ad that doesn't — award juries and buyers are not the same audience.

### On sex, taste, and relevance in advertising

Ogilvy is candid that his very first professional ad — showing a nude woman — was a mistake, not because nudity is inherently wrong, but because it had no relevance to the product being sold (a cooking stove). His governing principle throughout this section is *relevance*: irrelevant titillation doesn't sell (bosoms wouldn't sell detergent), but functionally relevant nudity can work in categories like beauty products, where the human body is the actual subject matter. He also notes that advertising tends to trail broader social norms rather than lead them — pointing out that explicit language and sexual content appear in mainstream fiction well before they appear in ads, and that longstanding taboos (showing women smoking or drinking in ads) only lifted long after those behaviors were already normalized in public. He personally recalls being the first to depict women in liquor advertising, doing so decades after women had already been drinking in public.

He also touches briefly on humor and cultural sensitivity — he personally dislikes trends like using religious figures as comic props even where legal, but says he has no objection to crude scatological humor in the right cultural context, citing a Japanese commercial he happily awarded a top prize despite (or including) an off-color premise, because it worked commercially (he notes a real sales lift tied to it).

**Apply:** Test any provocative element (sex, edgy humor, taboo-adjacent content) against one question: is it functionally relevant to the product's actual benefit? If it's just there to grab attention, it's a liability, not an asset — and it may not even be the ad's real risk; irrelevance is.

### Chapter close

Ogilvy signs off the chapter (its closing lines are garbled in the surviving OCR of this scan) urging the reader to keep pursuing exactly the kind of researched, positioned, big-idea-driven, relentlessly sales-focused work described above — the recurring standard across the whole chapter being simply: does it make the cash register ring.

## 3. Wanted: Renaissance in Print Advertising (Headline and Body-Copy Rules)

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapter 7, "Wanted: a renaissance in print advertising." Ogilvy opens by arguing television has pulled the best creative talent away from print, leaving a skills gap in the one medium where — he insists — near-mechanical formulas reliably grab and hold attention. His evidence base: commissioned readership studies from Gallup & Robinson and the Starch Readership Service, direct-response split-test results, and decades of his own campaign experience — all pointing, he says, to remarkably consistent reader behavior across categories, layouts, and countries. This is the densest concentration of concrete, numbered rules in the book: headlines, illustrations, body copy, layout, typography, posters, and trademarks, in turn.

### Correcting the shorthand on headline value

`legends-library.md` Volume 2 summarizes Ogilvy's position as "the headline carries 80% of the ad's value." That exact figure doesn't appear in this chapter, and the real numbers are more specific. Ogilvy states that, on average, five times as many people read a headline as read the body copy — so if the headline doesn't sell the product by itself, roughly 90 percent of the ad's budget is wasted, since only about one reader in five ever reaches the sales argument in the body copy. Separately, he notes that if the brand name isn't in the headline, roughly 80 percent of readers (the same body-copy-skipping majority) will never learn what was even being advertised. The "80%" in the shorthand summary looks like a compression of that second, narrower point about brand identification, not a general claim about "value." The two real figures worth keeping are the 5-to-1 headline-to-body readership ratio and the 90-percent-of-budget-at-stake framing — both more precise and more useful than the rounded "80% of value" version.

### Headline rules

- **Lead with a reader benefit** (whiter wash, better mileage, clearer skin) rather than a seller's-eye description. Ogilvy suggests counting how many headlines in an average magazine actually promise a benefit — implying most don't.
  **Apply:** State the reader's benefit in their own words before drafting, and confirm it survives into the headline itself.

- **Lead with genuine news** — a new product, an improvement, or a new use for an old one. Ads with news in the headline are recalled by 22 percent more people than ads without it. He defends plain signal words like "amazing," "introducing," "now," and "suddenly" as functional, not clichéd.
  **Apply:** Put any real news in the headline, not buried in body copy nine out of ten readers won't reach.

- **Use specifics over generalities.** When shoppers overestimated Sears' profit margin, a headline stating the real, smaller specific figure beat a vaguer claim that profits were "less than you'd suppose."
  **Apply:** Swap any headline adjective for a real number wherever one is available.

- **Quote the headline.** Framing it as something said, in quotation marks, increases recall by an average of 28 percent.
  **Apply:** Test a quoted version against a flat declarative one when recall is the goal.

- **Name the city in local advertising** — readers respond to news about where they live.
  **Apply:** Work the city/region name into the headline itself for geo-targeted campaigns.

- **Test emotional word choice.** A psychologist measuring emotional response to hundreds of flashed words found "darling" scored highest; Ogilvy used it in a Dove headline on that basis.
  **Apply:** Treat individual word choice as testable, not a matter of taste.

- **Avoid cleverness that obscures meaning** — puns, double meanings, deliberate obscurity. A newspaper headline competes with roughly 350 others; readers skim too fast to decode a puzzle.
  **Apply:** Read a draft headline once, fast, as a stranger would; if the meaning doesn't land immediately, it's too clever for the format.

- **Never write a "blind" headline** that withholds the product or its benefit — these score about 20 percent below average recall.
  **Apply:** State the category or promised outcome in the headline; save mystery for the copy or visual.

- **Put the brand name in the headline.** Since roughly 80 percent of readers skip body copy, a headline without the brand name leaves most of the audience not knowing what was advertised.
  **Apply:** Default to including the brand name; treat leaving it out as a real cost.

- **Flag a niche audience directly** — a word like *asthma*, *bedwetters*, or *women over thirty* stops the specific reader a mass headline would miss.
  **Apply:** For a narrow-audience product, find the one word that would make your exact buyer stop, and use it.

- **"How to" and other helpful headlines draw above-average readership.**
  **Apply:** Reframe a claim as advice or instruction where the offer supports it.

- **Headline length is a wash.** Starch found headlines over ten words get lower overall readership, but a separate retail study found ten-word headlines sold more merchandise than short ones. Ogilvy's conclusion: write to the message, not to a target word count — his "Lemon" headline for Volkswagen shows a short headline can be just as powerful.
  **Apply:** Let the necessary information (or the power of one word) set the length, not a rule of thumb.

- **Never run an ad with no headline at all** — the single silliest mistake, since headlines decide success or failure more than any other element.
  **Apply:** Make "does this have a working headline" a non-negotiable pre-publish check.

Ogilvy points readers wanting more to John Caples' *Tested Advertising Methods*, and shares two personal favorite headlines (a joking baldness-cure question, a wry pile-remedy guarantee) as proof a strong headline needn't be solemn.

### Illustrations — fifteen rules

1. **The idea matters more than the execution** — no photographer can save a picture built on a weak concept.
2. **Curiosity-inducing photos work hardest.** Researcher Harold Rudolph called this "story appeal": the reader silently asks "what's going on here?" and reads on to find out.
3. **When there's no story, photograph the package.**
4. **Show the end result — before-and-after especially.** Of 70 campaigns Gallup reviewed with known sales results, every before-and-after campaign increased sales.
5. **Photographs generally beat drawings** for readership, believability, and memorability — Ogilvy's switch from drawings to photos in a British tourism campaign tripled both readership and tourism. Exception: where reproduction quality is poor, a good line drawing can outperform a muddy photo (scratch-board drawings beat photos in one shoe campaign).
6. **Reuse characters known from TV commercials** — it boosts recall of the print version.
7. **Keep illustrations simple, one focal person** — crowd scenes underperform.
8. **Don't enlarge faces beyond life size** — it repels readers.
9. **Historical subjects bore most readers.**
10. **Don't assume your own interests match the audience's** — Ogilvy, a former chef, assumed readers would find chefs interesting and got poor results with the housewives who were his actual audience.
11. **Babies, animals, and sex reliably draw attention** — per a tabloid editor's blunt answer to what interests readers, which Ogilvy says still holds.
12. **People prefer images of their own sex** — moviegoers and ad readers alike identify more with their own sex; ads featuring a woman tend to get ignored by men.
13. **Color pays for itself** — four-color ads cost about 50 percent more than black-and-white but are about 100 percent more memorable.
14. **Resist ego-driven choices** — enlarging a client's logo or showing their factory (or face) to placate them trades away reader engagement.
15. **For food/cooking ads, show the finished dish, not raw ingredients.**

**Apply:** Use this list as a pre-shoot checklist — subject/idea first, curiosity or before-after structure where possible, one focal person, own-sex representation considered, color budgeted for, finished result (not process) shown for food.

Ogilvy closes with a warning about misreading: a reader once mistook a hotel-lobby photo in a cigarette ad for a hospital ward.
**Apply:** Show any illustration to someone unfamiliar with the campaign and ask, unprompted, what they think it depicts.

### Body copy rules

Ogilvy reframes "nobody reads body copy" as depending on two variables: how interested people generally are in the category, and how well the headline and illustration pulled a given reader in. Average body-copy readership in magazine ads is about 5 percent — which still equals roughly 1.5 million people for a 5-percent slice of Reader's Digest's circulation.

- **Write to one person, not a crowd** — second person singular, like a personal letter. Ogilvy's model: Queen Victoria preferred Disraeli, who spoke to her as one human being to another, over Gladstone, who addressed her as if at a public meeting.
  **Apply:** Read a draft aloud as if to one named person; if it sounds like a speech, rewrite it as a letter.

- **You can't bore people into buying — only interest them.**
  **Apply:** Treat a dull-but-accurate passage as a failure to fix, not an acceptable cost of thoroughness.

- **Keep sentences and words simple.** Ogilvy's own copy once called Dove "obsolete" — most housewives didn't know the word, so he switched to "old-fashioned"; a Hathaway ad's use of "ineffable" drew a confused call from a reporter, and Ogilvy admits he wasn't fully sure of the word either.
  **Apply:** If a word might send a reader to a dictionary, replace it.

- **Write in the vocabulary of ordinary conversation.** His advice to a copywriter defending a fancy word: spend a week talking to a farmer, then to strangers on a train, and keep the word only if it survives.
  **Apply:** Test copy against how an ordinary person actually talks, not the writer's or client's own register.

- **Tell it as a story, with specifics, not an essay.** He points to narrative ads — a Zippo lighter that survived inside a fish, Caples' famous piano-lesson story — as proof specific, story-driven copy beats abstraction.
  **Apply:** Replace an abstract claim with a concrete anecdote demonstrating the same point.

- **Avoid analogies** — Gallup found readers widely misread them. Comparing skin's need for moisture to a plant's leaves readers not completing the equation; comparing a product to a Rembrandt risks readers thinking you're selling the painting.
  **Apply:** Replace "just as X, so too Y" reasoning with a direct statement of the actual benefit.

- **Avoid superlatives and "Brag and Boast"** claims like "best in the world" — Gallup found they convince nobody.
  **Apply:** Replace an unqualified superlative with a specific, checkable fact implying the same thing.

- **Use testimonials for credibility.** Readers trust a fellow consumer's endorsement more than a copywriter's own claim. Ogilvy paraphrases veteran copywriter James Webb Young's point that every advertiser's real problem is being believed, and testimonials are the mail-order world's answer to it — one general advertisers underuse. An entire ad can be a testimonial, as in Ogilvy's own Austin car ad written as a letter from an anonymous diplomat (a journalist later correctly guessed Ogilvy was the "diplomat").
  **Apply:** When credibility is the obstacle, cast the copy as a specific person's account rather than the advertiser's own voice.

- **Be wary of celebrities; expert testimonials hold up better.** Celebrities score high on recall but readers remember the celebrity and forget the product, and assume (often rightly) they were paid. An expert's testimony — Ogilvy's example is a former safecracker vouching for a safe brand — persuades through relevant expertise instead.
  **Apply:** Before hiring a celebrity, weigh whether their fame will eclipse the product in memory; prefer a credible expert for capability claims.

- **Don't shy from markdowns and special offers** — copywriters assume they're boring, but they score above average in recall.

- **Always state the price** where you control it. Ogilvy compares a priceless ad to a jeweler's window with no price tag — most people just move on rather than ask. This matters far more for considered purchases (cars, refrigerators) than package goods.
  **Apply:** Treat withholding a controllable price as a real conversion cost.

- **Sign the agency's work.** Ogilvy argues accountability sharpens effort — he cites working harder on a Reader's Digest house ad specifically because his name would be on it; the practice is standard in the US and spreading in Germany and France.
  **Apply:** Write every piece of copy as though your name will be publicly attached to it.

### Short copy or long copy?

Ogilvy says his experience overwhelmingly favors long copy, and that he's failed with it only twice (a cigar, a whiskey). Nine examples he cites, paraphrased:

1. Louis Engel's 6,450-word Merrill Lynch ad pulled 10,000 responses from one New York Times insertion, with no coupon.
2. Claude Hopkins' five-page Schlitz ad helped move the brand from fifth to first in sales within months.
3. Ogilvy's own 700-word Good Luck margarine ad drove sales.
4. His first Puerto Rico ad, about 600 words, drew 14,000 coupon responses, several leading to real factories built there.
5. An 800-word Shell newspaper series got 26 percent of male readers past the halfway point and coincided with reversing a seven-year market-share decline.
6. Francis X. Houghton's 4,750-word US Trust ad succeeded.
7. Ogilvy & Mather's own 2,500-word self-promotional ads brought in new business.
8. A World Wildlife Fund ad ran 3,232 words.
9. An 800-word Morgan Guaranty series benefited the bank.

He also credits long copy with worldwide Mercedes-Benz success. His interpretation, offered as personal belief rather than proven research, is that long copy signals the advertiser has something real to say, whether or not a given reader finishes it — reinforced by researcher Charles Edwards' finding that the more facts a retail ad includes, the more it sells, and by direct-response split tests where long copy reliably beats short.

**Apply:** Don't default to short copy on the assumption length repels readers — for an interested prospect, more real information tends to convert better. This is one of the most consistently tested claims in the chapter.

The catch: long copy only works if it's well written, and the opening paragraph carries the highest stakes. Ogilvy contrasts a flat, obvious vacation-ad opener with a Harvard professor who reportedly opened a lecture series with a shocking one-line account of Cesare Borgia's family scandal.
**Apply:** Before committing to long copy, make sure the first sentence creates real tension or curiosity — a generic opener loses the reader before length ever becomes an asset.

### How to become a good copywriter

Ogilvy treats deliberate imitation as a legitimate stage of craft development. He paraphrases art director Helmut Krone's account of disagreeing with a colleague who valued "doing his own thing" over making the ad as good as possible — Krone's view was that a beginner should copy the best available work until earning a better way; he'd closely copied Bob Gage (down to line spacing), who himself had copied Paul Rand, who had copied German typographer Jan Tschichold. Ogilvy says he did the same early in his career, copying the best American ads while working in London before developing his own style.
**Apply:** Early on, deliberately study and closely imitate specific admired ads — structure, rhythm, even typography — rather than assuming originality has to come first.

### Layout rules

Ogilvy dismisses what he calls "Artdirectoritis" — designers treating copy as a mere design element and talking about abstract "movement" and "balance." His counter is the acronym KISS (Keep It Simple, Stupid). He used one consistent magazine layout for all early Ogilvy & Mather ads; after a competitor accused him of a rigid house style, he added a second layout with more room for copy, using each depending on whether copy or image should carry the selling weight.

- **Follow the natural scan order: illustration, then headline, then copy, top to bottom.** Headlines set below the illustration are read by about 10 percent more people than headlines above it — meaningful at scale (10 percent of tens of millions of readers) — yet Ogilvy says 59 percent of magazine ads still put the headline on top, against the grain of how people actually read.
  **Apply:** Default to illustration-top, headline-below-it, copy-below-that; treat any other order as needing specific justification.

- **Always caption illustrations**, since more people read captions than body copy; the caption should carry the brand name and the promise.
  **Apply:** Never publish a photo without a caption written like a mini-ad.

- **Don't make ads look like ads.** Inherited advertising conventions silently tell readers "skip this." Making an ad resemble editorial content raises readership: roughly six times as many people read the average article as the average ad, and fewer than one ad in twenty is read by more than one person — evidence, Ogilvy argues, that editors simply communicate better than admen do.
  **Apply:** Ask whether a layout looks more like an article than a conventional ad; closer to editorial is the safer readership bet.

- **Copy the shared conventions of successful news magazines** (Time, Newsweek, and their era's foreign equivalents): copy prioritized over illustration, serif type, three columns of 35–45 characters, every photo captioned, copy opening with a drop-initial, black text on white.
  **Apply:** Use that six-item list as a checklist for any long-copy layout.

- **Notice that most ads violate every one of those conventions** — illustration over copy, sanserif type, single columns of 120+ characters (too wide), uncaptioned photos (despite captions getting roughly four times body copy's readership), missing drop-initials, and reverse type (even, Ogilvy notes, on coupons — literally unusable without white ink).
  **Apply:** Audit a layout against this specific failure list to catch avoidable readership losses fast.

Ogilvy praises the FCB-Impact agency in Paris (copywriter Pierre Lemonnier, art director Philippe Saalburg) for taking his editorial-layout approach and refining it further.

- **Don't approve layouts from across a room.** Layouts judged from fifteen feet away, like posters, tend to end up with illegible 72-point headlines at the actual twenty-inch reading distance.
  **Apply:** Review and approve print layouts at true size and reading distance, not pinned to a wall.

- **Two-page spreads rarely earn their cost.** They cost twice as much as a single page but seldom get twice the readership or response, and are usually self-indulgence rather than necessity (a genuinely long product needing horizontal display is the real exception). Skipping spreads lets you run twice as many ads for the same money. Caveat from Starch/Edwin Bird Wilson: spreads average only 28 percent higher readership than single pages overall, but financial advertisers' spreads get about 150 percent more — low-interest categories benefit more from bigger space than high-interest ones.
  **Apply:** Justify a spread only by real physical need or category evidence (like financial services), not as a default upgrade.

### Posters and transit advertising

Little formal research exists on posters, but Ogilvy offers what he has. Quoting designer Savignac, a poster should be a kind of "visual scandal" — arresting enough to stop a passerby — without overdoing the shock to the point of disrupting traffic. It should carry the promise in both words and image, be visible at a distance, use strong pure colors, and use no more than three design elements.

For transit cards specifically, don't design them like miniature billboards (five or six words, all display). The average New York subway rider is exposed to a card for 21 minutes, and 85 percent have nothing else to read — a captive-audience situation unlike a glimpsed billboard. Ogilvy used that logic to write 76 words for a Chase bank subway card.
**Apply:** Match copy density to actual dwell time — a captive, longer-dwell format can and should carry far more copy than a glance-and-move one.

### Trademarks

Ogilvy calls trademarks "an anachronism" — a holdover from when illiterate consumers needed a purely graphic mark to identify a brand — that mostly adds clutter today and signals "this is just an ad," reducing readership. He recounts a client paying $75,000 for a new company symbol, telling a company vice-president privately that a design student could have produced something as good for $75 — the reply was that a $75 version wouldn't have let leadership feel they'd earned it through debate.
**Apply:** Question whether a proposed logo redesign solves an actual readership problem or mainly satisfies an internal need to feel invested in identity.

### Typography rules

Good typography helps people read; bad typography stops them — the underlying idea being that the eye is a creature of habit, conditioned by a lifetime of reading familiar type conventions.

- **Avoid all-capitals headlines.** Stanford's Professor Tinker found capitals slow reading because they lack the ascenders/descenders that let a practiced reader recognize whole word shapes, forcing letter-by-letter reading instead.
- **Don't superimpose headline text on the illustration** — another way to make it hard to read.
- **Don't end headlines with a period** — full stops halt the reader's momentum exactly where you don't want it to; newspaper headlines never carry one.
- **Match column width to reading habit** — readers are used to newspaper columns around 40 characters wide; departing far from that hurts legibility.
- **Prefer familiar serif faces** (in the tradition of Caslon and Jenson-style types) over novel or sanserif type — the more unusual the typeface, the harder it is to read; drama belongs in the message, not the typeface.
- **Avoid reverse type (white on black) and decoratively "shaped" copy blocks.** Ogilvy found dozens of reversed ads in a single magazine issue, nearly unreadable — including coupons set in reverse, which are unusable without white ink. A Charles Saatchi ad set in reverse type raised only about half as much money for a children's charity as an otherwise identical version reset in normal black-on-white — direct behavioral evidence, not just a readership score, that reverse type suppresses response.
  **Apply:** Treat reverse type as a measurable cost, not a style choice — the Saatchi case shows it can roughly halve response on a direct-response offer.

For long copy specifically, ten typographic devices that increase readership:

1. A two-line subhead between headline and body copy builds anticipation.
2. Opening body copy with a drop-initial increases readership by an average of 13 percent.
3. Keep the opening paragraph to a maximum of eleven words.
4. Insert a cross-head every two or three inches, continuing throughout — phrasing some as questions builds curiosity for what follows.
5. Let paragraphs end in natural short lines ("widows") rather than justifying every line to full width.
6. Set a few key paragraphs in bold or italic.
7. Use arrowheads, bullets, asterisks, and marginal marks to guide the reader into paragraphs.
8. When reciting unrelated facts, skip clumsy connectives and simply number them.
9. Set body copy around 11-point type; 14-point, he says, is noticeably too large.
10. Insert extra leading between paragraphs — this increases readership by an average of 12 percent.

**Apply:** Use this as a literal pre-publish checklist for long-copy ads: subhead, drop-initial, sub-eleven-word opener, cross-heads every few inches, ragged paragraph endings, bolded key points, guide marks, numbered facts, ~11-point type, extra leading.

Ogilvy closes by anticipating the objection that he's overstating small typographic details, answering with the Mies van der Rohe line he uses as the chapter's epigraph: "God is in the details." No single rule decides success alone, but ignoring several at once compounds into an ad that goes unread — made concrete by the Saatchi example, where one typesetting decision measurably cut a real charity's response in half.
**Apply:** Don't dismiss any single typography rule as trivial in isolation — Ogilvy's own point is that these details compound, and enough of them ignored at once can quietly cut real-world response, not just readership scores.

## 4. How to Make TV Commercials That Sell

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapter 8. Ogilvy admits up front that his personal TV track record was thin, so this chapter leans almost entirely on outside data — chiefly the factor-analysis studies he commissioned from Mapes & Ross, which tracked *changes in brand preference* after exposure to a commercial rather than how well people remembered it. That distinction is his organizing principle for the whole chapter: he reports that people who shift their brand preference after seeing a spot go on to buy that brand roughly three times more than people who don't, whereas he found no reliable link between a commercial's recall score and its ability to move product off the shelf. Everything that follows ranks techniques by proven preference-shift, not memorability — a different axis than Bly's twelve-format TV list (`bly-copywriters-handbook.md`), which is worth reading alongside this for format mechanics; what Ogilvy adds is the research ranking behind each format.

**Ten formats that test above average for shifting preference:**

1. **Humor**, cautiously. Ogilvy notes that Claude Hopkins-era wisdom held that "people don't buy from clowns," and he believed that for most of his career — until newer factor-analysis data showed humor could sell after all. His caveat is sharp: almost nobody can actually write a commercial that is genuinely funny, so unless you're certain you're one of the few, don't attempt it.
2. **Slice of life** — two characters debate a product's merits until the skeptic is converted. Copywriters loathe the format as corny, but it keeps testing well precisely because it's been refined for decades; the skill is executing it so it feels real rather than embarrassing.
3. **Testimonials**, especially candid-camera-style ones where the subject doesn't know they're being filmed and an interviewer needles them into defending the product with real conviction. Ogilvy's Shell "hidden camera" script has an actor posing as a rival pump attendant trying to talk a loyal customer out of buying Super Shell — the more amateurish and unpolished the performance, the more credible viewers find it. A French campaign built around an unglamorous 80-year-old laundress became recognized by three out of four French viewers and moved the washing-machine brand from fourth place to second in sales.
4. **Demonstration** of the product actually working. It doesn't have to be dull: International Paper built a bridge out of its own paperboard and drove a loaded truck across it; Ogilvy's own Paris office proved a glue's strength by using it to hang an announcer's shoes from the ceiling. One caution: comparative demos that name a competitor directly tested as *less* clear and more confusing to viewers than demos that don't, and risked leaving audiences with the vague impression that the disparaged brand — not yours — was the one being advertised.
5. **Problem–solution**, one of the oldest formats: show the viewer a problem they recognize, then resolve it with the product (his example: a wife's cool head and a box of matches that strike instantly in humid weather, versus a husband failing with ordinary matches).
6. **"Talking heads"** — a presenter simply extolling the product. Agencies consider the format uncreative and are tired of it, but it keeps outperforming for undifferentiated categories; Ogilvy notes that of over a hundred new cigarette brands launched in Germany in one stretch, the only one that succeeded used a talking head.
7. **Continuing characters** built up over years, who become identified with the product — his examples are a folksy baker "spokesman" for a bread brand and a long-running coffee-brand character — provided the character is genuinely relevant to the product, not just charming.
8. **Reason-why** commercials that give viewers a rational justification for buying. When instant coffee was pitched as superior because it was freeze-dried, that single manufacturing detail was interesting enough to move viewers who otherwise (per Ogilvy) don't care how a product is made.
9. **News.** Anything genuinely new about a product tests above average, yet Ogilvy says copywriters routinely bury or omit real news — a failure he says should be "boiled in oil." For a mature product with nothing new to say, you can manufacture news by promoting an unfamiliar use for it.
10. **Emotion** — nostalgia, charm, even sentimentality — which he rates as being just as persuasive as a rational pitch, particularly for categories with no unique product claim to make (his standing examples: a bread campaign and a beer campaign built almost entirely on wistfulness). His partner Hal Riney's point, which Ogilvy endorses, is that clients and agency people alike routinely overrate how much consumers actually care about a product's "rational" benefits — ask what the real, provable benefit of a candy bar or a soda actually is. Ogilvy's caveat: give the viewer a rational alibi somewhere in the spot to justify the emotional decision to themselves, and never reach for emotion you can't genuinely deliver.

**Below average:** *celebrity testimonials* test worse than ordinary loyal-user testimonials, because viewers correctly assume the celebrity was paid, and they remember the star while forgetting the brand — Ogilvy's own cautionary tale is paying Eleanor Roosevelt $35,000 to pitch margarine, only to have her mail split evenly between people who thought she'd embarrassed herself and people glad she had. *Cartoons* work on children but underperform live action with adults; in a matched test for a fabric softener, the live-action version reversed a sales decline that the cartoon version did nothing to stop.

**A working checklist of smaller, testable rules**, several with hard numbers behind them: repeat the brand name early and often rather than being too clever to say it; end by showing the package, which measurably lifts preference change; make food look appetizing by showing it *in motion* — sauce pouring, syrup dripping; use close-ups when the product itself is the hero; grab attention in the very first frame, since viewers screen out anything that opens dull (if you're selling fire extinguishers, open on the fire); avoid jingles for anything but decoration — they test below average for shifting preference, and if people can't make out the words when you play the script to someone cold, don't air it (plain background music, by contrast, tests as neither helpful nor harmful); sound effects, unlike music, do help — a coffee commercial built around the sound of percolating ran successfully for five years; on-camera dialogue outsold an otherwise identical voice-over version in a direct split test; on-screen text ("supers") reinforcing your promise lifts sales, but only if the printed words exactly match the spoken words, since any mismatch confuses viewers; give the commercial a distinctive, odd visual detail that stands out from the roughly 30,000 commercials the average household is exposed to yearly (his example: a herd of bulls thundering at the camera for a financial brand); keep scene changes to a minimum, since spots with many quick cuts test below average, even though a few directors can pull it off; and build a recurring visual device ("mnemonic," his term) that reinforces brand identity over years, the way a certain gasoline brand's car crashed through the same paper barrier in ad after ad. Show the product actually being used, ideally with the end result visible (a diaper actually keeping a baby dry; motor oil's effect on an engine after 50,000 miles). He also flags a 1979 Purdue study by Professor Jacoby that tested 25 typical commercials for viewer misunderstanding: every single one was misunderstood by some viewers, and the best-understood spot was still misread by at least 19 percent of the audience — his conclusion is to make commercials so simple that misreading becomes hard.

Ogilvy is also blunt about cost discipline, calling it "the great scandal" that commercial production runs roughly $2,000 a second against about $4 a second for the programming that surrounds it. He blames agency indulgence more than genuine necessity — needless exotic locations, custom-composed music where stock would do, expensive celebrities where an unknown actor would sell as much or more — and notes that cutting each actor from a storyboard saves real money without necessarily costing sales; he cites a partner's experience remaking a $15,000 commercial for $100,000, after which sales actually fell.

On radio, which he calls advertising's "Cinderella medium" for how little research attention it gets, his own small pilot study surfaced four rules that most commercials still ignore: identify the brand early, identify it often, promise a benefit early, and repeat that promise. His Red, White and Blue Beer radio campaign, built on a wry "foreign aid" conceit and adhering to those rules, lifted sales by 60 percent.

**Apply:** before writing a TV or video spot, decide which above-average format actually fits the product's truth — don't default to humor or celebrity casting, both of which are either hard to pull off or measurably weaker at shifting preference than they feel in the room. Then run the small checklist: name and package visible, food (if any) in motion, attention grabbed in frame one, on-camera dialogue over pure voice-over, supers that match the audio word for word, and a script simple enough that a stranger can't misread it.

## 5. Direct Mail: "My First Love and Secret Weapon"

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapter 12. Ogilvy traces his own start in advertising to direct mail: as a young office boy handed a hopeless assignment (a client with $500 and a country hotel to fill), he spent it on postcards mailed to well-off local prospects and had a full house within six weeks — the experience that, in his words, meant he "had tasted blood." He calls direct mail his career-long "secret weapon" because it was the tool he used to win the string of new agency accounts that built Ogilvy & Mather, and he spent decades pushing the wider industry to stop treating direct-mail specialists as second-class.

His core reasoning for why the medium deserves more respect isn't sentimental — it's measurement. Any advertiser selling through wholesalers and retailers has real trouble isolating what its advertising alone contributed, because too many other factors blend into the final sale. A direct-mail advertiser, by contrast, can trace results to the exact dollar for each mailing, which is what makes rigorous testing of *every variable* possible in a way no other medium fully supports. This measurement argument is the piece worth adding on top of what `sugarman-handbook.md` and `bly-copywriters-handbook.md` already cover about direct mail's letter-first, personal-voice mechanics (see `sugarman-handbook.md`'s direct mail section and `bly-copywriters-handbook.md` §6) — Ogilvy's distinctive contribution is less about how to write the letter and more about the database-driven targeting and disciplined test-and-control methodology behind it.

He was writing in the early 1980s about what was, for the time, a genuinely new capability: computers let a mailer de-duplicate names across and within lists ("merge and purge"), segment prospects by fine-grained demographics, purchase frequency, and purchase amount, drop a name into a letter's salutation *and* repeatedly through the body copy, and — because most direct-response purchases were already made by credit card — target people based on what they'd actually bought (his example: someone who charged a Disney World trip is a good prospect for a Disneyland mailing). He's candid that the medium has always attracted fraud (he cites contemporary reports of thousands of fake charity "pastors" and well over a million consumer fraud complaints in a single year) but maintains the great bulk of direct-mail advertising is legitimate.

Because results are traceable, he treats testing as the medium's whole discipline, with a strict prioritization: you can only test one variable at a time, so test the variables likely to matter most — offer and positioning first, then price, payment terms, premiums, and format — and skip variables experience has already shown make little difference. His examples make the case for testing over assumption: a magazine testing three subscription-payment plans found that the cheapest, shortest-term offer ($2.95 for 29 issues) cut price-per-issue sharply yet produced 35 percent *more* net revenue than either pricier alternative; and a coin-collection mailing that offered a smaller *starter* set outsold an otherwise identical mailing offering the complete collection outright. Premiums and sweepstakes reliably lift initial response, he notes, but the customer drawn in by a giveaway isn't always the customer who becomes a loyal long-term buyer — so test premiums against long-term value, not just first-response rate. Once a mailing proves itself, he says to freeze it as the "control" and only test challengers against it; sometimes the winning challenger is a *cheaper* package (dropping personalization, cutting a brochure from four colors to two, or removing it altogether) that holds orders steady while cutting cost — his phrase for this is that "less can be more."

The chapter's anecdotes also illustrate techniques distinctive to the medium's reach for attention with high-consideration or offbeat prospects: he sold a $600,000 business jet partly through a mailing that sent live carrier pigeons to prospects with an invitation to release the bird carrying their address, as an entry into a free demo flight; his brother sold cooking stoves to school headmasters with a letter in Greek, and when some replied they couldn't read it, followed up in Latin. He's also emphatic that a good letter alone, without any brochure or premium, can carry very high-value sales if it's long enough to do the job — a five-page letter cleared out over a thousand obsolete diesel cars for Mercedes-Benz, and an eight-page letter sold successfully for Cunard.

The second half of the chapter applies the same discipline to **direct-response print and TV** — ads and commercials that ask the reader or viewer to order directly rather than visit a store. In print, he treats the headline as decisive (he'd seen one headline outpull another five to one), real photography as worth its extra cost over illustration except for cutaways that can't be photographed, long copy as outperforming short copy whenever the ask is a significant sum, and testimonials as reliable *unless* they're from a celebrity who isn't a genuine authority in the category (his allowed exception: a professional golfer endorsing golf clubs). He wants coupons treated as self-contained mini-ads — brand name, promise, and a small photo of the product — because readers often jump straight from headline to coupon to see the offer, and because a large share of intended responses (he cites a study putting it at roughly double what actually gets mailed) evaporate when a reader means to send it in "later" and never does; his fix is a hard deadline device — a limited edition, a limited supply, an expiring price, or a reward for promptness. Tidy, uncluttered layouts outpulled busy ones in his experience, reversing an older industry assumption. On media placement, he stresses that response is trackable to the exact insertion, so watch which titles your competitors keep re-running (a signal of continued profitability) and watch for editorial shifts that change who reads the surrounding pages; two-page spreads rarely earn back their doubled cost, but adding a bound-in reply card to a single page — for a fraction of the incremental cost of a spread — can multiply response several-fold; and response to the *same* ad in the *same* publication decays with repetition, so track how many times a given title can profitably carry your ad before retiring it there.

For **direct-response television**, he defers heavily to his partner Al Eicoff's specialist experience: the format that works sets up a problem, demonstrates the product solving it, offers a money-back guarantee, states the price on screen, and asks explicitly for the order — with at least 20 seconds reserved purely for ordering information (a toll-free number given as text and repeated verbally at least twice). Unlike the single-benefit discipline many packaged-goods advertisers follow, he notes direct-response demonstrations do better promising *several* benefits, not one. Commercials shorter than two minutes rarely produce profitable direct orders in Eicoff's experience, and a single long spot outperforms a cluster of short ones running back to back. Success should be measured in orders generated per airing, not cost-per-thousand-viewers — by that measure, the best time slots are early morning, late evening, and weekends, the best months are January through March, and — counterintuitively — a commercial run during a weak or boring program often out-orders the same spot run during a gripping hit show, because bored viewers are more likely to pick up the phone. Audience size and order volume, in his data, simply don't correlate.

**Apply:** if a client's advertising touches direct mail or any direct-response format, push for database-level targeting (recent purchase behavior, not just demographics) before writing the letter, and insist on a test-and-control regime rather than a single "best guess" mailing — test the offer and price before anything else, and don't retire a working control until a challenger beats it in an actual split test.

## 6. 18 Miracles of Research

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapter 15. Ogilvy's own background was in research before copywriting — he ran Gallup's Audience Research Institute, predicting box-office performance before movies were even released — and this chapter is his list of the concrete things research can do for an advertiser, followed by a section on where it goes wrong and how to run it on children. It complements Starch's and Hopkins' testing material already in this skill (`starch-advertising-principles.md`, `hopkins-scientific-advertising.md`) rather than repeating it: those two build the case for measurement as a discipline; Ogilvy's list is closer to a menu of specific business questions research can settle.

**The eighteen findings, paraphrased:**

1. Research can measure how your company's reputation actually looks across very different audiences at once — consumers, financial analysts, government officials, the press, and academics — rather than assuming one group's opinion speaks for all of them.
2. Mathematical forecasting models (he names several proprietary systems of his era) can estimate likely sales for a new product and the ad spend needed to maximize profit from it, and can tell you whether it's even worth the cost of a full test market — useful given that, by his figure, roughly 60 percent of new products that reach test market fail there anyway.
3. Research can react to a product concept before a dollar is spent building it. One client had already sunk $500,000 into a food line aimed at elderly consumers with declining digestion; concept research found the target group had little enthusiasm for it, and — to Ogilvy's surprise — the client accepted the finding immediately rather than disputing the methodology, killing the project as a "dry hole."
4. Once a product exists, research can benchmark it directly against what consumers already buy; if it loses that comparison, the fix belongs with product development, not with the ad agency.
5. Research can guide which formulation, flavor, fragrance, or color will appeal to the most consumers before those choices are locked in.
6. Research can determine which package design actually sells best — and, separately, whether ordinary people can even open the package (his anecdote: an actress publicly demonstrated she needed pliers to open a major food company's package).
7. Research can help identify the most effective market positioning for a product.
8. Research can define the target audience concretely — sex, age, income, education, lifestyle, and media habits — rather than leaving it as a vague impression.
9. Research can surface which factors actually drive the purchase decision in a category, and the vocabulary real consumers use to talk about that category, which often differs from the industry's own jargon.
10. Research can identify which "line extension" is likeliest to succeed: when a soap brand wanted to extend its name, research pointed to a dishwashing liquid over other options, and that extension went on to succeed.
11. Research can catch early signs that an established brand is losing its appeal — often, he notes, because consumers have quietly noticed a cheapened ingredient or formula before management has admitted it internally.
12. Research can effectively reverse-engineer a competitor's test-market performance — including estimates of their cost of goods and profit margin — from information that's publicly available if you know where to look.
13. **Selecting the right promise is, in Ogilvy's judgment, the single most valuable contribution research makes to advertising.** He invokes Samuel Johnson's line that a large promise is the soul of an advertisement, and cites Starch's own finding (see `starch-advertising-principles.md`) that ads containing a clear consumer benefit are read roughly four times more than ads that don't — yet he says most campaigns still contain no real promise at all, a point he flags as the most important sentence in the book. He describes two ways to find the winning promise: showing consumers a set of candidate promises framed as different hypothetical products and asking them to rate each for importance and uniqueness; or his own preferred method, a live split-run test — running two versions of a real ad, each headlining a different promised benefit and each offering a free sample, with a publication's circulation split evenly between them, and letting the volume of sample requests declare the winner. The split-run's advantage is that it measures real behavior instead of a hypothetical interview answer, though it can only compare two headlines at once — and he warns that the most persuasive promise you find is sometimes one a competitor already owns.
14. Research can rank which of several possible premiums will perform best — when one advertiser tested 35 candidate premiums, steak knives won outright, after which different knife designs were tested against each other; separately, a seashell premium that tested poorly was later used untested in another market and failed there too, underscoring that skipping the test doesn't change the underlying consumer response.
15. Research can confirm whether an ad is actually communicating what you intend it to — echoing E. B. White's warning that saying something is no guarantee you've actually said it.
16. Research can identify which of several TV commercials will sell more, but Ogilvy is pointedly skeptical of the industry's favorite pretesting method, recall. He lists four objections: nobody has shown a real link between recall and sales; some formats (celebrity spots, notably) score high on recall while scoring low on actually shifting brand preference; recall scores are trivially easy for a copywriter to inflate with a bizarre, irrelevant image that has nothing to do with selling (his partner's line about getting a high recall score by simply showing "a gorilla in a jock strap"); and it's unclear recall tests even measure recall, as opposed to a viewer's ability to *describe* what they recall, which is a different skill. He prefers tracking studies that measure brand-preference change directly over time — his example is a five-year mileage-focused campaign whose tracking scores plateaued, after which switching to consumer testimonials resumed the upward trend.
17. Research can tell you how many people actually read your ads and how much they remember, a discipline he credits George Gallup with founding — Gallup's method of asking readers to point to exactly what they'd read in a real issue surprised editors by showing comic strips consistently outdrew editorials, a finding Raymond Rubicam then adapted specifically to measure advertisement readership, eventually syndicating those scores across the industry.
18. Research can pressure-test a promotional idea against skeptical decision-makers with hard audience data rather than opinion — his supporting example is his own finding, from his movie-research days, that a clear majority of a sampled audience said they would pay to see a historical film about Abraham Lincoln, the kind of concrete number that settles an argument a boardroom debate can't.

Two questions, Ogilvy is careful to note, research in his experience could *not* reliably answer: which of several campaigns will build a brand the most over a period of years (still a judgment call), and what price a product should actually be sold at.

**On sample size:** small samples work fine for simple factual questions — he says twenty housewives are enough to learn whether an unfamiliar word is understood — but tracking a trend over time needs a larger sample, held to a rigidly consistent question wording and respondent mix from wave to wave, or apparent "changes" may just be noise.

**Pitfalls he flags from direct experience:** interviewers sometimes find it easier to let respondents self-administer a questionnaire in a quiet room than to accost strangers on the street (he mentions a London pub that set aside a private room for exactly this). Respondents often don't tell interviewers the truth: he used to open surveys with a planted question (would you rather hear Jack Benny or Shakespeare on the radio tonight) and treated anyone who claimed to prefer Shakespeare as a liar and ended the interview. Simple wording changes fix social-desirability bias — asking whether people had read a bestselling novel produced an inflated "yes" count because nobody wanted to admit they hadn't; rephrasing to ask whether they *planned* to read it let honest non-readers answer truthfully and produced a far more credible result. Field execution can also fail in ways no methodology fixes: he once was stopped by a street interviewer reading a questionnaire he himself had written two days earlier and found the questions simply unanswerable as written, so he scrapped the entire survey rather than trust the data it would produce. Test design can also smuggle in a hidden variable: a manufacturer testing his product in cans versus glass jars found housewives strongly preferred the jarred samples on taste — except the two formats hadn't actually been blind-tested apart from their packaging, so what the test really proved was a packaging preference, not a taste difference. And a French government field study cut identical wheels of cheese in half, priced the halves differently, and confirmed people judge quality by price: the higher-priced half of the same cheese sold faster.

**Research with children** needs its own methods, since young children struggle to articulate real opinions and tend to tell adults what they think the adults want to hear. Ogilvy describes three workable techniques: *group dynamics*, where children who've seen a commercial act it out or role-play about it with each other, which surfaces confusion or negative reactions adults wouldn't otherwise catch; the *communication-discrepancy* method for older children, where you compare what they say they liked about the commercial against what they say they like about the real product afterward — if a commercial undersells a genuine feature (his example: far fewer children mentioned liking a doll's ability to walk from the ad than mentioned it once they saw the actual doll), that's a signal the ad needs to change; and the *prize-pad test*, where children circle a wanted toy on an illustrated pad before and after seeing a commercial, told between rounds that the first pad's names weren't recorded, so a genuine before/after shift in choice measures the commercial's real persuasive power. He closes the topic by noting that children's advertising is now bound by strict regulations he lists in part — no peer-pressure appeals, no fear or anxiety appeals, no war-context toy dramatization, mandatory disclosure of batteries or accessories not included, and toys shown only in play settings a child could realistically recreate.

Ogilvy closes the chapter acknowledging that peers like Bill Bernbach (see Section 7 below) considered research the enemy of creativity; his own experience was the reverse — research talked him into wild ideas he'd otherwise have rejected outright (an eyepatch for a shirt campaign, headlines written in French for French tourism, both of which his own partners initially thought were mistakes) and saved him from expensive ones. His closing caution is aimed at misuse, not the tool itself: agencies and clients too often use research "as a drunkard uses a lamppost — for support rather than illumination," reaching for data that confirms a decision already made instead of data that might overturn it.

**Apply:** before defending a creative choice on instinct alone, ask which of these eighteen questions is actually in dispute — many have a cheap, fast test attached (a 20-person comprehension check, a two-headline split run, a before/after prize-pad) rather than requiring a full study. And when you do get research back, separate a genuine finding (a measured shift, a real behavior count) from a rationalization search — the same discipline Ogilvy applied when a client accepted a "dry hole" finding instead of arguing with it.

## 7. Lasker, Resor, Rubicam, Burnett, Hopkins, and Bernbach (Ogilvy on the Other Legends)

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapter 18, "Lasker, Resor, Rubicam, Burnett, Hopkins and Bernbach." Ogilvy calls these six the giants who invented modern advertising, and notes what they had in common: all American, all had worked other jobs before advertising, most were workaholic perfectionists, four built their reputations as copywriters, and only a minority had college degrees.

### Albert Lasker (1880-1952)

Lasker ran Lord & Thomas and, in Ogilvy's telling, made and spent more money than anyone else in the business's history. He started as a teenage newspaper reporter in Galveston before his father secured him a job at Lord & Thomas, where he began by cleaning spittoons and rose to run the agency for over 40 years, eventually owning 95 percent of it and operating it as an outright dictatorship — he told his staff the agency was simply "Albert D. Lasker practicing advertising."

Ogilvy credits Lasker's transformation into an advertising force to a chance encounter: copywriter John E. Kennedy convinced him that advertising was "salesmanship in print," a definition Lasker later said had never been improved upon. From that point, Lasker's whole philosophy was that copy that sold the product was the only thing an agency needed — he resisted hiring art directors for years and had open contempt for market research, preferring to ask his own wife and friends whether they used a product rather than commission studies. Ogilvy retells Lasker's own anecdote about the Kotex account: instead of surveying "millions of women," Lasker's people simply asked their wives why sales were flat, learned women were embarrassed to ask druggists for it by name, and fixed it by putting plain-wrapped packages on the counter — a fix that let customers avoid saying the product's name aloud. By stripping out the marketing, art, and research overhead, his agency ran at an extraordinary profit margin.

Lasker was also personally ferocious: he hired talented people, paid them well, then burned through them so fast that at one point nine major agencies were headed by his former staff — his own line was that he made his people "so good that I can't keep 'em." Ogilvy paints him as brilliant but erratic — a philanthropist who gave away much of his fortune to medical research and amassed a major art collection, yet also a man who cut every employee's salary 25 percent during the Depression while paying himself millions, and who suffered three prolonged nervous breakdowns. He simply walked away from the business in 1912, handing Lord & Thomas to three junior partners (who renamed it Foote, Cone & Belding) for a token payment, on the condition his name come off the door.

**Apply:** Lasker's Kotex story is a model for finding the real psychological barrier to purchase (shame, not indifference) through a few honest conversations rather than a formal study — sometimes the fix is a small, un-glamorous mechanical change (plain wrapping) rather than a new pitch.

### Stanley Resor (1879-1962)

Resor took over J. Walter Thompson when it billed $3 million a year and, over 45 years, grew it into the largest agency in the world at $500 million in billings. Ogilvy's summary of his method is that Resor's whole success rested on his ability to recruit unusually capable people and then keep them by treating them with genuine respect — the agency ran with no hierarchy and no fixed job titles, structured more like a partnership of equals than a company with a boss.

Unlike Lasker, Resor was a committed believer in research, hiring economists, a former Census Bureau director, and even running taste-test kitchens and early television experiments for clients well before TV carried advertising. He also had firm personal standards — he turned down the lucrative Camel account rather than let cigarette ads run, and he never drank alcohol. Ogilvy singles out one Resor innovation as his most consequential: he was the first agency head to hire women as copywriters, starting with his own future wife, Helen, who became one of the best copywriters in the country and, as Ogilvy tells it, was arguably more responsible for the agency's creative and cultural distinction than Resor himself (she helped found the planned parenthood movement and shaped JWT's art collecting). Despite marrying a copywriter, Resor tended to regard copywriters generally as lightweights and let account "representatives" dominate the agency; he also believed heavily in celebrity endorsement, using Hollywood stars for Lux and titled Englishwomen for Ponds.

Ogilvy adds a personal anecdote: after Ogilvy lost his biggest early account to JWT, he called Resor to congratulate him, and Resor immediately tried to recruit him — twice, the second time offering to buy Ogilvy's whole agency. Ogilvy's verdict on Resor's one real failure is that he simply stayed too long, still running the agency into his eighties after its ideas had gone stale, having let his ablest potential successors retire before him.

**Apply:** Resor's structure argues for retaining senior talent through genuine autonomy and respect rather than hierarchy — and his late-career stagnation is a caution that even a great operator needs to plan succession before, not after, the work goes stale.

### Raymond Rubicam (1892-1978)

Rubicam is the figure Ogilvy treats with the most personal warmth in the chapter, calling him his "conscience for 40 years" and stating he knew and loved him longer than any of the other five giants. Rubicam left school at 15, drifted through jobs (shipping clerk, bellhop, movie projectionist, door-to-door salesman) before landing a copywriting job in Philadelphia by way of a confrontational letter to his prospective boss after being kept waiting nine days. He later moved to N.W. Ayer, writing campaigns still anthologized today (Steinway, Squibb), before co-founding Young & Rubicam on a $5,000 shoestring; by the time Ogilvy was writing, it was among the one or two biggest agencies on earth.

Ogilvy credits Rubicam with being the first to fold research formally into the creative process — hiring George Gallup to measure readership of ads and using those findings to systematically produce the most-read advertising of any agency of the era. Rubicam's own summary of the strategy was blunt: "the way we sell is to get read first." He also fixed the agency's early weakness in visual execution by hiring the best art director he could find, after which Y&R's graphic standards changed American advertising taste. But the achievement Ogilvy says Rubicam was proudest of wasn't research or craft — it was ethics: Rubicam told Ogilvy late in life that he'd proven you could sell products without deceiving the public, and defined a truly good advertisement as one that leaves both the audience and the advertiser proud of it long after the sale.

Rubicam consistently sided with creative staff over account management in agency power struggles (he dismissively called account executives "contact men") and was willing to resign large, profitable accounts — including American Tobacco — rather than let a client bully his staff. He also advised General Foods, then his biggest client, to split its business across multiple agencies once it outgrew Y&R, which is how a competitor got its first major account — a move that cemented client trust rather than costing him business. Off the job, Ogilvy notes Rubicam was politically outspoken in ways Resor never was, publicly opposing the atomic bombing of Japan and later criticizing television's effect on literacy and crime. Rubicam retired at 52 having run the agency only 21 years — far shorter than Lasker's 40 or Resor's 45 — and, unlike them, left behind no codified rules, just an ethos: resist the usual.

**Apply:** Rubicam's "get read first" reframes the job of every headline and opening line as clearing a readership bar before persuasion can even begin — and his stance on ethics (copy you and the client can both be proud of years later) is a durable filter for judging your own work, separate from whether it merely converts.

### Leo Burnett (1891-1971)

Burnett personified what Ogilvy calls the "Chicago school" of advertising, a self-consciously Midwestern, plain-spoken counterpoint to the New York agencies. He worked as a reporter and in Cadillac's ad department before founding his own agency in Chicago in 1935, but Ogilvy notes he didn't hit his real stride until age 60, after which his agency became the largest outside New York within two decades. Ogilvy quotes Burnett's own account of Chicago advertising's character: he described it as rooted in "prairie-town views and values," built by copywriters willing to write "ain't" when that's what a character would actually say, in contrast to more polished coastal copy.

Ogilvy lists three of Burnett's core creative maxims: that every product contains an inherent drama the copywriter's job is to dig out; that reaching for the stars, even if you fall short, beats aiming low and succeeding; and that a writer should immerse in the subject, work hard, and trust — "love, honor and obey" — instinct once earned through that immersion. Burnett ran his shop through a Creative Review Committee so tough he compared presenting to it as being "nibbled to death by ducks," and he explicitly rejected novelty for its own sake, quoting an old boss's warning that being different purely to be different invites disaster. Ogilvy identifies the Marlboro campaign — which turned an obscure brand into the world's best-selling cigarette and was still running decades later — as Burnett's single greatest achievement, and notes Burnett, unlike Ogilvy, had little interest in direct-response long copy, favoring ads that read like miniature posters.

Burnett also kept a folder on his desk literally labeled "Corny Language," collecting overheard vernacular phrases and folksy analogies to mine for ads later — a deliberate, ongoing practice rather than a one-time inspiration. Ogilvy quotes a memo Burnett sent staff after catching someone using a competitor's product, arguing that using your own client's products isn't a matter of vanity but of basic honesty toward the people who pay the agency's bills. Late in life, Burnett told his own staff the one condition under which they should ever remove his name from the door: the day the agency started caring more about its own size and profit than about doing good work for clients — a line Ogilvy says he wishes he'd written himself.

**Apply:** Keep a running "corny language" file of authentic, overheard phrasing to draw on later, and before writing, spend real time hunting for the specific "inherent drama" already sitting inside the product rather than inventing a claim from nothing.

### Claude Hopkins — Ogilvy's Assessment (not a repeat of Hopkins' own principles)

Ogilvy is explicit that *Scientific Advertising* changed the direction of his own career, and he says he has told everyone entering the business that nobody should be allowed near advertising without reading it — by his account — repeatedly. That personal debt is the frame for the rest of his assessment, which is notably more mixed than reverent.

Ogilvy credits Hopkins concretely with a set of firsts: forcing distribution for new products, inventing test marketing, inventing coupon sampling, and inventing copy research as a discipline — plus building famous campaigns for Pepsodent, Palmolive, and several car brands while at Lord & Thomas under Lasker (who reportedly paid him the equivalent of roughly $2 million a year in today's money). At the same time, Ogilvy is willing to say Hopkins was simply wrong on at least one specific, testable point: Hopkins argued advertisers should write only for new customers because existing users won't bother reading their ads, and Ogilvy states later research proved the opposite — existing users actually read a brand's advertising more than non-users do. Ogilvy also notes, citing researcher Alfred Politz, that Hopkins wasn't always careful to distinguish rigorous experimental findings from mere general observation dressed up as data — a methodological knock on the very "scientific" reputation Hopkins built his name on.

The sharpest external judgment Ogilvy reports isn't his own — it's Raymond Rubicam's. Rubicam, Ogilvy writes, "abhorred" Hopkins, believing he'd spent his career deceiving the public, and once told Ogilvy directly, "You are Hopkins with a college education" — which Ogilvy dryly calls "a backhanded compliment if ever I heard one." Ogilvy doesn't fully endorse that verdict, but includes it prominently rather than defending Hopkins against it, which is itself telling. He also gives a more sympathetic, personal closing note: Hopkins was a narrow, work-obsessed man with almost no interests outside advertising, and Ogilvy finds something bleak in the final line of Hopkins' own autobiography about happiness belonging to those who live close to nature — from a man who apparently never did.

**Apply:** Treat Hopkins' rules as a starting toolkit, not gospel — Ogilvy himself, Hopkins' most famous advocate, publicly overturned one of his central claims (write only for new customers) once better data existed. Test the classics rather than inherit them unexamined.

### Bill Bernbach (1911-1982)

Bernbach founded his agency the same year as Ogilvy and, like Ogilvy, made his name as a copywriter first. He came up through the Schenley mailroom, then speechwriting, then the Weintraub agency (where he worked alongside Bauhaus-trained art director Paul Rand), before starting Doyle Dane Bernbach with Ned Doyle and Max Dane on a modest investment — despite his name appearing last on the letterhead, Ogilvy is clear it was unambiguously Bernbach's agency, and it grew into a billion-dollar-billing top-ten shop.

The real substance of this profile is the contrast Ogilvy draws between Bernbach's approach and his own. Ogilvy states plainly that Bernbach "worshipped at the altar of originality" and was known for openly denouncing research as the enemy of creativity — a direct, named repudiation of the research-driven discipline Ogilvy built his entire reputation on. Ogilvy doesn't pretend this is a minor stylistic difference; he calls out that stance as exactly what made Bernbach "the hero of the creative fraternity," implicitly distinguishing that fraternity's values from his own more numbers-driven camp. Yet Ogilvy still credits Bernbach with a real, structural creative innovation: an unusual gift for integrating copy and image as a single idea, rather than treating art direction as subordinate to copywriting — Ogilvy admits this was a mistake he made in his own work that Bernbach avoided. The two men did agree on one first principle, however: both held that the quality of the underlying idea and the excellence of its execution were the ultimate measure of an ad, whatever their disagreement over how to arrive at that idea.

Ogilvy singles out the Volkswagen and Avis campaigns as the Bernbach work he admires most, while noting Bernbach was less successful on package-goods accounts where clients pushed back with more conventional research-driven discipline — an implicit point in Ogilvy's own favor. He also relays a personal anecdote showing Bernbach's stubborn confidence: when Ogilvy warned him that a United Negro College Fund fundraising commercial Bernbach had volunteered to make, while artistically strong, wasn't built to actually raise cash, Bernbach simply replied that there were plenty of other agencies happy to do the job if Ogilvy wanted a different approach — and the ad ran as he'd made it. Elsewhere Ogilvy notes Bernbach reportedly carried a card reminding himself "maybe he's right," a rare admission of doubt from a man Ogilvy otherwise found quietly but thoroughly self-assured; Ogilvy witnessed him concede a client was correct exactly once, at a White House lunch. Bernbach's own view of what wouldn't change in advertising, quoted near his death, was that human nature is essentially fixed, and that a communicator's real job is to understand the primitive, unglamorous instincts that actually drive people rather than the surface language culture uses to dress them up.

**Apply:** Bernbach's copy/art fusion — treating the visual and verbal idea as one inseparable thought rather than writing copy and then illustrating it — is worth deliberately practicing even inside a research-driven process; the tension Ogilvy names (originality vs. testability) is a real trade-off to make consciously per project, not something to resolve permanently in either direction.

## 8. Briefly: Agency Management, Clients, Business-to-Business, and Predictions

Source: David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), Chapters 3–6, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, 17, 19, 20, and the Reading List. These chapters range from career advice and agency-hiring practices to country-tourism advertising and Cold War-era musings on American competitiveness — most of it agency-operations, personnel, or dated cultural commentary rather than copywriting craft. Chapters 3, 4, 5, 17, and 20 are compressed hard for exactly that reason: they are about running a business or reading the geopolitical tea leaves, not about writing an ad. Chapters 11 and 19 get the deepest treatment because they hold the most transferable technique.

### Chapters 3–5: careers, running an agency, winning clients (heavily compressed — agency-operations content)

These three chapters are Ogilvy's advice to young agency employees and agency owners: how to break into copywriting, art direction, account management, or research; how to keep a hothouse of anxious creative people functioning and happy; how to pitch and win new accounts. Almost none of it is copywriting technique. The few ideas a copywriter can actually use:

- The traits Ogilvy lists as predicting a good copywriter are worth self-auditing against: restless curiosity about products and people, a sense of humor, a hard-work habit, and the ambition to outdo every campaign that came before yours.
- Write short, plain memos and briefs. Ogilvy notes that the longer and more turgid a memo, the less likely a busy decision-maker reads it — he cites Churchill demanding Admiralty strategy summarized on one side of one page.
- A researcher's job is to be intellectually honest even when it's inconvenient; a brief padded with confirming-bias findings does real damage down the line. Treat research the same way when you commission or read it yourself.
- When pitching new work, don't hide behind case histories and data dumps — clients hire people who can solve their specific problem, not people reciting somebody else's market-share win.

### Chapter 6: Open letter to a client in search of an agency

This is Ogilvy's advice to clients on managing the agency (or, read the other way, freelance copywriter) they've hired — and it contains a genuinely durable line for anyone paying for creative work: **"any fool can write a bad advertisement, but it takes a genius to keep his hands off a good one."** Once you've hired someone competent, resist the urge to rewrite their headline yourself. Ogilvy also advises giving your writer/agency a real, honest annual performance review rather than a slow accumulation of unspoken irritation, and to reject weak work gently while praising strong work lavishly — thin-skinned as they may be, copywriters do their best work for clients who make them want to keep earning the praise.

### Chapter 9: Advertising corporations

Corporate ("image") advertising — the kind that sells the company rather than a specific product — differs from consumer copy mainly in how easy it is to get wrong. Ogilvy found most of it drowns in interchangeable, meaningless slogans:

- He lists real examples he considers nearly useless — lines like "the resourceful company" or "where science gets down to business" — because they're generic enough that any competitor could paste them onto their own name with no loss of meaning. Treat that as a test for your own tagline: could a rival swap in their name and lose nothing?
- Corporate campaigns fail most often because nobody sets a measurable objective for them and because they're abandoned too soon — Ogilvy points to DuPont's decades-long, tracked corporate campaign as the rare exception that worked.
- Don't rebrand a well-known company name into an unrecognized acronym; it can take years and real money just to teach the public what the letters mean.
- A company's own executives appearing in its ads is a two-edged sword — it can add credibility, but an amateur usually delivers lines worse than a professional voice would.

### Chapter 10: How to advertise foreign travel

Ogilvy's tourism-advertising work is really a case study in letting research — not committee opinion or political pressure — decide what you feature:

- When British officials wanted "Come to Britain" ads to project a modern industrial nation, Ogilvy's research said American tourists wanted castles, tradition, and royalty, and he ran what the research said, not what the client's ego wanted. The campaign later moved Britain from fifth to first among European destinations for American tourists.
- Advertise what is genuinely unique to the thing you're selling, not features it shares with every competitor — Ogilvy's rule was not to sell the Swiss on American ski resorts (they have their own) or the French on American food.
- When the audience's existing image of a place or product is wrong (Puerto Rico as "dirty and squalid" when it wasn't), correcting that specific false belief head-on, with proof, can outperform generic aspirational copy.
- Give real, specific how-to information (prices, logistics) alongside emotionally appealing imagery — Ogilvy found dreamy photography without concrete detail doesn't convert desire into a booked trip.

### Chapter 11: The secrets of success in business-to-business advertising

This chapter draws heavily on McGraw-Hill's research into what Ogilvy still called "trade" or "industrial" advertising, and it holds up as some of the most concretely actionable material in the whole book.

**Why advertise at all, if salespeople close the deal:** McGraw-Hill's math (reported in early-1980s dollars) put a sales call at $178, a letter at $6.63, and a phone call at $6.35 against roughly 17 cents to reach one prospect through an ad. Advertising's job isn't to close the sale itself — it's to warm up the prospect and generate leads before a rep ever calls, and 60 percent of the "specifiers" who write technical requirements read trade advertising specifically to learn what's on the market.

**The techniques are the same as consumer advertising, made more specific:**
- The same benefit-promise/news/testimonial/helpful-information toolkit works, but the benefit has to matter to a technical buyer. A software vendor proud of its company's size learned through research that customers actually wanted responsiveness and support — size wasn't a benefit to them at all.
- Make every promise concrete: percentages, dollars saved, hours cut — you're talking to engineers, not daydreamers.
- Testimonials work well only from credible experts inside the field — a tugboat engineer trusts a 25-year Mississippi river veteran, not a celebrity.
- Demonstrations land best when they let the reader test the claim themselves (Ogilvy's example: inviting a reader to scrape a coin against an air-duct liner to feel how tough it is) or when they show your product outperforming a competitor's side by side.
- News that the reader can apply directly to their own job outperforms an equivalent article in the same trade journal by roughly 2:1, according to the McGraw-Hill data Ogilvy cites — so state genuine news plainly and clearly, not buried under jargon.

**Layout mechanics specific to trade/technical print:** headlines pull roughly five times the readership of body copy, so a headline that doesn't carry the offer wastes the ad. Body copy is read by only around 10 percent of a publication's audience, but that 10 percent are self-selected prospects, so don't dumb it down for them — and don't be afraid of long copy (350+ words) for a complex product like a computer or a generator; it actually pulls more readers than short copy does, the reverse of what most people assume. Captions get roughly double the readership of body copy, so write them as mini-ads, not photo credits. Four-color ads in trade publications cost about a third more than black-and-white but pull double the readership — a good trade.

**TV works for B2B too:** the sports and news programming that skews toward business viewers makes TV an efficient buy even for industrial products, and the same principles that work for consumer TV (news, demonstration, and — carefully deployed — humor that still carries the sales promise, as in the Federal Express "when it absolutely has to be there overnight" spots) transfer directly. Some products can't be sold in 30 seconds; Ogilvy used three-minute spots for IBM computers rather than compress a real message into an ineffective short one.

**Escaping the commodity trap:** citing a Harvard Business Review study of eight industries, the most successful "commodity" companies differentiated on one of two axes — lowest cost, or best reputation for quality/service — and advertising is what builds and spreads that reputation once you've earned it. Owens-Corning turned its insulation from an interchangeable commodity into the preferred brand for old-home fuel savings by locking onto one small, ownable feature (its distinctive color) rather than inventing a new claim from nothing.

**Generating and proving the value of leads:** always include a toll-free number, a reply card, and a coupon — McGraw-Hill found seven in ten trade-journal readers already use toll-free numbers to respond. Put your contact information at the end of the body copy too, since a business publication is typically read by several people besides the subscriber. To answer the inevitable "what did this advertising actually do for us" question, Ogilvy recommends three concrete steps: survey a sample of people who inquired to learn their real purchase intent, debrief the salespeople who followed up on each lead, and track which specific media placements produced which inquiries so you can reallocate the budget toward what's working.

### Chapter 13: Advertising for good causes

A short chapter of case studies — worth skimming for proof that direct, blunt copy works as well for causes as for products. A Norwegian government anti-drinking campaign aimed at teenagers used a shockingly plain headline ("I vomit almost every time I drink") and got the highest readership of any ad in the campaign; a parallel ad aimed at parents used a specific, alarming statistic rather than a vague warning, and was read by over 70 percent of Norwegian parents. The lesson generalizes: specificity and directness beat euphemism whether you're selling soap or fighting teenage alcoholism.

### Chapter 14: Competing with Procter & Gamble

Ogilvy's account of P&G's methods (via his partner Kenneth Roman) doubles as a checklist of disciplined marketing practice that any advertiser — not just a P&G competitor — can study:

- P&G plans thoroughly and minimizes risk rather than chasing novelty; they never enter a small product category unless they expect it to grow, and they aim to dominate whatever category they do enter.
- They invest heavily in sampling to get real product trial before spending on persuasion — Ogilvy quotes their own chairman saying advertising and selling only become "fully productive" once satisfied customers have already tried the product firsthand.
- Their central premise: **advertising cannot save a product with no real, perceptible superiority.** They build a genuinely better product first, verify the improvement is perceptible to consumers in blind in-home tests, and only then advertise it — echoing Ogilvy's own "improve the product" argument in Chapter 19 below.
- Their commercials follow a rigid formula worth borrowing pieces of: state the promise within the first 10 seconds and repeat it roughly three more times, deliver the promise verbally and reinforce it with on-screen supers, and close by restating the promise once more.
- They test relentlessly and patiently — one product spent six years in regional test markets before national rollout — and once a campaign strategy is proven, they keep running it for a decade or more rather than getting bored with it and changing for change's sake.

### Chapter 16: What little I know about marketing

A grab-bag of Ogilvy's practical marketing observations, several of which sit right next to copywriting decisions:

- **New products fail** most often not because they're too novel, but because they aren't novel *enough* — they lack any perceptible point of difference (better quality, flavor, value, convenience, or problem-solving) that a customer can actually notice and repeat-purchase on.
- **Naming:** personal names (Ford, Campbell) read as human and are hard to copy; invented names (Kodak, Kotex) take years and real money to load with meaning; descriptive names (Band-Aid, 3-IN-ONE Oil) start with built-in sales appeal but box you in if you ever want to extend the line. Pick deliberately, not by whichever domain happens to be free.
- **Don't starve winners to nurse losers.** Ogilvy's advice is blunt: spend your time, brainpower, and budget on your successes, and have the discipline to cut a failing product loose rather than pouring more creative effort into reviving it.
- **Promotions are not a substitute for advertising.** Price-off deals can produce a short-term "kink" in the sales curve, per Ogilvy's colleague, but buyers drift back to their habitual brand once the deal ends — sustained brand advertising, not couponing, is what builds durable share.
- **Don't cut advertising in a recession.** Multiple studies Ogilvy cites found that companies that maintained spending through downturns came out ahead of those that cut back, especially for products still in their early growth phase — cutting an unestablished brand's advertising in a downturn can kill it outright.
- Price and perceived quality correlate far more weakly than most marketers assume — one study found almost no relationship between the price and the actual quality of hundreds of food brands — so don't assume you have to race to the bottom on price to compete; a genuinely superior product can usually support a premium.

### Chapter 17: Is America still top nation? (heavily compressed — nationalistic commentary, not technique)

This chapter is mostly Cold War-era hand-wringing about whether American advertising still leads the world, plus travelogue-style comparisons of national advertising styles. The one durable, transferable observation: British advertising of the era favored subtlety, self-deprecating humor, and a "sense of proportion" about how much any single purchase decision actually matters to the buyer, in contrast to the more direct, hard-sell American style — a useful reminder that tone should scale to how emotionally weighty the purchase decision genuinely is to the reader, not to how much the advertiser wants to shout.

### Chapter 19: What's wrong with advertising?

Ogilvy's direct response to advertising's critics doubles as his clearest statement of where he thinks the profession's real ethical lines sit — useful for calibrating your own standards, not just for winning arguments with skeptics.

- His core defense: advertising is "no more and no less than a reasonably efficient way to sell," and he quotes P&G's own former president that they'd abandon advertising the day a more efficient selling method existed. That's a useful frame for evaluating your own copy too — the test is efficiency at persuasion, not artistic merit.
- **Advertising is regulated far more tightly than people assume** — every ad passes through legal review, industry codes, and bodies like the Better Business Bureau and the FTC, which is why Ogilvy argues most commercial advertising is more honest than critics give it credit for.
- **The one category he singles out as genuinely, flagrantly dishonest is political advertising** — because it's classified as protected speech, it escapes every code and regulatory check that applies to a toothpaste ad, and Ogilvy catalogs specific presidential campaigns (Johnson's attacks on Goldwater, in particular) as far more deceptive than anything the FTC would ever let a consumer brand get away with. The lesson for any copywriter: don't assume "nobody's checking" is a genuine advantage — the categories of advertising with the least oversight are also the ones the public trusts least.
- **Manipulation and subliminal advertising are, in his firsthand account, a myth** — the famous 1950s "eat popcorn/drink Coke" subliminal-message study was fabricated by its own researcher, who never even ran the test; Ogilvy states plainly that in decades in the business he never saw a genuine subliminal technique used or proven to work.
- **Advertising can sell an inferior product exactly once.** Consumers who are disappointed simply don't repurchase, which is a direct financial deterrent for the manufacturer — meaning the single most effective lever for increasing sales, in his experience, is actually improving the product, not writing cleverer copy around a flawed one.
- His closing complaint, aimed squarely at his own industry: **most advertising doesn't give the reader enough real information to make a decision.** When he had to buy a new car, six months of reading car ads gave him "fatuous slogans and flatulent generalities" instead of facts — he contrasts this with his own fact-driven Rolls-Royce and Mercedes campaigns, both of which sold well on small budgets by simply telling the truth in detail.

### Chapter 20: I predict 13 changes (brief — a period piece)

Ogilvy's 1983 predictions for advertising's future are interesting mainly as a historical artifact rather than a technique source — some aged well (better research, more overseas creative talent, multinational brands adapting centrally-produced campaigns to local culture), others did not (he expected billboards to be abolished and political advertising to clean up its act; neither happened). Worth a glance for perspective on how hard durable prediction is, not for actionable guidance.

### Reading list (brief)

Ogilvy's own bibliography for further reading includes several titles already covered elsewhere in this skill's references — Caples' *Tested Advertising Methods*, Rosser Reeves' *Reality in Advertising*, Strunk & White's *The Elements of Style*, and his own *Confessions of an Advertising Man* — plus titles on direct marketing, plain writing, and the Spero book on dishonest political advertising discussed above.
