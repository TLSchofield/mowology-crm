# Bencivenga Bullets — Proof, Reason-Why and Offers, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of Gary Bencivenga's free e-letter *Bencivenga Bullets* (22 issues, Accountable Advertising, archived 2007). Bencivenga's stated record is beating client controls by 25 to 100 percent or more; each issue teaches one idea, backed by split-run results rather than opinion. **Source caveat:** this is an e-letter archive, not a book. Several issues (B#8, B#11, B#13, B#22) are life-philosophy pieces with a marketing coda, and B#2 is mostly a promotion for the archive. Despite the name, the letters contain **no instruction on writing "bullets" or fascinations** as a copy device; section 9 reconstructs the anatomy from the teaser examples he actually uses, and says so. Issue numbers are cited as [B#n].

Use this reference when you need to **make a claim believable, build a reason-why argument, choose or strengthen an offer or guarantee, or decide what to research before writing**, rather than for a full long-copy method (`sugarman-handbook.md`) or a headline taxonomy (`bly-copywriters-handbook.md`).

## 1. Bencivenga's Stance

Four beliefs from the first issue that every later one serves [B#1]:

1. Advertising exists to sell, not to win applause or awards.
2. One measurement outweighs a thousand opinions; everything taught came from paid split-run tests.
3. Sell with integrity. The strongest ads tell the truth dramatically; you never need to lie, shortchange or cut corners.
4. The reader's own ability is real, so each issue carries a creativity or productivity tip alongside the selling lesson.

He adds the 80/20 rule as editorial policy [B#2]: a handful of factors produce most of the response, so master the vital few (proof, reason-why, offer, research) instead of collecting hundreds of tips, and re-read those few until they are habits.

*Apply:* before adding a technique to a page, ask whether it belongs to the vital few or the trivial many.

## 2. The Credo Technique

State plainly and boldly what you believe and what you specialise in, especially in a welcome letter or first contact [B#1]. Borrowing Tom Watson's line that a great company needs a religion, he calls it *credo* ("I believe"). Standing for something specific separates you from me-too competitors who try to be everything to everyone and mean nothing to anyone; it also attracts the like-minded, who become the most loyal customers.

How he does it in the same issue:
- Number the beliefs (he uses four): one sentence each, then one sentence of consequence ("this is why…").
- Make one belief a promise about *how you operate* (his: results from measurement) and one about *ethics* (his: sell with integrity).
- Close by inviting the reader to self-select: if you share these, we are kindred spirits.
- Repeat it as identity, not a one-off (his footer says "a hype-free zone" and "not an affiliate for anyone" on every issue).

For a landscaping firm pitching strata councils this is the "how we work" block on the proposal or about page: three or four numbered commitments (show up on the scheduled day, photograph every visit, quote extras in writing first) that a council can hold you to and a low bidder will not match.

*Apply:* write your credo as numbered beliefs with consequences, and put it where a first-time reader meets you.

## 3. "Yeah, Sure": Proof Over Claims

The two most powerful words in advertising are the ones the prospect thinks at any big claim: "yeah, sure" [B#3]. The classic books taught ever-bigger benefits and words like FREE and NEW to an under-marketed public; today those words trip a mental spam filter.

The rule, stated in B#3 and repeated in B#7 as the most reliable response-booster he knows: never make your claim bigger than your proof, and join claim and proof "at the hip" in the headline so neither appears without the other.

- Prospects ask two questions: is this of interest to me, and is it believable or hype? Most copywriters work only on the first [B#7].
- Turning up the promise makes it look like a bigger pile of hype; raising the proof sets you apart and earns further reading [B#7].
- Testimonials help but are themselves overused; find every other proof element you can [B#3].
- Avoid phrases so worn they trigger the alarm on sight (get rich quick, lose weight fast), and drop exclamation marks [B#3].
- Candor is "gloriously effective": a requirement that is only moderately easy is *more* believable than a trivial one [B#3].
- Caples' definition, which he adopts: a believable promise to the right audience. Without belief nobody buys; without a hint of proof in the headline most never read on [B#7].

**The jury test** [B#7]: read every sentence as a skeptic and ask what proof a fair-minded jury would need to accept it beyond reasonable doubt. Patch the holes your own objections find; otherwise the prospect finds them. Every market is searching for someone to believe in; be that person.

For service copy: the strata proposal leads with what can be checked (years on that property type, named references, photographed visits, insurance and WorkSafe numbers), not "reliable" and "professional."

*Apply:* write the proof beside every claim; where none exists, shrink the claim until it fits the proof you have.

## 4. Headlines

No taxonomy here (use Bly). Instead, a few tested constructions and a way to predict winners.

**Predict the winner by proof** [B#7]. Faced with two headlines, ask which offers the more compelling proof. The Caples aspirin test: "Tension Headache?" against a headline asking what doctors do when they get headaches; the second won by 71 percent because the single word "doctors" raised the proof. The formula has kept working for decades, up to a current health-newsletter control about how doctors stay well while treating sick people all day, which combines promise, proof, curiosity and an immediate reward for reading.

**IF/THEN** [B#3]. State a requirement the prospect must meet, then a strong promise: "If you've got 20 minutes a month, I guarantee…" The IF clause switches off the "yeah, sure" alarm even when the promise is large. Formula: a reasonably easy requirement, followed by a strong promise; pay off in the body why so little effort produces the result. Tested wins: a financial newsletter (beat "The Millionaire Maker" by a wide margin and ran for years), a weight-loss newsletter using the same construction with only the promise changed, and a long-running speedwriting ad written in the shorthand it taught.

**Adapt a proven winner** [B#6]. "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?" was unbeaten for 40 years; he has adapted it repeatedly (his health-newsletter version asked about mistakes with vitamins) and says it gives a breakthrough every time. The key: fill the body with the common mistakes people make when they lack your product. It transfers directly to a lawn-care page ("Do you make these mistakes with your lawn in spring?") followed by the mistakes crews see on every property.

**Rules in passing:** puns in headlines are advertising malpractice [B#19]; most of Schwab's famous 100 headlines are worn out, and after testing he would not use 90 of them [B#15], so proven structure is not proven wording; put the reason-why in the headline when you can (Kleenex absorbs more *because* two layers thick; Slice tastes better *because* 10 percent fruit juice) [B#9].

*Apply:* draft the headline as claim + proof element; try an IF/THEN and a "Do you make these mistakes" version; pick by which carries the most proof.

## 5. Reason-Why and the Word "Because"

He calls himself a reason-why copywriter in the Ogilvy line and says the two most persuasive words in advertising are "reason why" [B#9]. Copy must give reasons in three places:

1. **Why you:** why your product beats every alternative, *including doing nothing*.
2. **Why true:** reasons to believe what you say.
3. **Why now:** reasons to act today.

Audit every underperforming piece against those three [B#9]. Slice took 7 percent of a $30 billion market on one proof element in the headline (10 percent fruit juice); strip it and "a better-tasting soft drink" is a bland claim. A half-price sale slides off the mind, but 50 percent off *because of the fire* gives a rationale the reader can accept.

**The trigger word** [B#10]. "Because" makes reasons land. Langer's copier experiment (via Cialdini): a request plus "because I'm in a rush" got 94 percent compliance, the bare request 60 percent, and "because" followed by a non-reason still 93 percent. His rules: fill copy with "because" clauses, but since readers are more deliberative on the page than in a queue, follow each with a genuinely compelling reason; stack them (he rewrites "you'll be glad you did" as four consecutive "because" reasons); and note it is the most ethical persuasion available, since accurate reasons to act in self-interest build trust and repeat sales.

For a property manager: because we service twelve other stratas within 10 minutes of yours (why you), because every visit is photographed and logged to your portal (why true), because the spring aeration window closes in three weeks (why now).

*Apply:* every claim needs a "because" after it, and the page as a whole needs all three reasons.

## 6. Strategy Before Execution

The most important sentence in marketing, from Rosser Reeves: "a gifted product is mightier than a gifted pen" [B#19]. Bernbach's version: advertising cannot create a product advantage, only convey one.

- **For the client:** inventing the advantage is your job, not the copywriter's. Real money is made on repeat sales, and copy can get a trial but cannot make a customer delighted enough to reorder. Aim for a product so superior that once explained, demonstrated or sampled the only conclusion is "I want it."
- **For the copywriter:** the product's unique benefit is the star of the ad. Research deeply before writing a word (section 7). Resist being clever, cute or entertaining. A jockey cannot carry the horse, so learn to spot strong products and get assigned to them.

**The controversy** [B#20]. Readers objected that the *prospect* should be the star. His answer, via Reeves' USP: separate **strategy** (the unique benefit you will communicate) from **tactics** (how you present it). Decide strategy first, always. Tactics must put the prospect front and centre at every step, but a message built only on what the prospect obviously wants is the message every competitor sends, and dies of boredom. Research the product's unique strengths first; it may satisfy a different desire than you assumed. M&Ms: a journeyman would have sold chocolate taste; Reeves found the candy shell, sold "melt in your mouth, not in your hand," then dramatised it from the prospect's side (lunch boxes, glove compartments). Formula: brilliant strategy (message from unique benefits) + compelling execution (how that benefit improves the prospect's life) = blockbuster. Ogilvy closes it: what you say matters more than how you say it.

For a service business the "product" is the operation: scheduling, crew consistency, reporting. If nothing is genuinely different, fix the operation before writing the page.

*Apply:* write one sentence of strategy (the unique benefit) before any headline; if you cannot, the problem is upstream of the copy.

## 7. Research Before Writing

**The ten questions** [B#19]. Start every assignment like an investigative reporter, questioning the client and their best salespeople:

1. Why is the product made the way it is?
2. What consumer problems, desires and needs is it designed for?
3. What is special about it; why does it meet those needs better than the competition?
4. Who says so besides you?
5. What are the strongest proof elements that make the case believable?
6. What are all the best features, and how does each translate into a benefit?
7. With unlimited funds, how would you improve it?
8. Who are the heavy users, the 20 percent who generate 80 percent of sales?
9. What irresistible offers might trigger a jump in sales?
10. What premiums could be added to press the prospect's hot buttons?

**The six serving men** [B#14]. Kipling's What, Where, When, How, Why and Who: who are you advertising to, what does the product do for them, why is it superior, how can you prove it, where should you advertise, when. "Why" is the captain: ask one good why every day and gnaw on it. Come back with at least seven times more material than you can use; a factual nugget outweighs a hundred adjectives, and there are no boring subjects, only boring writers. Then sequence the facts, fasten each to a reader benefit, and polish.

**"What are we really selling?"** [B#4]. The master closer's five-word question. Grass seed sells a greener lawn; boilers sell warm winter nights at a 27 percent fuel saving; baseball tickets sell a father's memories. List every answer, then test an ad built around each of the best. Asked of the whole enterprise it opens new markets (railroads were in transportation; Jacuzzi moved from physical therapy to home hot tubs; refrigerators became kitchen decor). For landscaping, "a council meeting with no landscaping complaints on the agenda" beats "lawn maintenance" as the thing to sell.

**Identify wants, not demographics** [B#12]. People buy aspirin because they have a headache, not because they belong to a segment. Three ways to find wants: study best-seller lists in your market (titles show what people want and make premium ideas); watch films to sharpen your feel for the emotions that drive purchases; listen to your own heart, because others' respond to the same things. Reasons and proof are the skeleton of the copy, emotions its flesh and blood.

*Apply:* run the ten questions and 5W1H with whoever sells the service face to face, and do not write until you have more facts than you can use.

## 8. Offers, Guarantees and Premiums

**Which offer pulled best?** [B#21] Three identical discounts: 50 percent off, half price, buy one get one free. The bogo wins invariably; Bob Stone reports it beat the others by 40 percent. Variants: a creative bogo (a free ounce of silver with every ounce of gold) and the gogo (give one, get one free), used for a newsletter renewal where every gift subscription extended the giver's own.

**The fifteen-item checklist of proven offer ideas** [B#21]:

1. The offer is the second most important element after the list; copy is third. Spend as long on the offer as on the headline.
2. Hopkins' standard: make the offer so good only a lunatic would refuse.
3. Give a believable reason for the special offer (fire, anniversary, founder retiring, new service, overstock, seasonal tie-in, charity share, at-cost to win new customers). If you have none, find one; a reason gives the offer traction.
4. Sell one thing per ad and sell it thoroughly; the only exception is a catalogue or a set of closely related items.
5. But always offer a deluxe version. Some prospects always take the top tier, and the decision shifts from yes/no to A-or-B, lifting response and average order. Good/better/best works too.
6. Test whatever offer competitors keep repeating (they repeat it because it works), and also something dramatically more generous than anyone in the market.
7. Make the guarantee as strong as possible. Longer guarantees *reduce* refunds: one year beats one, three or six months on both response and refunds, and five years works better still, because the disappointed customer feels no urgency and usually never gets around to it.
8. Test a price ending in 7 (Ted Nicholas).
9. Continuity is where the money is: sell a membership, maintenance plan or subscription rather than a one-shot, and get permission to charge the card automatically so you initiate the repurchase.
10. The takeaway: a hard deadline or quantity limit boosts response, but only if you mean it; accept orders past the limit and you kill the device forever. (He advised capping a 500,000-subscriber newsletter at one million to drive demand; the publisher refused and never got near it.)
11. If prices are rising, say so; beat-the-increase works especially on the existing customer list.
12. Always offer a unique premium. Related premiums usually win for information products, but test: Dick Benson found the best premium for a gasoline credit card was steak knives.
13. An early-bird bonus for prompt reply. Offline, never fix a calendar date (a printing or postal delay kills it); say "within 7 days" and test 10, 15 or 30.
14. A mystery early-bird gift, described with tantalising copy, can outpull a specific description (section 9).
15. Other proven offers: free shipping, sample, trial, information, estimate, consultation, or "free money" (a coupon toward purchase). The common element is the word free, which lowers the customer's risk. He includes the free talent test ("Draw the Pirate").

His closing addition: let genuine affection for the customer show through every offer; they feel it and respond in kind [B#21].

**The monkey's fist** [B#15]. Every sale is two: the sale before the sale (getting an audience) and the main sale. Ships are moored by throwing a small weighted ball on a light line, then hauling in the heavy rope; likewise make the prospect's first step irresistibly easy rather than asking cold. Hopkins' coffee salesmen gave a free half-pound and returned a week later with another gift for the first order, because "any apparent effort to sell creates corresponding resistance." Ways to throw the fist: free sample, checkup, audit, analysis, first lesson, consultation, quiz, guide. The most versatile is **free, valuable, targeted information**, since it opens the sale and sets up the close (a carpet cleaner's dust-mite booklet, a chiropractor's back guide, a local real-estate trend report). The service-business equivalent is a free property walk-through with a written "what we found" report, which is both the fist and the proof.

**Loss framing** [B#13]. Fear of loss motivates more than desire for gain: "it costs you an extra 75 cents a day if you don't" beat "you can save 75 cents a day" by a wide margin (Rosenspan). Spell out what the prospect loses by not acting.

*Apply:* pick the offer before the headline; run it against the fifteen items; add an easy first step, a reason for the offer, a long guarantee and a real deadline.

## 9. Teasers, Open Loops and Blind Copy

Honest note: the letters teach nothing explicit about bullets or fascinations. What they *show* is the form in use, three ways.

**Archive teasers** [B#2]. To send readers to back issues he lists three items with one anatomy: a specific-sounding promise ("The Two Most Powerful Words in Advertising"), then a parenthetical that kills the obvious guess and deepens the gap ("No, they are not new and free"). The third adds a precise, odd number (11-fold, not 10, not 12) to signal a real mechanism. Anatomy: promise + rule out the reader's first guess + one concrete specific.

**The split-test quiz as open loop** [B#3, B#6, B#7]. He ends an issue with two real headlines, asks which won, gives a clue that points to a principle, and reveals the answer next issue. It works because the reader can *check* the principle against a real result: proof and curiosity in one package. On a service site this is a "which quote would you accept?" comparison with the reasoning below the fold.

**The mystery premium** [B#21]. His Richard Simmons early-bird gift is a worked example of blind copy: refuse to name the gift, list what it is *not* (not a potion, not a padlock for the fridge), state the outcome it produces (lasting motivation), pretend to almost let it slip, and make keeping the secret part of the fun. He used it because the literal description (a booklet of member testimonials) read flat; the blind version made readers want the club more than the gift.

*Apply:* when a fact reads flat, rule out the reader's guess, keep the mechanism hidden, attach one precise detail; when you have a real test result, turn it into a quiz.

## 10. Where to Sell

**Intercept prospects where they are already looking for someone like you** [B#16]. Nobody searches for "car accessories" (49 million results); they search for "fuzzy dice," so build a dedicated page and campaign per star product and match the selling process to the way people search. Go vertical and deep: one product, a full sales presentation, cross-sell once they are customers. Not "retirement planning" but retiring to Mexico on $500 a month; not "insurance" but flood insurance for homes in the Hamptons. Offline the same rule took a car dealer from 48th to 9th in its state and from a $250,000 loss to a $1.8 million profit in 18 months: decided buyers act within about 10 days and look first in the newspaper, so the budget moved from TV to newspaper ($5,000 to $25,000 a month). For landscaping: one page per searched service and property type ("strata landscaping [city]") rather than one "services" page.

**The seven-step online formula** [B#18]:
1. Carve out a niche; when in doubt go narrower.
2. Give something valuable away free within it (e-zine, course, tip series, mistakes-to-avoid list).
3. Promote the free thing everywhere (ads, articles, talks, press, take-ones, swaps).
4. Capture email addresses; never give the best tips away on the open site, only for a sign-up, with a free report as inducement.
5. Pile on value so readers open every issue; get personal, resist "sales lust."
6. Never sell hard in the e-zine. Dance the two-step: mention briefly that a product extends the tip just given, link out, and sell hard on the dedicated page. Selling too much, too soon, too often trains the list to delete you on sight (list suicide). His rule: "establish trust before you sell with lust."
7. Capture physical addresses with a free gift that must be posted (report, CD, book, sample). Halbert reported direct mail to online sign-ups pulled up to 400 percent higher sales than the same copy on the web.

*Apply:* one page per searched service, a free information offer to open the relationship, and sales messages only by the two-step.

## 11. Naming with a Built-In Benefit

The best names carry the benefit inside them [B#17]: Kleenex, Mr. Clean, Swiffer, Easy Off, Draft Dodgers, Blue Blockers, Gentle Dental, The Fresh Coat Paint Store. Painless Parker's dentistry chain (about 30 offices, 70 dentists, $3 million a year in his era) rested on a name so valuable that when regulators tried to ban the word "painless" he legally made it his first name. Rules: in a service business consider naming the company after yourself, because clients like dealing with the person whose name is on the door; run a name search and trademark anything unique; collect good names as a hobby.

*Apply:* name services and packages for the outcome ("Complaint-Free Strata Program"), not the activity.

## 12. Creativity and Working Method

**SCAMPER** [B#6]. Osborn's acronym gives 11 moves for a fresh take on any headline, offer, product, market or layout: **S**ubstitute (a new element for a tried one), **C**ombine (winning elements from two sources), **A**dapt (a winning headline or offer from another category), **M**odify, minify or magnify, **P**ut to other uses (who else could use this?), **E**liminate (drop something always included), **R**earrange, reverse or redefine. Doubleday substituted genres into one winning book-club offer and built an empire of niche clubs. Keep a swipe file per letter for when you must beat a strong control.

**The red shirts** [B#5]. You do not notice red shirts in a crowd until told to look for them. Intention facilitates perception, but only when you look for one thing specifically, exclusively and persistently. Decide the single thing that could most easily double response (a headline, a premium, a lead) and hunt for that alone until found.

**Setbacks as seeds** [B#8]. His biggest advances came from lost split-runs, each time by asking why the copy lost. The copy lesson is Uncle Jim's apples: a hail storm marked the crop, so a card in each box reframed the marks as proof of high-altitude growth and superior flavour; refunds fell and customers began requesting hail-marked apples. A defect explained honestly becomes a proof element. A bed that looks rough after a hard renovation gets a "why it looks like this now, what it will look like in six weeks" note rather than an apology.

**Luck** [B#13]. Build the risk muscle (Joyce Brothers' rule: "If you can take the worst, take the risk"); make persistence the job description (a top broker's "rule of nine": nine contacts before giving up, while rivals quit after three or four; apply it as nine hooks in the water via newsletters, mail, calls); and spend sixty seconds picturing the successful outcome before writing an ad or running a meeting.

**Humanity** [B#11]. Prospects are people; they will not care what you know until they know you care. Occasionally do something for your list with no sales purpose.

**From Bob Parsons' sixteen rules** [B#22]: measure everything of significance, because whatever is measured improves; anything not managed deteriorates; decide quickly; quantify the worst case rather than fearing an undefined one. Bencivenga's gloss: we need not so much to be taught as reminded.

*Apply:* when stuck, run SCAMPER on the offer and headline; when a test loses, write down why before the next draft.

## 13. Testing and Measurement

- Trade in measurements, not opinions; the letters cite only what was split-run tested [B#1].
- Predict before testing: the headline or theme with stronger proof usually wins, so use proof as the tiebreaker when you cannot afford the test [B#7].
- Test the constructions he names (IF/THEN, "Do you make these mistakes," a price ending in 7, early-bird windows of 7, 10, 15 or 30 days, related vs. unrelated premiums, longer guarantees) rather than assuming [B#3, B#6, B#21].
- Copy what competitors repeat, then test something far more generous [B#21].
- When you lose, extract the reason; the loss is the source of the next win [B#8].
- Never test a headline formula because a book lists it; most of Schwab's 100 are dead [B#15].
- Measure everything of significance and manage what you measure [B#22].

*Apply:* log every test with the predicted winner and the reason; the misses are the curriculum.

## 14. Bencivenga vs. Sugarman, Bly, Schwartz and the Other Legends

**With Hopkins and Caples** he is in direct line: reason-why, one thing per ad, offers too good to refuse, believable promise to the right audience, measurement over opinion. His modern correction: the big-benefit style they taught now trips the "yeah, sure" filter, so proof must rise where the promise once did.

**With Sugarman** (`sugarman-handbook.md`) they agree on curiosity, specifics, easy first steps and testing. Sugarman's method is the whole slide from first sentence to close; Bencivenga says almost nothing about structure and instead sharpens three components Sugarman treats lightly: proof welded to the claim, the "because" audit, and the offer checklist. Use Sugarman for the shape of the page, Bencivenga to harden the claims and choose the offer.

**With Bly** (`bly-copywriters-handbook.md`) they agree on clarity over cleverness and research first. Bly gives the headline taxonomy and the 4 U's; Bencivenga gives two constructions that beat controls (IF/THEN, borrowed proof) and the rule for choosing between drafts. Bly's "prove it" step becomes, in Bencivenga, a headline discipline.

**With Schwartz** (`schwartz-breakthrough-advertising.md`): Schwartz starts from mass desire and market sophistication; Bencivenga starts from the product's provable difference and works out to the prospect. Reconcile by doing both: find the desire (Schwartz), find the unique proof that satisfies it (Bencivenga), then execute (Sugarman or Bly).

**With Halbert and Kennedy** the shared ground is the free-information first step and the physical-mail follow-up; the 400 percent figure in B#18 is Halbert's own report.

**What only Bencivenga adds:** the credo technique; the "yeah, sure" diagnosis and jury test; the three reasons (why you, why true, why now) with the "because" evidence; the fifteen-item offer checklist and the longer-guarantee finding; the ten research questions; SCAMPER as a control-beating tool; hail-marked apples as the model for turning a defect into proof.
