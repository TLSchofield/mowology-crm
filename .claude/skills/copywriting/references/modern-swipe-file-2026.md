# Modern Swipe File (2026) — Current Homepages, Technique Only

Every named example elsewhere in this skill is from 1903 to 2019. This file bridges to now: real, currently-live homepages, fetched and read directly (not secondhand claims from a blog) on 2026-09-26, described for technique the way Sugarman's ad-example chapter does — no performance stats claimed (none of these are independently verifiable from outside the company), no copy reproduced beyond short phrases in quotes.

Three examples, deliberately different registers: a technical B2B tool speaking to practitioners (Linear), a data platform speaking to a broad marketing audience (Ahrefs), and a founder-voiced B2C/SMB tool (Basecamp). Read all three before writing a homepage — the differences in register are more instructive than any one of them alone.

---

## Linear — Technical Audience, Maximum Compression

**What it's selling:** Product-development software (was: "Linear" the issue tracker; now: "The product development system for teams and agents," explicitly repositioned for an AI-native audience).

**Technique:**
- **Headline states the category claim and the audience in one line, nothing else.** "The product development system for teams and agents" — no adjective, no benefit word, just what it is and who it's for (Dunford's market-category component, Vol. 17, stated as plainly as possible).
- **Subheadline adds exactly one qualifier**: "Purpose-built for planning and building products. Designed for the AI era." Two short sentences, both under ten words — this reads as Sugarman's short-first-sentence discipline applied to a subhead, not just an opener.
- **The hero visual does the demonstrating, the words do the labeling.** Section headers are two to four words each ("Intake and integrations," "AI and automations") — the copy trusts the product screenshot to make the actual case, and only names what's being shown.
- **Social proof is three named, senior, recognizable people at name-brand companies** (OpenAI, Ramp, Opendoor), each with one specific, short, non-generic line — none of the quotes are the generic "great tool!" pattern Bly warns against (§6, testimonial selection).
- **The close is a plain declarative claim with no hedge**: "Built for the future. Available today." — Hopkins' positive-framing rule (Ch. 18) in six words, immediately followed by the only two CTAs on the page ("Get started," "Contact sales").

**Lesson:** for a technical, high-context audience, the words can shrink almost to labels once the category and audience are named up front — the compression itself signals "built by people who respect your time," which is a claim about the product before a single feature is described.

## Ahrefs — Data-Heavy, Category-Repositioning Under Pressure

**What it's selling:** SEO/marketing data platform, visibly mid-repositioning from "SEO tool" to "AI-search visibility platform" as the category itself is being redefined by AI search changing how people find things.

**Technique:**
- **The headline claims the new, larger category before competitors do**: "Make your business discoverable—in search, AI, and beyond." This is Dunford's market-category move (Vol. 17) executed live, in public, by an established brand trying to escape a shrinking category ("SEO tool") for a growing one ("AI/search discoverability") — the same strategic move Hopkins made when he reframed a preventive product as a cure (§10 of the Hopkins file).
- **A real-time, specific number sits directly under the CTA**: "20,941 users joined Ahrefs in the last 7 days" — Hopkins' specificity rule (§7) in its most literal modern form: a precise, oddly-specific number reads as a live, checkable fact, not a marketing round number.
- **Authority is borrowed at scale before it's earned individually**: "Marketers at 44% of the Fortune 500 use Ahrefs" — a single sentence doing Cialdini's authority and social proof simultaneously (Vol. 15).
- **New, unproven AI features get their own contrastive framing rather than blending into the existing feature list**: "AI that creates results, not noise" — a direct, named contrast against a category-wide anxiety (AI features that are noise), addressing the objection before the reader can raise it (Sugarman's raise-and-resolve, `sugarman-handbook.md` §5b elements 8–9).
- **Segment-specific value props, one per named role** (Content Marketer, PPC Specialist, SEO/Link Builder, AEO Specialist...): each gets three bullet-length outcome claims in that role's own vocabulary — Dunford's target-segment component applied as a literal page section, not just a strategic exercise done once and forgotten.

**Lesson:** when the market category you built your brand on is being redefined out from under you, the homepage headline is where you stake the new category claim — and pairing it with one very specific, real-time number does more to make an ambitious claim credible than any amount of adjective-stacking.

## Basecamp — Founder Voice, Maximum Directness

**What it's selling:** Project management software, explicitly positioned against the entire category's complexity.

**Technique:**
- **The headline names the competitive alternative and dismisses it in the same breath**: "Basecamp is project management without all the nonsense." This is Dunford's competitive-alternatives component (Vol. 17) — "every other PM tool" — stated as a single dismissive noun phrase, "nonsense," rather than a feature comparison.
- **A first-person, signed letter from the actual co-founder/CEO opens the real argument**, not a marketing paragraph: "Hey there— Tell me if this sounds about right." This is Sugarman's harmony technique (`sugarman-handbook.md` §3, Chapter 7) executed almost verbatim, eighty years later — short true statements the reader nods along to ("you're juggling people, projects, and expectations... spreading everything across different apps... doesn't work") before the product is even named.
- **The origin story is the reason-why, not decoration**: "20 years ago we were in the same boat... So we invented Basecamp" — Hopkins' reason-why (Vol. 1) reframed as founder narrative instead of a technical mechanism, because the product's whole claim *is* "built by people who had your exact problem."
- **Nine real, named, attributed customer quotes, each stating a specific outcome** ("Productivity is up. Errors are down. Clients are happier" / "even the 80-year-olds on our committee are using it") — real names and specific roles throughout, exactly Bly's testimonial-selection rule (§6: real names, specific results, not generic praise) and the FTC's actual-customer requirement (`legal-compliance-guardrails.md` §1) both satisfied by the same choice.
- **Absurdly precise, large cumulative numbers as trust signal**: "1.7 billion to-dos," "15 billion notifications," "99.99% uptime" — Hopkins' specificity rule (§7) pushed to its logical extreme: numbers too large and too oddly specific to have been rounded for effect.
- **A dedicated section rebuts a category-wide skepticism directly by name**: "Remember when companies cared about service?" raises the reader's actual cynicism about SaaS support before answering it — Sugarman's candid-flaw opener (`sugarman-handbook.md` §3, Chapter 8) turned into a whole page section.
- **The letter closes with a plain, warm, unhedged sign-off**: "we'd be honored to have you as a customer... Have a great day!" — no urgency device, no fake scarcity; the confidence itself is the close.

**Lesson:** a founder-signed letter that talks like a person, admits the reader's cynicism about the category by name, and backs every claim with a real name or a specific number can carry an entire homepage with almost no traditional "marketing" language at all — the restraint is the technique.

## Cross-Cutting Observations (all three, 2026)

- **None of these three pages uses an exclamation point in a headline or subheadline.** Confidence reads as flat, declarative statement now, not enthusiasm — consistent with this skill's existing "remove exclamation points" rule (SKILL.md Quick Quality Check), and worth treating as a hard default for SaaS/B2B in particular.
- **Real-time or oddly-specific numbers (not round ones) are doing more trust-building work than testimonials alone** on two of the three pages (Ahrefs' live signup counter, Basecamp's precise cumulative stats) — a live or precise number is functioning the way Hopkins' 1923 "9 percent profit" claim functioned: too specific to have been made up.
- **Category repositioning is happening in public, in real time, on at least one of these pages** (Ahrefs) — worth checking, for any established client, whether their own category claim still matches where the market is actually moving, using Dunford's market-category question as the diagnostic.
- **AI is a live category anxiety text is now expected to address directly** (Ahrefs' "not noise," Linear's "and agents") rather than avoid — a 2026-specific instance of Sugarman's raise-and-resolve principle: readers already have the objection in mind, so naming it first reads as candor, not as inviting doubt.

**Apply:** before writing a new homepage, read the client's two or three closest real competitors' current live pages the same way — fetch them, don't guess from memory — and note which of the moves above they're already making, so the new page earns its place rather than repeating what every other page in the category already does.
