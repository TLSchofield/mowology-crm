# The Psychology of Advertising — Walter Dill Scott, Distilled

An original-wording distillation of *The Psychology of Advertising* (Small, Maynard & Co., 1908/1913) by Walter Dill Scott, Ph.D., Director of the Psychological Laboratory of Northwestern University. Scott was the first person to bring a working experimental-psychology lab — stopwatches, recall tests, mailed surveys, tabulated data — to bear on why advertisements work, publishing his first findings in 1903 and this fuller treatment in 1908–1913. Almost everything that came after in this field owes him a debt whether or not it says so.

Placed against the other legends in this skill: Hopkins (`legends-library.md`, Volume 1) arrived fifteen years later with "reason-why" copy and treated advertising as salesmanship in print — every claim needs a mechanism, every campaign needs a number. Scott asked a different question first: not "what's the argument?" but "what is actually happening in the reader's mind, and can I measure it?" He is explicitly skeptical of over-relying on reasoned argument at all (§1). Where Hopkins gave the profession its discipline, Scott gave it its psychology, and the line from his suggestion/instinct/habit vocabulary runs forward to Cialdini's *Influence* (1984): reciprocity, social proof, authority, liking, and commitment are Scott's mechanisms, re-derived under stricter lab conditions three-quarters of a century later. Use this file for the *why* under a persuasion technique; use Hopkins or Sugarman for the *how* of executing it.

Some of what Scott presents as settled science is 1908-vintage psychology that did not survive scrutiny — fixed-instinct catalogs, hard aesthetic ratios, flatly gendered claims about how women decide. These are flagged plainly as historical rather than folded in as current findings (§9).

## 1. Suggestion

Scott's central claim, and the one that puts him at odds with a "reason-why" view of advertising before that view even existed: most human action isn't the output of deliberation, it's suggestion acted on with less than the normal amount of thought. An idea proposed by another person or object and taken up almost without criticism is suggestion; the same idea reached by weighing evidence is reasoning. His flat statement of the point: **"We reason rarely, but act under suggestion constantly."**

He illustrates with his own case: months of exposure to a Chicago tailor's ads left him believing, when he finally ordered a suit, that a friend had recommended the shop — he'd never spoken to anyone about it, only absorbed the ad until its source was forgotten and reattributed to "someone I know." He calls this a "social illusion": repeated advertising gets mistaken for social consensus once its origin fades. The most powerful suggestions come from watching another person act (a model using the product), the next most powerful from a companion's words — hence testimonial devices, like a Postum ad where a "venerable doctor's" face implies an endorsement the copy never actually claims is his.

Scott warns against the era's growing enthusiasm for "reason-why" copy: valuable, but customers are moved more often because a purchase is suggested at the right moment than because they were argued into it. Suggestion and argument aren't opposed — he pairs a direct command ("Hand Sapolio should be on every washstand") with supporting reasons — but suggestion shouldn't be subordinated to persuasion.

**Apply:** don't just argue for the product — show the action you want imitated (someone already using it, satisfied), and give an explicit, simple command. Distrust an ad with a watertight argument that never once depicts anyone doing the thing you want the reader to do.

## 2. Memory

Scott opens with the era's memory research (Ebbinghaus): recollection fades fastest in the first seconds and minutes, so whatever a reader still holds after twenty minutes tends to last. From this he draws four principles for an ad that survives:

1. **Repetition** — a claim seen often gets fixed in memory, but crude identical repetition (a "Vitalized Phosphates" ad run unchanged for years) is the *expensive* way to do it.
2. **Intensity** — the more sharply an impression registers the first time, the fewer repeats it needs; color, humor, rhyme, and anything emotionally provocative all intensify it. First and last position — of a publication, or of the copy itself — beats the middle.
3. **Association** — the strongest of the four. A fact tied to something the reader already values is retained effortlessly (a bond's interest rate is unforgettable to an investor, forgettable to everyone else); an isolated fact costs real effort. Scott also flags getting a *response* — even a postcard — as a memory multiplier: acting on an ad fixes it in a way reading never does.
4. **Ingenuity** — clever names or number tricks ("Uneeda"; a tailor who built his identity around the number 33 — address, phone, price) work, but sparingly; overused, it backfires.

**Apply:** repeat a consistent core claim but vary the surface each time (Scott's "Cream of Wheat" chef reappears in every ad, never posed the same way) so repetition doesn't read as staleness. Lead and close with the strongest line. Give the reader something small to *do* — a completed action outlasts a read impression.

## 3. Feelings and emotions

Pleasure and pain aren't just mental states for Scott — they're bodily ones with opposite physiological signatures. Pleasure measurably increases heart efficiency and breathing depth, and the body "opens" (limbs extend, posture expands), which correlates with an expanded, suggestible, receptive mind, prompt to act. Pain contracts the body inward and correlates with a suspicious mind that "refuses to receive suggestions." Scott quotes Dickens on the "well-breakfasted juryman" versus the hungry one who "always finds for the plaintiff" to make the point that judgment is mood-dependent, not purely logical — priming, a century before the term existed.

He extends this into aesthetics: certain proportions test as measurably more pleasing than others, and he reports bisected-line and rectangle experiments converging on the classical "golden section" (roughly 3:5) as the preferred ratio for a shape needing both unity and diversity — applied directly to choosing ad-space shapes and layout proportions.

**Apply:** put the reader in a comfortable, unhurried state before asking for a decision — clutter and aggressive layout trigger the same defensive "contraction" Scott describes for pain, and a defensive reader resists every suggestion that follows. A cramped layout isn't a neutral choice, it's a hostile one.

## 4. Appeals to sympathy

Sympathy is strongest, in Scott's account, for people we recognize as being like us or like who we want to become — not for people we look down on. His paired examples still land: tourists he doesn't identify with leave him cold, while a couple who "approximate my ideals" make him want to imitate their action. Showing the product used by a class the reader considers beneath them actively repels — he cites a shoe ad whose lower-status figure made him refuse the brand outright.

He also compares sorrow- and joy-based appeals: depicting hardship can drive action harder than depicting pleasure (charitable and political appeals lean on it constantly), but there's a line — a "diseased lungs" cough-syrup ad that graphically listed symptoms disgusted rather than persuaded, because cultural norms suppress open displays of suffering and an ad that breaks them reads as an assault, not an appeal.

**Apply:** show the buyer becoming the aspirational version of themselves, not a generic or low-status stand-in for the "before" state. Hardship-framed appeals can outperform pure-benefit ones, but restraint reads as sympathy where graphic detail reads as attack.

## 5. Human instincts

Scott catalogs the instincts he sees advertisers already exploiting — acting to produce an end without foreseeing it or being taught to. Modern psychology abandoned this "fixed instinct" model within a few decades (§9), but the motive list still works as a copy checklist:

- **Bodily self-preservation** — taste overrides reasoned self-interest ("it makes you eat" sold harder than any health claim). *Apply:* sensory/immediate language often outperforms a rational-benefit claim.
- **Ownership, including clothing** — we identify with possessions almost as body-extensions. *Apply:* "your," "own" outpulls abstract description.
- **Hoarding/acquisitiveness** — collecting for its own pleasure, not calculated utility (Scott's squirrel; a literal miser's den inventory). *Apply:* points, streaks, "collect the set" tap a real, old motive.
- **Hunting/competitive instinct** — an appetite for contest and rivalry outliving its economic necessity (hunters give away the meat; the thrill was the point). *Apply:* leaderboards, "beat your best," limited slots engage a real system independent of payoff.
- **Constructive instinct** — the urge to build, remodel, improve by hand. *Apply:* DIY and customization sell the process, not just the object.
- **Parental instinct** — parents pay disproportionately, price resistance suspended, for anything serving a child's needs. *Apply:* still true commercially; treat Scott's florid maternal-self-sacrifice passage as dated rhetoric (§9), not evidence — the spending mechanism is the durable part.
- **Gregariousness/social approval** — we want to be seen and well-regarded; purchases follow a reference group, not private preference alone. *Apply:* show the right social context, and never one your reader wants distance from.
- **Exercise instinct** — organs and faculties (physical and mental) seek their own use. *Apply:* "challenge yourself" framing taps a real developmental drive.
- **Moral/religious instinct** — a book that failed as *The Wonders of Nature* sold a second printing retitled *...the Architecture of God*. *Apply:* framing a claim inside a value system the reader already holds beats stating it neutrally.
- **Aesthetic instinct** — beauty persuades even when the reader never credits it consciously. *Apply:* the best design work is often invisible to the person it's working on.
- **Curiosity** — an unresolved question compels attention disproportionate to its stakes ("What did the woggle bug say?"; puzzle and prize-guess ads). *Apply:* an open loop the reader wants closed is a cheap, durable attention device.

## 6. The will: a decision model, and five types of deciders

Scott breaks any deliberate purchase into five steps: (a) an idea of the possible ends — the product, clearly presented; (b) an idea of the means to each end — exactly how and where to buy it, which he flags as one of the *most common* weaknesses in period advertising; (c) a felt sense of each end's worth; (d) a comparison of ends and means; (e) a choice, then effort to carry it out. At (d) the advertiser's job isn't to attack competitors but to make your own case strong enough it doesn't suffer by comparison; at (e), fill the reader's mind with only your product and supply an explicit device — his example is the return coupon or a direct command — to force closure.

He then borrows William James's five decision types and maps each to an advertising approach:

1. **Reasonable type** — waits until arguments settle a clear balance, then acts without hesitation. Served by a fully reasoned case; matters most for expensive, rare purchases (pianos, insurance — high-consideration, in modern terms).
2. **Drift type** — decides by whatever's in front of them once they're tired of indecision. Scott's blunt point: even a poor ad can "succeed" here purely by being present at the right instant — a caution against crediting creative for wins that were really about placement and timing.
3. **Impulsive type** — decision by sudden emotional discharge, driven by artistic/sentimental cues rather than argument. (Scott attributes this mode more to women — a period stereotype, flagged in §9 — but the mode itself, low-deliberation and emotionally triggered, is real and category-general.)
4. **Mood-shift type** — the same ad, ignored for months, suddenly persuades once the reader's circumstances change (insurance, big commitments, "someday" purchases). *Apply:* don't discard a prospect as unresponsive — the moment may simply not have arrived; an argument for retargeting, not rewriting.
5. **Effortful-resolve type** — both options stay in view, chosen by a "slow dead heave" of will. Least useful to advertisers — mainly the mode to avoid triggering, since it means a competitor is still in the reader's mind.

**Apply:** diagnose which decision mode your category actually triggers — a reasoned dossier wastes effort on an impulsive-mode purchase, and a purely emotional pitch under-serves a reasonable-type buyer of a rare, expensive item.

## 7. Habit: the drive-wheel

Scott's explanation of habit is physical: every thought or action leaves a "crease" in plastic nerve tissue, the way a fold persists in paper — easier the second time because the crease exists. His practical conclusion: the *first* purchase is worth far more than its own price, because a formed habit becomes a "drive-wheel" producing future purchases with steadily less persuasive effort. He explicitly criticizes advertisers who cut spend the moment a habit forms — it still needs reinforcing, and new buyers still need converting, so stopping leaves compounding value on the table.

**Apply:** budget the first conversion against the lifetime value of the habit it creates, not against its own margin; keep reminder/retention marketing running after conversion rather than treating "they bought once" as the finish line.

## 8. Progressive thinking: Scott's method

Scott lays out a four-step method advertisers should run deliberately: **observation, classification, inference, application** — the method of any applied science. His worked example is the closest thing here to modern qualitative research: an advertising manager collected thousands of reader letters on which ads interested them and why, and had Scott code the open-ended reasons into categories. Ranked by frequency in one month: reliability (607 mentions), financial/value considerations (508), quality of the ad's construction (418), present need (408) — then fed back into planning future campaigns, "an endless chain of observation, classification, inference and application." He also rebuts critics who said psychology "can't invent" anything: true, but knowing the laws of electricity doesn't make you an Edison either — science multiplies what ingenuity can do, it doesn't replace it.

**Apply:** treat open-ended customer feedback (reviews, tickets, survey free-text) as data to be systematically coded into ranked categories, not anecdotes to cherry-pick — the same move as modern voice-of-customer analysis, done by hand with a stack of letters.

## 9. Attention value, mortality rate, and the questionnaire method

Three Scott investigations are genuinely early market research, not just observation:

**Attention value of ad size.** Scott predicted from a lab test (flashing a card of letters for 1/25 second; recall of any one drops as competing letters are added) that a full page should have a large, measurable recall edge over a small ad — then verified it by handing the same magazine to ~500 people, having them read normally, then unexpectedly asking them to write down every ad recalled. Full-page ads (excluding books/periodicals) were recalled ~20 times per 500 readers; small ads, under once. Predict from theory, then test with a surprise recall task — a direct ancestor of modern ad-recall testing.

**Mortality rate of advertisers.** Across eight years of the *Ladies' Home Journal*, Scott tracked annual ad spend against how long firms kept advertising at all: ~56 lines/year and firms typically quit after one; ~600 lines/year and firms were still advertising eight years later. His logic: advertisers stop paying for what doesn't work, so a firm's own renewal behavior is a market-validated signal of return, independent of anyone's stated opinion.

**The questionnaire method.** A mailed survey to 4,000 prominent Chicago professionals (plus a 1,000-name comparison list) on newspaper habits, response rate over 50%. Scott is unusually self-aware for 1908: he flags that the size of the answer blank silently biases responses; used a disinterested university sender rather than the advertiser to raise honesty; converted ranked open preferences into comparable numbers with a weighted score (5 points for first choice down to 1); distinguished a reader's primary paper from merely "subsidiary" ones. Finding: readers spend ~15 minutes a day split across several papers, so any ad gets a glance, not a careful read — driving the recommendation that an ad must land in a single glance.

**Apply:** verify ad-size assumptions with a real recall test rather than instinct; treat renewal/churn as revealed-preference evidence; and if you survey your audience, watch your own instrument for bias — answer-space size, who's asking, how open rankings get scored — as carefully as the answers themselves.

## 10. Unconscious influence: captive attention and constructed perception

**Captive attention.** Scott's streetcar-advertising chapter argues its effectiveness has nothing to do with better creative and everything to do with *forced idle time*: a magazine reader spends roughly 10% of reading time on ads because competing content is everywhere on the page, but a streetcar passenger has nothing else acceptable to look at, so re-reads the same placard out of sheer boredom, at no cost to the advertiser. He pairs this with an optical-illusion example (an arrow-tipped line judged longer than an equal one) to argue people routinely make judgments driven by an unconscious factor, then *invent* a plausible reason afterward, believing it was reasoned all along — a plainspoken account of what would later be called motivated reasoning.

**Constructed perception (the "tasting game").** Blindfolded subjects fed careful, unseasoned samples routinely can't tell pork broth from chicken, or strawberry syrup from peach — much of what feels like a taste preference is really association: turkey outsells pork not because it tastes better blind, but because it carries a "halo" of feasts and rarity that pork doesn't. A direct ancestor of the modern blind-taste-test finding (Pepsi Challenge and descendants) that branding changes the actual experience, not just the willingness to buy.

**Apply:** look for genuinely captive moments in your own funnel (a loading screen, a queue, an empty onboarding step) where repeated exposure is free. Don't assume a category's premium is a real sensory or functional difference — much of it is association, so you can compete on narrative against a functionally identical rival. And treat customers' stated reasons for buying with some skepticism; people confidently report causes that weren't the actual cause.

## 11. What's dated or superseded

Several specific claims here are 1908-vintage science that did not survive, and shouldn't be carried into modern practice as current findings:

- **Fixed-instinct psychology itself.** Scott's "instinct" framework — a discrete, inborn instinct behind each behavior, no learning involved — was largely abandoned within a few decades, replaced by drive theory and later by modern motivation research. Treat §5 as a historically useful checklist of persistent motives, not a validated model of the mind.
- **The golden-section formula.** Real experiments (bisected-line judgments) get over-extended into treating ~3:5 as close to a governing law of beauty. Modern design research treats proportion preference as real but weaker and more culturally contingent than Scott's confident percentages suggest — one input, not a formula to satisfy.
- **Gendered decision-making claims.** Scott states outright that women decide more often by sudden emotional impulse ("a woman's reason") and are "more susceptible to suggestion." Unsupported period stereotyping, not evidence — discard it. The decision-mode taxonomy in §6 still works once stripped of the gender attribution: categorize by purchase context and involvement, not by the buyer's sex.
- **The maternal-instinct passage.** Scott quotes a period author describing motherhood as total, biologically fixed self-erasure. Edwardian gender ideology presented as science. The commercial mechanism he's actually pointing at — parents suspend ordinary price sensitivity for their children — is separable and remains true.
- **"Native memory retentiveness is unchangeable."** Scott claims memory training doesn't work because raw capacity is fixed. Modern cognitive science shows *techniques* (spaced repetition, elaborative encoding) meaningfully improve retention. His four practical principles for a memorable ad (§2) hold up regardless of the theory underneath them.

## 12. Problem → Scott Tool

| Problem | Scott tool |
|---|---|
| Copy argues well but nobody converts | Suggestion (§1) — show the action, don't just make the case |
| Message doesn't survive past one exposure | Memory's four principles (§2) — repeat with variation, tie to what's already valued, get a response |
| Reader feels talked at or defensive | Feelings and emotions (§3) — clutter triggers the same withdrawal as pain |
| Testimonial or social proof feels generic | Appeals to sympathy (§4) — show the aspirational self, not a generic stand-in |
| Can't find the emotional hook for a category | Human instincts catalogue (§5) |
| A prospect is "still thinking about it" | The Will's five decision types (§6) — diagnose the mode before choosing an approach |
| First purchase converts but nobody returns | Habit (§7) — budget the sale against lifetime value, reinforce after conversion |
| No process for turning feedback into direction | Progressive thinking (§8) — observe, classify, infer, apply |
| Unsure whether ad size/format works | Attention Value experiments (§9) |
| Deciding whether a channel is worth trusting | Mortality Rate / renewal-as-signal (§9) |
| Need real audience data instead of guesses | The Questionnaire Method (§9) — watch your instrument for bias |
| A funnel step has unclaimed idle attention | Captive attention (§10) |
| Product is hard to differentiate on substance | Constructed perception / the tasting game (§10) |

## 13. Where this sits in the lineage

Scott is the point where advertising first borrowed its authority from a laboratory instead of a salesman's instinct — a university psychologist running recall experiments with a stopwatch and coding thousands of reader letters by hand, fifteen years before Hopkins wrote a word of *Scientific Advertising*. Hopkins inherited Scott's insistence on testing and evidence but recast it as salesmanship in print — reason-why, specificity, proof — and it was Hopkins' frame, not Scott's suggestion-and-instinct frame, that dominated the profession for the next sixty years. Cialdini's *Influence* (1984) reads less like a departure from Hopkins and more like a return to Scott: reciprocity, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and commitment/consistency are Scott's suggestion, sympathy-driven imitation, instinct-driven acquisitiveness and rivalry, and habit, independently re-derived under stricter conditions three-quarters of a century later. Sugarman and modern conversion copywriting inherit both halves — Hopkins' discipline of proof and specificity, and Scott's (and Cialdini's) vocabulary of psychological triggers — which is why so much of what Scott groped toward with a stopwatch and a mailed questionnaire in 1903 now reads, in the "Apply" lines above, as unremarkable modern practice.
