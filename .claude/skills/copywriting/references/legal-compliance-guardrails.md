# Legal & Compliance Guardrails for Copy

A pre-ship checklist for the claims and mechanics most likely to create real legal exposure in the US — the FTC Act's ban on "unfair or deceptive acts," the Endorsement Guides, CAN-SPAM, and a handful of product-specific rules. **This is not legal advice and this skill does not replace a lawyer.** Its job is narrower: catch the patterns that predictably draw FTC attention or a demand letter, flag them before the copy ships, and say plainly when a human lawyer needs to sign off before publishing. Treat every "flag" below as "stop and ask," not as permission to proceed once the wording is softened.

Facts here reflect FTC guidance as of 2026; rules in active rulemaking (noted below) may change — verify current status before relying on this for a real campaign, especially anything already in a gray zone.

## 1. Testimonials & Endorsements

Every testimonial or endorsement used in copy must be truthful and reflect a real, honest opinion — not written by the marketer and attributed to a customer, not selectively edited to reverse its meaning, and not implying a typical result if the result shown is unusual (see §2). Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised 2023) and the 2024 Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule:

- **Any material connection must be disclosed** — if the person was paid, given a free product, given a discount, or is an employee/affiliate, that connection has to be clearly and conspicuously stated, next to the testimonial itself, not buried in a footer link or a separate terms page.
- **"Clear and conspicuous" is the actual legal standard** — a disclosure a reasonable consumer could miss (tiny font, low contrast, off to the side) doesn't count, even if it's technically present.
- **This applies everywhere the testimonial appears** — social media, a testimonial widget, an influencer post, a case study — the disclosure travels with the content, not just the original placement.
- **Fake or fabricated reviews are a bright line** — writing a customer quote yourself and attributing it to a real or invented customer is treated by the FTC as a deceptive practice on its own, regardless of whether the underlying claim is true.

**Apply:** for every testimonial in a draft, confirm (a) it's a real customer's real words, (b) any payment/freebie/employment relationship is disclosed next to it, and (c) it isn't implied to be typical unless it actually is.

**Source:** [FTC — Endorsements, Influencers, and Reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/endorsements-influencers-reviews); [16 CFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255).

## 2. Claims That Need a Number Behind Them

The FTC's general deception standard applies to any express or implied claim likely to mislead a reasonable consumer about something material — before an ad runs, the advertiser must already have adequate substantiation for every objective claim in it, not evidence gathered afterward to defend it if challenged.

- **A specific claim ("cuts setup time by 40%") requires a real basis for that number** *before* the ad runs, not just a hope it's roughly true.
- **"Up to X" and "as low as X" claims are a known enforcement target.** The FTC's 2024 Arise Virtual Solutions settlement addressed exactly this pattern: if only a small fraction of customers achieve the advertised ceiling or floor, the claim itself can be deceptive even if technically someone somewhere hit that number.
- **The historical bar is high, not superstitious:** Hopkins' 1923 rule — a specific number is believed at face value, a vague claim is discounted — cuts both ways. A specific number that isn't actually substantiated is a specific *lie*, not just a specific claim, and it invites more scrutiny than a vague one, not less.

**Apply:** for any "up to," "as low as," "X% faster/cheaper/better," or similar claim, ask what percentage of customers/cases actually the number applies to. If it's a best case dressed up as a headline, flag it for the client to either substantiate broadly or reword as the specific case it actually represents ("our fastest customers cut setup time by 40%").

**Source:** [FTC deception standard](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing); [Hudson Cook — "Up To" Advertising Claims](https://www.hudsoncook.com/article/ftc-settlement-offers-plenty-to-think-about-regarding-up-to-advertising-claims/).

## 3. Pricing & Discount Claims

Reference pricing (a struck-through "was" price next to a lower "now" price) has been an FTC enforcement target since its first pricing guides in 1958, and remains active — a 2026 warning letter went to 97 auto dealership groups over exactly this pattern.

- **A "was" price must be a real price the item actually sold at for a meaningful period**, not an inflated number invented specifically to make the discount look bigger.
- **A "compare at" or "elsewhere" price must reflect a real, current market price**, not a stale or fabricated one.
- **Advertised promotional prices that are effectively unavailable to most buyers** (bait pricing) are their own deception category — the LasikPlus case (an "as low as $250" procedure that typically cost far more) resulted in an FTC order specifically on this pattern.

**Apply:** before using a strikethrough or "compare at" price, confirm the reference price is real and recent. Flag any promotional price that most customers won't actually qualify for.

**Source:** [FTC — Deceptive Pricing warning to auto dealers, 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-warns-97-auto-dealership-groups-about-deceptive-pricing); [FTC — LasikPlus order, 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-order-requires-lasikplus-pay-its-bait-switch-eye-surgery-ads).

## 4. Health, Wellness & Supplement Claims

The FTC's December 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance replaced the older 1998 dietary-supplement guide and now explicitly covers *any* health-related product — supplements, food, OTC products, health apps, wearables, diagnostic tools.

- **Health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence"** — the FTC's own definition typically means well-controlled human clinical trials, not animal studies, not in-vitro research, and not a single industry-funded study cherry-picked to support the claim.
- **This applies to implied claims, not just explicit ones** — a headline that strongly implies a cure or prevention effect can be judged the same as an explicit medical claim, even without a literal diagnosis-related word.
- **"Not evaluated by the FDA" disclaimers don't rescue an unsubstantiated claim** — a disclaimer doesn't cure a deceptive headline; it just sits next to it.

**Apply:** any claim that a product treats, prevents, cures, or meaningfully affects a health condition needs a documented, appropriate clinical basis before it ships — route to the client's regulatory/legal contact, don't draft around it with softer language and assume that's sufficient.

**Source:** [FTC — Health Products Compliance Guidance, 2022](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Health-Products-Compliance-Guidance.pdf).

## 5. Earnings, Income & "Get Rich" Claims

This is an area of **active rulemaking** as of 2026 — the FTC proposed (January 2025) an Earnings Claim Rule targeting multi-level marketing and an expanded Business Opportunity Rule covering business coaching and "money-making opportunity" sellers more broadly. Current enforcement already applies regardless of the new rule's final status.

- **Any specific income or earnings figure in copy for a business opportunity, coaching program, or MLM needs written substantiation on file**, available on request, in the same language the claim was made in.
- **Testimonials showing atypical results ("I made $50k in my first month") need a clear disclosure of what's typical**, not just a small-print "results not typical" — the FTC's direction here is toward requiring the *actual* typical result to be disclosed, not just a disclaimer that atypical results exist.
- **Calling a money-making opportunity an "employment opportunity" when it isn't one is treated as a standalone misrepresentation.**

**Apply:** for any coaching, MLM, "side hustle," or business-opportunity copy containing a specific dollar figure, confirm the client has documented substantiation and a real typical-result disclosure ready, and flag this category for legal review given the rule is actively in flux.

**Source:** [FTC — Proposed Earnings Claim Rule, Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-proposes-rule-changes-new-rule-deter-deceptive-earnings-claims-multilevel-marketers-money-making-opportunity-sellers).

## 6. "Made in USA" and Origin Claims

Codified in the FTC's 2021 Made in USA Labeling Rule, with heightened enforcement attention flagged again in 2026.

- **An unqualified "Made in USA" claim requires "all or virtually all"**: final assembly/processing in the US, all significant processing in the US, and all or virtually all components/ingredients sourced in the US.
- **Civil penalties are per-violation and substantial** (statutory maximum adjusts annually; check current FTC guidance rather than assuming a fixed number).
- **The obligation is ongoing** — if sourcing changes and the claim stops being true, marketing has to be updated; a claim that was accurate when written doesn't stay compliant on its own.

**Apply:** never write an unqualified "Made in USA" line without direct confirmation from the client on sourcing and assembly location; if any component is foreign-sourced, use a qualified claim ("Assembled in the USA from imported parts") instead, and confirm the client is comfortable with that wording.

**Source:** [FTC — Made in USA Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/made-in-usa).

## 7. Subscriptions, Free Trials & Auto-Renewal

The FTC's 2024 "Click-to-Cancel" Rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025 on procedural grounds, and a new rulemaking process (ANPRM) opened January 2026 — the specific rule is unsettled, but **ROSCA (the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act) remains fully in force** and covers the same core territory.

- **Three ROSCA conditions apply to any internet-based subscription/auto-renewal offer:** (1) clear and conspicuous disclosure of all material terms *before* billing information is collected, (2) the consumer's express informed consent before any charge, and (3) a simple mechanism to cancel and stop future charges.
- **"Simple mechanism to cancel" is judged against the sign-up experience** — a subscription that's one click to start and requires a phone call, a retention gauntlet, or a multi-step form to cancel is a live enforcement target regardless of the specific rule's status.
- **Free-trial copy must disclose the conversion to paid clearly** — the price, the date it starts, and how to avoid it, stated before the customer enters payment details, not only in the confirmation email afterward.

**Apply:** for any free-trial or subscription offer, confirm the actual cancellation flow is genuinely as easy as the sign-up flow before the copy promises "cancel anytime" — copy shouldn't promise an ease of cancellation the product doesn't deliver.

**Source:** [ROSCA overview via Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/); [FTC negative option rulemaking, 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events).

## 8. Email (CAN-SPAM)

Applies to *any* commercial email, not just bulk sends — the law defines coverage by the email's primary purpose, not its volume.

**Seven core rules:**
1. Accurate "From" name and email address — no misleading header info.
2. Subject line must match the actual content — no bait-and-switch subjects.
3. The email must be identifiable as an ad where applicable.
4. A valid physical postal address, somewhere in the email.
5. A clear, working opt-out mechanism.
6. Opt-out requests honored within 10 business days, with no fee and no requirement beyond an email address.
7. The sender is responsible for compliance even if a third party sends on their behalf.

**Apply:** every marketing email template needs a real postal address and a working one-click (or near-one-click) unsubscribe; never write subject-line copy that misrepresents what's inside just to lift open rate.

**Source:** [FTC — CAN-SPAM Act Compliance Guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business).

## 9. Comparative & Competitor Claims

Not covered by a single FTC rule, but governed by the same deception standard (§2) plus the Lanham Act's false-advertising provisions, which let a named competitor sue directly.

- **Any claim naming or clearly identifying a competitor must be accurate and provable**, not just plausible-sounding.
- **A comparison chart with cherry-picked or outdated competitor features/pricing is a common, easily-triggered dispute** — competitors monitor these actively and demand letters are common even when the underlying claim is defensible.
- **"Nobody does X better" or similar unprovable superlatives are legally safer than a specific, false comparative fact** — puffery (obvious exaggeration no reasonable person takes as literal fact) has more legal protection than a specific, checkable claim that turns out to be wrong.

**Apply:** date and source every fact in a competitor comparison at the time of writing, and flag comparison pages for periodic re-verification since competitor pricing/features change.

## 10. Guarantees

- **A "money-back guarantee" must actually be honored as described** — a guarantee with hidden conditions not disclosed near the claim (restocking fees, narrow eligibility windows, exclusions) is itself a deceptive-advertising pattern.
- **A satisfaction or performance guarantee implies the company can and will pay out at the rate implied** — if a business can't actually afford to honor its stated guarantee at scale, the guarantee itself becomes the deceptive claim, independent of the product's own claims.

**Apply:** before writing "guarantee" copy, confirm with the client what the actual fine print is, and make sure the headline claim and the fine print don't contradict each other.

## 11. Pre-Ship Flag List

Run this over any draft before it ships. Anything checked routes to the client's legal/compliance contact — this list identifies what to flag, not how to resolve it.

- [ ] Any testimonial — is it real, and is any payment/freebie/employment relationship disclosed next to it?
- [ ] Any specific number, percentage, or "up to"/"as low as" claim — is there real substantiation behind it, and does it apply broadly or only to a best case?
- [ ] Any strikethrough or "compare at" price — is the reference price real and recent?
- [ ] Any health, wellness, or supplement claim — does it need clinical substantiation?
- [ ] Any income, earnings, or "make money" figure — is there documented substantiation, and is the *typical* result disclosed alongside any atypical example?
- [ ] Any "Made in USA" or origin claim — confirmed with the client's actual sourcing?
- [ ] Any free trial or subscription offer — does the actual cancellation flow match what the copy promises?
- [ ] Any marketing email — real postal address, working unsubscribe, honest subject line?
- [ ] Any named-competitor comparison — dated, sourced, and current?
- [ ] Any guarantee — does the fine print match the headline claim?

If two or more boxes are checked on a single page, that page needs a legal review pass before it ships, not just a copy edit.
